# Chapter: Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) — Articles 36 to 51
### For AP SI Aspirants with Zero Background

> How to use this chapter: Read in order. Each Article is its own section. For each Article remember: WHAT it says (1 line) + WHETHER it is Socialist / Gandhian / Liberal + ONE amendment or case linked to it. That alone solves 80% of AP SI questions.

---

## Section 1: Meaning and Purpose of DPSP — What Are They?

**What:** DPSP are Articles 36 to 51 in Part IV of the Constitution. They are instructions and ideals given to the governments (Centre and State) about how to run the country to achieve social and economic justice.

**Why they exist:** Fundamental Rights (Part III) give *political* freedom — like right to speak, equality before law. But in 1947 India was poor, unequal, illiterate. Freedom alone would not fill stomachs. So the makers added DPSP to tell the future governments: "Use your laws and schemes to make India a welfare state — reduce poverty, give education, health, fair wages."

**Exact meaning in simple words:**
- Article 37 says it clearly: DPSP are **not enforceable in court** (you cannot go to court and demand a job or liquor ban), BUT they are **fundamental in governance**. Government *must* keep them in mind while making laws.
- Think: FR = "you must not break this rule." DPSP = "you must try to reach this goal."

**Key terms:**
- Non-justiciable = cannot be enforced by court.
- Welfare state = state that works for well-being of all, especially poor.
- Social and economic democracy = FR gives political democracy (vote); DPSP aims for real-life equality (food, work, education).

**Real-life + SI-duty example:**
- Real-life: MGNREGA (100 days work), Mid-day meals, Legal Aid, Panchayats — all are DPSP put into action by law.
- SI duty: You raid a factory using child labour or bonded labour. The *punishment* comes from law/IPC/BNS, but the *idea* behind that law is DPSP Article 39(e)-(f) and 23 (FR against exploitation). As SI, you explain to villagers: "Government must protect children — that is why this law exists."

**Related articles:** Art 37 (nature), Art 38 (welfare goal), Preamble (justice — social, economic, political).

**Cases / Amendments:** No amendment changes the basic idea. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) first explained DPSP vs FR (see Section 25).

**Confusion to avoid:** Do NOT say DPSP are "useless because not in court." Supreme Court has said: FR and DPSP must be read together; governments that ignore DPSP fail their duty. Also non-justiciable does NOT mean non-important.

**Exam traps:**
1. Trap: "DPSP are justiciable." — False. They are non-justiciable.
2. Trap: "DPSP apply only to Centre." — False. They apply to all authorities making law — Centre, State, local bodies.
3. Trap: Part number — DPSP is Part IV, Articles 36-51. FR is Part III, 12-35.

**Practice MCQs:**
1. Which statement about DPSP is correct?
a) They are enforceable in Supreme Court b) They are fundamental in governance but not enforceable in court c) They apply only to citizens d) They are in Part III
**Answer: (b) — Art 37.**

2. The main purpose of DPSP is to:
a) Give political rights b) Establish social and economic democracy / welfare state c) Punish criminals d) Regulate elections
**Answer: (b).**

## Section 2: Origin and Irish Source

**What:** Idea of DPSP was borrowed mainly from the **Constitution of Ireland (1937)**, which itself was influenced by Catholic social thinking and ideas of social justice. Our makers adapted it to Indian conditions. Gandhian ideas and the freedom struggle promises (Karachi Resolution 1931, Nehru Report) also shaped it.

**Why it exists:** Dr. Ambedkar and others wanted a middle path: not only Western individual rights (USA), but also social duties of the state. Ireland was the only example then of non-enforceable social goals in a Constitution.

**Exact meaning in simple words:** "Borrowed from Ireland" does NOT mean copied word-for-word. It means the *technique* — putting non-court-enforceable social goals in the Constitution — came from Ireland.

**Key terms:** Borrowed feature; Instrument of Instructions (British Government of India Act 1935 also had similar instructions to Governors — DPSP replaced that idea in free India).

**Real-life + SI-duty example:** If a villager asks "Why is cow protection and panchayat in Constitution?" You can say: "Our leaders took good ideas from other countries (Ireland) and from Gandhi, and wrote what India needed."

**Related articles:** Preamble; Art 37.

**Confusion to avoid:** Do NOT say "DPSP came from USA / USSR." USA gave FR; USSR gave Fundamental Duties (later). DPSP = Ireland. AP SI often asks "which country?" — answer Ireland.

**Exam traps:**
1. Trap: "DPSP borrowed from USA Bill of Rights." — Wrong. FR from USA; DPSP from Ireland.
2. Trap: "DPSP were enforceable in Ireland, so must be here." — No, in both they are non-justiciable.

**Practice MCQs:**
1. DPSP in Indian Constitution are borrowed mainly from:
a) USA b) UK c) Ireland d) Canada
**Answer: (c) Ireland.**

2. Which Indian document also inspired DPSP ideals before 1947?
a) Karachi Resolution 1931 on Fundamental Rights and Economic Programme b) Morley-Minto Reforms c) Cabinet Mission Plan only d) None
**Answer: (a).**

## Section 3: Article 36 — What is "State" for DPSP?

**What does it say?**
Article 36 says that for Part IV (DPSP), the word "State" has the same meaning as in Part III (Article 12).

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: "State" means all government authorities — Central Government and Parliament, State Governments and Legislatures, all local authorities like municipalities and panchayats, and all other authorities doing government work (like police department) in India.

**Why was it included?**
To avoid writing the long definition again. It makes clear that DPSP orders are for the same authorities that must follow Fundamental Rights. No confusion about who must act.

**Real-life example**
A panchayat building a water tank, a municipality running a school, or AP Government giving pensions — all are "State" under Article 36 and must keep DPSP in mind.

**AP SI relevance**
You as SI are part of the "State." So DPSP tells your department too: help legal aid, protect women and child workers, support prohibition drives. You cannot say "DPSP is only for MLAs."

**Related Articles**
Article 12 (original definition of State for Fundamental Rights); Article 37 (who must follow DPSP).

**Exam points**
1. Article 36 = definition only, no new right or duty.
2. Definition is same as Article 12, not different.
3. "State" includes local and other authorities, not only President or Parliament.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 36 defines State differently from Article 12" — False. It is the same. Trap: "Article 36 is about welfare" — No, welfare is Article 38.
Sample MCQ: Article 36 provides — a) Definition of State same as Article 12 b) Welfare of people c) Equal pay d) Village panchayats. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 4: Article 37 — Nature: Not in Court, But Fundamental

**What does it say?**
Article 37 says two things: (1) DPSP shall not be enforceable by any court, (2) but they are fundamental in governance and it is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: you cannot file a writ and ask the court "give me a living wage under Article 43." The court will reject it. But Parliament and State Governments must try to make laws for living wage, and courts will use DPSP to understand laws. FR = you must not break this rule. DPSP = you must try to reach this goal.

**Why was it included?**
To balance reality and responsibility. In 1950 the government had no money to give a job to all immediately, so no court case. But government cannot ignore the poor either, so moral and political duty. Voters can question if government ignores DPSP.

**Real-life example**
A person demands free house under DPSP. No court orders it. But government schemes like housing for poor, MGNREGA, and legal aid come from this duty — the person must apply through Mandal office under the scheme law.

**AP SI relevance**
When villagers ask "SI sir, get me free house under DPSP," you explain: "Court cannot order this directly, but schemes come from this duty — apply through Mandal office." You enforce the scheme law, not DPSP directly.

**Related Articles**
Articles 32 and 226 (writs for Fundamental Rights, not for DPSP); Preamble; Article 38 (welfare goal).

**Exam points**
1. Non-justiciable but fundamental in governance — exact words of Article 37.
2. Word used is "duty" of State to apply DPSP in making laws.
3. Courts cannot directly enforce DPSP alone, but can use DPSP to interpret laws — from Champakam (1951) to harmonious reading later (see Section 25-26).

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Courts cannot even refer to DPSP" — False. Courts use DPSP to interpret Fundamental Rights and laws, they just cannot directly enforce DPSP alone. Trap: Article number for non-enforceability is Article 37, not Article 36.
Sample MCQ: Article 37 states DPSP are — a) Enforceable like FR b) Non-enforceable but fundamental in governance c) Only for judiciary d) Only for emergency. **Answer: (b).**

## Section 5: Article 38 — The Welfare Promise: Social Order

**What does it say?**
Article 38(1) says the State shall strive to promote welfare of people by securing a social order with justice — social, economic, political — in all institutions of national life. Article 38(2), added by 44th Amendment 1978, says the State shall strive to minimise inequalities in income and eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must not only keep law and order, but build a fair society. Reduce gap between rich and poor, and remove caste and status discrimination in real life. This is the umbrella Article — all other DPSP from Article 39 to 51 are details of this one.

**Why was it included?**
To write the headline goal of a welfare state clearly. Fundamental Rights give political freedom, but Article 38 says use laws and schemes to bring social and economic justice too.

**Real-life example**
Land reforms, reservations, progressive tax, welfare hostels and scholarships — all flow from Article 38 thinking.

**AP SI relevance**
When you enforce SC/ST Atrocities Act strictly and ensure equal treatment at the station irrespective of caste or status, you are helping Article 38(2) on the ground. You protect dignity in daily police work.

**Related Articles**
Preamble (justice — social, economic, political); Article 39(b)-(c) (economic equality tools); Article 46 (weaker sections).

**Exam points**
1. Umbrella Article for all DPSP.
2. Clause (2) added by 44th Amendment 1978 — exam favourite. It is 44th, not 42nd.
3. Key word is "strive" — State shall strive, not guarantee overnight.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 38(2) added by 42nd" — Wrong, 44th Amendment 1978. Trap: "Article 38 is about panchayats" — No, that is Article 40. Trap: mixing Article 38 (general goal) with Article 39 (specific economic principles).
Sample MCQ: Article 38 directs the State to — a) Secure social order with justice, minimise income inequality b) Provide UCC c) Separate judiciary d) Protect monuments. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 6: Article 39 - Principles of Economic Justice

**One-line idea:** Article 39 tells governments to run economic policy so that ordinary people get work, fair wages, and protection — and wealth does not stay locked with a few rich hands. Note carefully: **Article 39 has only clauses (a) to (f). There is NO clause (g).** If any guidebook says 39(g), it is wrong — AP SI may use this as a trap.

### 39(a) - Adequate Means of Livelihood

**What does it say?**
That the State shall direct its policy towards securing that all citizens, men and women equally, have the right to an adequate means of livelihood.

**What does it actually mean?**
In plain words: government must create conditions where every person can earn food and support a family — jobs, land access, credit, skills, welfare schemes. It does NOT mean a court will order "give this person a job tomorrow." Like all DPSP, it is non-enforceable alone (Article 37), but government must try step by step.

**Why was it included?**
In 1947 India was poor, jobless, and unequal after colonial rule. Political freedom (vote, speech) alone would not fill stomachs. So the makers wrote 39(a) to say: economic policy must aim at livelihood for all, not just growth for a few.

**Real-life example**
MGNREGA (100 days rural work), PM employment schemes, skill missions, land assignment to landless poor, street-vendor loans. All are laws and schemes flowing from 39(a) thinking.

**AP SI relevance**
You will not file a case "under 39(a)." But you will: (1) explain to villagers demanding work that schemes come from this duty and guide them to Mandal/panchayat office; (2) handle disputes at employment queues, land-distribution sites, or migrant labour camps calmly, knowing livelihood pressure is behind them; (3) act firmly against bonded labour contractors who deny livelihood freedom — law punishes, 39(a) explains the why.

**Related Articles**
Article 38 (welfare goal — parent Article), Article 39(b)-(c) (tools for fair distribution), Article 41 (work within economic capacity), Article 43 (living wage), Fundamental Rights Articles 14-16 (equality) and 21 (life includes livelihood in court reading).

**Exam points**
1. Exact words: "men and women equally" appear in 39(a).
2. 39(a) = livelihood, NOT equal pay. Equal pay is 39(d).
3. 39(a) is Socialist/welfare in classification.
4. Non-justiciable alone, but courts use it to understand labour and welfare laws.

**Common trap (vs 39(d))**
Trap: "39(a) means equal pay for equal work." — False. 39(a) = right to earn a living at all. 39(d) = if two people do the same work, pay them equally without gender cut. AP SI loves swapping (a) and (d).

**Sample MCQ:**
Adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally is in:
a) Article 39(a) b) Article 39(d) c) Article 40 d) Article 44
**Answer: (a).**

### 39(b) - Material Resources of the Community

**What does it say?**
That ownership and control of the material resources of the community are so distributed as best to subserve the common good.

**What does it actually mean?**
Government policy on mines, forests, land, water, spectrum, oil should benefit the whole public, not just a few owners. It does NOT mean government owns everything. It means: when framing laws on important resources, ask "does this serve common good?"

**Why was it included?**
British-era zamindari, princely monopolies, and big landlords locked land and mines with a few. Freedom fighters promised land reform and public control of key wealth. 39(b) gave constitutional backing for ceiling laws, mine nationalisation, forest control, and auction rules.

**What 'material resources of the community' means**
It means resources that by nature and effect matter to the community — scarce, socially important, capable of public use. Classic examples below. It does NOT automatically mean every private house, shop, or small plot. Whether a particular private resource counts depends on a real test, not a label.

**Examples**
Clearly community-linked: coal mines, major minerals, forests, rivers, village commons, spectrum, petroleum. May be examined case-by-case: large surplus urban land, key private industrial resources affecting public supply. Clearly NOT automatic: one family house, small shop, personal savings — these are not "material resources of the community" just because someone calls them so.

**Real-life example**
Land-ceiling laws, coal-mine nationalisation laws (1970s), forest conservation regime, transparent auction of mines and spectrum for public revenue, AP land-distribution and mining-lease rules.

**AP SI relevance**
During sand-mining, quarry, land-grab, or encroachment of village commons disputes, you enforce the mining, revenue, and penal laws — but the idea "public resources must serve public" is 39(b). You protect government land records, stop illegal extraction, and explain to villagers why permits and auctions exist.

**Related Articles**
Article 38 (welfare umbrella), Article 39(c) (twin Article — see below), Article 31C (shield), Article 39(a) (livelihood link), Article 48A (environment link for forests).

**Article 31C connection**
Article 31C says: a genuine law made to fulfil 39(b)-(c) cannot be struck down only because it violates Fundamental Rights Article 14 (equality) or Article 19 (freedoms). Added by 25th Amendment 1971 for land reforms. Extended to all DPSP by 42nd Amendment 1976, but that extension was struck down.

**Supreme Court interpretation**
Kesavananda Bharati (1973) upheld the original narrow 31C for 39(b)-(c). Minerva Mills (1980) struck the 42nd wide extension and held: **31C shields only true 39(b)-(c) laws**, because harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSP is part of basic structure. So a law must really fulfil 39(b) or 39(c) to get the shield; merely writing "for DPSP" is not enough.

**2024 Property Owners Association present position**
On 5 November 2024, a 9-judge bench in Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra kept the Minerva limit — 31C still covers only 39(b)-(c), NOT all DPSP. On 39(b) meaning, it rejected both extremes: (1) "all private property is community resource" is false; (2) "no private property can ever be community resource" is also false. Present position: apply a real, case-by-case community-resource test — look at nature of the resource, its scarcity, its effect on the community, and whether public need requires it. No automatic takeover of every private house.

**Exam points**
1. Exact phrase "to subserve the common good" = 39(b).
2. Twin of 39(c); pair appears together in Article 31C.
3. 31C original = 25th Amendment 1971 for 39(b)-(c) only.
4. After Minerva 1980, 31C = only 39(b)-(c), not all DPSP.

**Common misconceptions**
1. "39(b) lets government take any private house." — False after 2024; needs real community-resource test.
2. "2024 restored 42nd wide shield for all DPSP." — False; 2024 kept narrow shield, only clarified 39(b) test.
3. "39(b) itself punishes or acquires." — False; 39(b) only directs policy. Actual taking needs a valid law with compensation procedure.

**Common trap (vs private ownership)**
Trap: "Material resources of the community = all private property." — False. Exam may give "A's house is automatically covered by 39(b)." Correct answer: No, only if it truly passes the community-resource test.

**Sample MCQ:**
"Ownership and control of material resources of the community to subserve the common good" is:
a) Article 39(b) b) Article 39(d) c) Article 40 d) Article 44
**Answer: (a).**

### 39(c) - Prevention of Concentration of Wealth

**What does it say?**
That the operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment.

**What does it actually mean?**
In plain words: the economy should not be run in a way where a few monopolists corner factories, mines, land, or market power and harm the public. Government must use tax, competition, ceiling, and licensing policy to prevent harmful concentration. It does NOT mean everyone must have equal money; it means harmful monopoly must be stopped.

**Why was it included?**
Makers feared replacing British monopolies with Indian monopolies. Without this rule, 39(b) distribution would fail — resources would again pile with a few. So 39(c) is the negative guard: stop harmful pile-up.

**Real-life example**
MRTP/Competition law against cartels, land-ceiling limits, prevention of black-marketing and hoarding, progressive taxation, AP action against illicit liquor or sand syndicates that corner local supply.

**AP SI relevance**
When you bust hoarding, black-marketing, cartel-rate sand or liquor rings, or a contractor-mafia cornering tenders, you act under competition, excise, mining, or BNS laws — the constitutional purpose is 39(c): economic power must not harm the common person. You also keep order during strikes or protests against monopoly practices, staying neutral but firm on law.

**Related Articles**
Twin Article 39(b) — (b) says "distribute for common good," (c) says "do not let it concentrate harmfully." Also Article 38 (reduce income inequality), Article 39(a) (livelihood suffers if wealth concentrates), Article 31C (shield).

**Article 31C connection**
Same as 39(b): a genuine 39(c) law is shielded from challenge only on Article 14 or 19 grounds. This was the original 1971 purpose — to save land-ceiling and anti-monopoly laws from being blocked by equality/freedom claims.

**Case / amendment link**
25th Amendment 1971 created this shield for 39(b)-(c); Kesavananda (1973) upheld it; 42nd Amendment 1976 tried to extend it to all DPSP; Minerva Mills (1980) struck that extension, so today only true 39(b)-(c) laws get it. 2024 Property Owners case did not change this — it kept Minerva narrow shield.

**Exam points**
1. Key phrase "to the common detriment" = 39(c). If options show "common good," that is 39(b).
2. 39(b) + 39(c) always go together in 31C questions.
3. Socialist bucket.
4. Does not itself fix tax rates or ceilings — only directs policy.

**Common trap**
Trap: "39(b) and 39(c) are the same." — Close but different. 39(b) = positive duty to distribute resources for common good. 39(c) = negative duty to prevent harmful concentration. Trap: "39(c) is about equal pay." — No, that is 39(d).

**Sample MCQ:**
Prevention of concentration of wealth to the common detriment is in:
a) Article 39(b) b) Article 39(c) c) Article 39(d) d) Article 40
**Answer: (b) — remember "detriment" = (c), "common good" = (b).**

### 39(d) - Equal Pay for Equal Work

**What does it say?**
That there is equal pay for equal work for both men and women.

**What does it actually mean?**
If a man and a woman do the same work under the same conditions, pay must be equal. No gender cut. This is a direction to the State to secure it by law and policy. Courts later also read it with Fundamental Rights Articles 14 and 16 in service cases, but 39(d) alone is DPSP and not directly enforceable by writ.

**Why was it included?**
Women were routinely paid less for the same farm, factory, and office work. Makers wanted economic equality written clearly so future labour laws would remove the gender gap.

**Real-life example**
Equal Remuneration Act 1976, Minimum Wages enforcement where women get same daily wage at construction sites, MGNREGA wage parity, government pay scales with no male/female difference for same post.

**AP SI relevance**
Classic SI-ground case: a contractor at a brick kiln, farm, or construction site pays women Rs. 300 and men Rs. 500 for identical work. As SI, you enquire, record statements, and book under labour laws. You also ensure women Home Guards, constables, and staff get due allowances on bandobast duty — same work, same pay logic starts in your own station.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(a) (livelihood base), Articles 14-16 (equality — courts link here), Article 42 (humane work), Article 43 (living wage), Article 15(3) (special provisions for women support this goal).

**Exam points**
1. Exact words "for both men and women" in 39(d).
2. Socialist bucket.
3. Implemented mainly by Equal Remuneration Act 1976 — favourite one-mark link.
4. 39(d) covers gender pay for same work, not reservations or maternity leave (those are Articles 16/42).

**Common trap (vs 39(a))**
Trap: "39(a) is equal pay." — False. 39(a) = chance to earn a living. 39(d) = equal pay once doing the same work. Mnemonic: (a) = Access to work; (d) = equal Dollars for same work.

**Sample MCQ:**
Equal pay for equal work for men and women is in:
a) Article 39(a) b) Article 39(d) c) Article 39(f) d) Article 41
**Answer: (b).**

### 39(e) - Worker Health and Strength

**What does it say?**
That the health and strength of workers, men and women, and the tender age of children are not abused, and citizens are not forced by economic necessity to enter avocations unsuited to their age or strength.

**What does it actually mean?**
In plain words two rules: (1) do not squeeze workers and small children till their health breaks; (2) do not let poverty force people into dangerous or age-unsuitable jobs (for example, a child in a chemical unit, or a sick person forced into heavy load work to survive). Government must make safety, hours, and child-protection laws.

**Why was it included?**
Colonial factories, mines, and landlords used long hours, no safety, and child labour. Poor families had no choice. Makers wrote 39(e) to force future governments to ban abuse of helplessness.

**Real-life example**
Factories Act (hours, safety), Mines Act, Bonded Labour Abolition, Child Labour (Prohibition) law, trafficking rescues.

**AP SI relevance**
This is core SI duty. You rescue bonded labourers from brick kilns, stop contractors forcing children or exhausted workers into hazardous work, enforce hour and safety violations reported to police, and produce trafficked children before Child Welfare Committee. When you write a rescue report, the legal sections come from BNS and special laws — the constitutional root is 39(e) plus Fundamental Rights Articles 23-24.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(f) (child development twin), Articles 23-24 (FR against exploitation and child labour — enforceable side), Article 42 (humane work), Article 47 (health duty).

**Exam points**
1. Key phrases: "tender age of children" and "forced by economic necessity" = 39(e).
2. Covers both adult workers AND children, male and female.
3. Socialist/protective bucket.
4. Does not itself fix working hours — that is done by laws like Factories Act.

**Common trap**
Trap: "39(e) is only about children." — Incomplete. It covers workers (men and women) plus tender age of children. Trap: "39(e) bans child labour in hazardous work by itself." — No, the direct ban is FR Article 24; 39(e) is the DPSP direction behind labour laws.

**Sample MCQ:**
Protection of health and strength of workers and tender age of children from abuse is in:
a) Article 39(e) b) Article 44 c) Article 49 d) Article 51
**Answer: (a).**

### 39(f) - Childhood and Youth Protection

**What does it say?**
That children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a healthy manner and in conditions of freedom and dignity, and that childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment.

**What does it actually mean?**
Every child should grow with health, schooling support, dignity, and safety — not through abuse, trafficking, begging rackets, or being left with no care. "Moral abandonment" means neglect of care and values; "material abandonment" means neglect of food, shelter, and essentials. Government must build protection systems, not just punish after harm.

**Why was it included?**
Children and youth are the most helpless in poverty. Makers first wrote a simpler child-protection line, then Parliament strengthened it by the 42nd Amendment 1976 to the present wider wording — healthy development plus freedom, dignity, and double protection from exploitation and abandonment.

**Real-life example**
POCSO Act, Juvenile Justice Act, Child Labour law, ICDS/anganwadi nutrition, school-protection committees, anti-trafficking units, de-addiction support for youth.

**AP SI relevance**
Your daily child-protection work flows from here: missing-children trace, POCSO investigation with child-friendly procedure, rescue of begging or labouring children, action on child-marriage information, and strict confidentiality for victims. You coordinate with Child Welfare Committee, DLSA, and anganwadi/school records. The arrest and punishment powers come from BNS and special Acts — 39(f) is the constitutional duty to protect, not abandon.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(e) (twin — health/strength), Articles 23-24 (FR exploitation ban), Article 45 (early childhood care below six after 86th), Article 21A (6-14 education right), Article 47 (nutrition/health).

**Exam points**
1. Present wording of 39(f) = after 42nd Amendment 1976. Favourite trap.
2. Key words: "freedom and dignity" + "moral and material abandonment" = 39(f).
3. Only Article 39 clause amended by 42nd.
4. Still DPSP (non-enforceable alone); enforceable protection comes from FR and special laws.

**Common trap**
Trap: "39(f) was always in present form." — False, strengthened by 42nd. Trap: "39(e) and 39(f) are identical." — Different: 39(e) = do not abuse health/strength or force unsuitable work; 39(f) = give positive opportunities for healthy, dignified growth and guard against exploitation and abandonment.

**Sample MCQ:**
Protection of childhood and youth against exploitation and moral and material abandonment is in:
a) Article 39(e) b) Article 39(f) c) Article 39(d) d) Article 40
**Answer: (b).**

## Section 7: Article 39A — Free Legal Aid

**What does it say?**
Article 39A says the State shall secure that justice is not denied to any citizen due to economic or other disability, and shall provide free legal aid by suitable laws and schemes. Added by 42nd Amendment 1976.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: courts are costly. Without help, the poor cannot use even Fundamental Rights. So government must run legal aid — Lok Adalats, NALSA/DLSA lawyers, free counsel for poor, SC/ST, women, children and disabled. Article 39A itself is DPSP and not directly enforceable by writ, but later the Supreme Court also read free legal aid into Fundamental Right Article 21.

**Why was it included?**
Because justice for all needs state help. During Emergency-era review, makers felt poor were losing in courts for lack of money, so access to justice was written as a state duty.

**Real-life example**
NALSA and DLSA free lawyers, Lok Adalat settlements, and free counsel under the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 — all flow from Article 39A.

**AP SI relevance**
At the station, when an arrested poor person asks for a lawyer, you must inform of the right to legal aid and produce the person before a magistrate within 24 hours. You give DLSA contact. That connects Fundamental Right Article 22 plus DPSP 39A plus law.

**Related Articles**
Article 22(1) (right to counsel — enforceable side); Article 14 (equality before law); Article 39 (economic justice base).

**Exam points**
1. Added by 42nd Amendment 1976, not 44th.
2. Implemented by Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 — NALSA/DLSA.
3. Still DPSP alone, but courts link it to Article 21 in practice.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 39A added by 44th" — Wrong, 42nd. Trap: "Legal aid only for rich" — Opposite, for those with economic or other disability. Trap: "Article 39A itself gives writ" — No, it directs the State to make law.
Sample MCQ: Article 39A on free legal aid was inserted by — a) 42nd Amendment 1976 b) 44th Amendment c) 86th Amendment d) 97th Amendment. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 8: Article 40 — Village Panchayats

**What does it say?**
Article 40 says the State shall organise village panchayats and give them powers and authority to function as units of self-government.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: create elected local village bodies with real work like water, roads and schools, with funds and powers. Article 40 itself is only a direction — it does not fix tenure or elections. That real structure came later through the 73rd Amendment 1992 (Part IX).

**Why was it included?**
Gandhian dream — democracy should reach the village, not stop at Delhi. British rule was centralised, so free India wanted self-government at the base.

**Real-life example**
Elected sarpanch, ward members, village water and road works, and Eleventh Schedule subjects handled by panchayats after the 73rd Amendment.

**AP SI relevance**
Sarpanch helps you in village disputes, prohibition awareness and missing persons work. You coordinate with panchayat for community policing, not bypass it. You respect elected local leadership while enforcing law.

**Related Articles**
73rd Amendment and Eleventh Schedule (real teeth to Article 40); Article 38 (welfare) and Article 46 (weaker sections support).

**Exam points**
1. Organisation of village panchayats = Article 40.
2. Given real teeth by 73rd Amendment 1992, not by Article 40 alone.
3. Classification = Gandhian, not Socialist.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Panchayats became constitutional by Article 40 alone" — No, by 73rd Amendment. Trap: "Article 40 fixes tenure" — No, only direction to organise and empower.
Sample MCQ: Organisation of village panchayats is in — a) Art 40 b) Art 44 c) Art 48A d) Art 50. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 9: Article 41 — Work, Education, Public Help (Within Capacity)

**What does it say?**
Article 41 says the State shall, within the limits of its economic capacity and development, make provision for securing the right to work, to education, and to public assistance in cases of old age, sickness, disablement and undeserved want.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must try for jobs, schooling and pensions step by step as funds allow. You cannot demand a job or pension as a Fundamental Right. Only one part later became a right — elementary education for age 6-14 under Article 21A after the 86th Amendment.

**Why was it included?**
The ideal is big but money is limited. So the Constitution honestly adds the "capacity" condition. It tells governments to expand help as the economy grows.

**Real-life example**
Old-age pensions, disability allowance, employment exchanges, skill missions and welfare hostels — all are Article 41 put into action by law.

**AP SI relevance**
You verify beneficiaries in pension fraud cases and guide destitute persons to welfare help. That is Article 41 on the ground. You also handle queues and disputes at employment or pension centres calmly.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(a) (livelihood base); Articles 41-43 group; Article 21A (education as Fundamental Right for 6-14 after 86th — exception where part of Article 41 idea became a right).

**Exam points**
1. Limiting words: "within limits of economic capacity and development."
2. Right to work is DPSP under capacity, not Fundamental Right.
3. Only elementary education 6-14 became Fundamental Right (Article 21A). Maternity relief is not here — that is Article 42.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 41 gives enforceable right to job" — False, subject to capacity. Trap: "Article 41 includes maternity relief" — No, that is Article 42.
Sample MCQ: Right to work and public assistance subject to economic capacity is — a) Art 41 b) Art 44 c) Art 50 d) Art 51. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 10: Article 42 — Humane Work and Maternity Relief

**What does it say?**
Article 42 says the State shall make provision for securing just and humane conditions of work and for maternity relief.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must make laws for working hours, safety, leave and paid maternity leave and benefits, so workers and especially pregnant women are not exploited with long hours and no rest.

**Why was it included?**
Workers, especially pregnant women, were exploited with long hours, no leave and unsafe work in colonial factories and fields. The makers wanted dignity at work written as a state duty.

**Real-life example**
Maternity Benefit Act, Factories Act safeguards on hours and safety, and rules against hazardous night work for pregnant women.

**AP SI relevance**
If a contractor denies maternity leave or forces a pregnant woman into hazardous work against law, your enquiry supports the Article 42-based law. You also follow humane norms for women police staff on bandobast and night duties.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(d)-(e) (equal pay and worker health); Article 43 (living wage); Article 15(3) (special provisions for women and children support this goal).

**Exam points**
1. Humane conditions of work plus maternity relief = Article 42.

3. Socialist and labour-welfare bucket.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Maternity relief is Article 43" — Wrong, Article 42. Trap: "Article 42 is equal pay" — No, that is Article 39(d).

## Section 11: Article 43 — Living Wage and Decent Life for Workers

**What does it say?**
Article 43 says the State shall endeavour to secure by law or economic organisation: work, a living wage, conditions ensuring a decent standard of life, full enjoyment of leisure and social and cultural opportunities; and shall promote cottage industries in rural areas.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: mere survival pay is not enough. Three levels — minimum wage (survival), fair wage (survival plus some comfort), living wage (survival plus education, health and dignity for family). Article 43 aims for living wage. It also says support village and cottage industries for rural jobs.

**Why was it included?**
Merely minimum survival does not give dignity. The makers wanted workers to live a decent life and support families, plus rural jobs so villages are not left behind.

**Real-life example**
Minimum Wages Act, Khadi and village industries boards, and rural artisan support schemes — all flow from Article 43 thinking.

**AP SI relevance**
During a labour dispute or strike, you keep order while knowing workers' demand for fair wage comes from the Article 43 ideal — you stay neutral and enforce law. You do not take sides, you protect life, property and peaceful protest rights.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(d) (equal pay), Article 42 (humane work), Article 43A (participation) and 43B (cooperatives — do not mix numbers); Article 46 for weaker sections.

**Exam points**
1. Living wage plus cottage industries = Article 43.
2. Living wage is higher and dignity-based, not same as minimum wage.
3. Article 43 (wage) is different from 43A (management) and 43B (cooperatives via 97th).

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 43 is only about cottage industries" — Incomplete, mainly living wage plus decent life. Trap: "Living wage = minimum wage" — No, living wage is higher. Trap: mixing 43, 43A and 43B.
Sample MCQ: Living wage and cottage industries are in — a) Art 43 b) Art 44 c) Art 48A d) Art 50. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 12: Article 43A — Workers' Participation in Management

**What does it say?**
Article 43A says the State shall take steps to secure participation of workers in management of industries. Added by 42nd Amendment 1976.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: workers should get a seat at the table — works committees and safety committees where labour representatives sit with owners, so decisions are fairer and disputes are less. It is about management voice, not wages.

**Why was it included?**
Workers know ground reality of machines, safety and workload. If they join management, decisions are fairer and strikes reduce. Emergency-era thinking added labour voice to industry.

**Real-life example**
Works committees, safety committees and joint councils under labour laws where worker reps meet managers.

**AP SI relevance**
During factory unrest, you as SI call both manager and worker representatives to keep peace. That joint-talk idea comes from Article 43A. You enforce law neutrally and protect both sides.

**Related Articles**
Article 43 (living wage base); Industrial Disputes Act link; Article 39(d) background on labour fairness.

**Exam points**
1. Added by 42nd Amendment 1976, not 44th or 97th.
2. About management participation, not wages and not cooperatives.
3. Cooperatives are Article 43B via 97th — separate.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 43A is about cooperatives" — No, that is Article 43B. Trap: amendment is 42nd, not 97th.

## Section 13: Article 43B — Cooperative Societies

**What does it say?**
Article 43B says the State shall endeavour to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies. Added by 97th Amendment 2011.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must support genuine cooperatives like milk, credit and housing groups, but not control them politically. Ensure free elections, proper accounts and professional work. "Voluntary" means no force; "autonomous" means no political bossing.

**Why was it included?**
Cooperatives empower small people but suffered political interference, fake membership and money misuse. So the Constitution gave them protection plus duties — Part IXB was also added by 97th, and "cooperatives" was added to Fundamental Right Article 19(1)(c).

**Real-life example**
AMUL-type dairy cooperatives, PACS credit societies, and housing cooperatives run with elected boards and audits under Cooperative Societies Acts.

**AP SI relevance**
In cooperative election disputes or embezzlement cases, you act under the Cooperative Societies Act which now has constitutional backing. You keep order at polls, investigate fraud, and guide members to registrar and audit channels.

**Related Articles**
Article 19(1)(c) (right to form cooperatives — also amended by 97th); Part IXB (cooperative structure); Article 43 (rural base).

**Exam points**
1. Added by 97th Amendment 2011, not 42nd.
2. 97th did three things: Article 43B plus Part IXB plus Article 19(1)(c) word "cooperatives."
3. Do not mix Article 43 (wage), 43A (workers in management, 42nd) and 43B (cooperatives, 97th).

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 43B added by 42nd" — Wrong, 97th Amendment 2011. Trap: "Cooperatives are only DPSP" — After 97th, also Fundamental Right 19(1)(c) plus Part IXB. Pending court issues on Parts of 97th are never treated as settled — stick to the added text.
Sample MCQ: Article 43B on cooperatives was inserted by — a) 97th Amendment 2011 b) 42nd Amendment c) 44th Amendment d) 86th Amendment. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 14: Article 44 — Uniform Civil Code (UCC)

**What does it say?**
Article 44 says the State shall endeavour to secure for citizens a uniform civil code throughout India.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: one common law for marriage, divorce, adoption and inheritance for all Indians, instead of religion-based personal laws. It does not mean one criminal law — we already have BNS for crime — and it does not remove religious freedom to pray under Articles 25-28.

**Why was it included?**
Different personal laws for Hindu, Muslim, Christian and others for marriage, divorce and inheritance create inequality, especially for women. Freedom struggle leaders wanted one common civil law respecting equality while protecting religion separately through Fundamental Rights.

**Real-life example**
Goa has a common civil code. Uttarakhand UCC law 2024 is a debated state example. For the rest of India, UCC remains a future goal, and any pending bills or debates are never treated as settled law.

**AP SI relevance**
In inter-religion marriage or maintenance disputes, you apply current personal law plus BNSS maintenance provisions, and explain UCC is a future goal, not current law except Goa. Do not misguide the public that UCC already applies everywhere.

**Related Articles**
Articles 25-28 (religious freedom — balance with UCC); Articles 14-15 (equality); Article 38 (justice goal).

**Exam points**
1. Uniform civil code = Article 44, not 43 or 45.
2. Covers personal civil matters only — marriage, divorce, inheritance.
3. Liberal-Intellectual bucket, not Gandhian. Still DPSP — State to endeavour, not directly enforceable by writ.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "UCC means uniform criminal law" — False. Trap: "UCC means Hindi as national language or police uniform" — False. Trap: "UCC is enforceable by writ" — No, DPSP.
Sample MCQ: Uniform Civil Code is directed in — a) Art 44 b) Art 40 c) Art 48 d) Art 50. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 15: Article 45 — Early Childhood Care (Below Six) — Changed by 86th

**What does it say?**
Article 45 in its present form says the State shall endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years. This present wording is after the 86th Amendment 2002. Before 2002, Article 45 said free and compulsory education for all children until 14 years.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must run anganwadis, nutrition and pre-school for small kids aged 0-6. Schooling for age 6-14 is no longer DPSP — it became a Fundamental Right under new Article 21A with RTE law. Parent duty for 6-14 was added as Article 51A(k).

**Why was it included?**
Brain and health grow fastest below six. Anganwadis, nutrition and immunisation need constitutional push. The old 14-year promise was split because India was ready to make 6-14 schooling a right.

**Real-life example**
ICDS, anganwadi nutrition, pre-school and immunisation records for 0-6 children, plus mid-day meals support at schools.

**AP SI relevance**
In child missing, trafficking or malnutrition-death enquiry, you coordinate with anganwadi records for 0-6 data. That system flows from Article 45. For 6-14 out-of-school children, you link to school and Article 21A right.

**Related Articles**
Article 21A (6-14 Fundamental Right); Article 51A(k) (parent duty); Article 39(f) (child protection) and Article 47 (nutrition and health).

**Exam points**
1. After 86th Amendment 2002: Article 45 = only below six (0-6).
2. 6-14 free and compulsory education = Fundamental Right Article 21A, not Article 45.
3. Old books saying "Article 45 = education till 14" are outdated. Amendment is 86th, not 42nd. Article 45 is still DPSP, enforceable part is 21A.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 45 still gives education till 14" — False after 2002. Trap: "Article 45 is enforceable" — No, still DPSP.
Sample MCQ: After 86th Amendment, Art 45 deals with — a) Early childhood care till age six b) Free education till 14 c) UCC d) Panchayats. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 16: Article 46 — SC, ST and Weaker Sections

**What does it say?**
Article 46 says the State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, especially Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and shall protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**

Article 46 itself does not fix reservation percentages; those come from laws under Articles 15-16. It gives the direction.

Centuries of caste discrimination and tribal isolation needed active state help for real equality. Formal equality on paper was not enough without special care.

Welfare hostels, scholarships, tribal sub-plan schemes, and protection under SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act.

## Section 17: Article 47 — Health, Nutrition, Prohibition

**What does it say?**
Article 47 says the State shall regard raising nutrition levels, standard of living and improvement of public health as among its primary duties, and shall endeavour to bring about prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health, except for medicinal purposes.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: government must work for hospitals, clean water and nutrition, and try to reduce alcohol and drug harm through excise policy and de-addiction. "Primary duty" are strong words for health. "Endeavour" means try step by step — Article 47 does not itself ban alcohol; ban needs a state law.

**Why was it included?**
Healthy citizens build a strong nation. The freedom movement also linked liquor to social ruin and family poverty, so health plus prohibition push was written together.

**Real-life example**
PHCs, nutrition missions, gutka bans, and excise plus NDPS laws on illicit liquor and drugs.

**AP SI relevance**
Your excise and NDPS raids, drunk-driving checks, action on spurious liquor, and referral of addicts to de-addiction centres reflect Article 47. The punishment power comes from AP Excise Act and NDPS Act — Article 47 is the why behind them.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(e)-(f) (worker and child health), Article 45 (0-6 care) and 48A (environment); Article 21 (health read into right to life by courts).

**Exam points**
1. Nutrition, public health and prohibition = Article 47.
2. Public health is "primary duty" — favourite exam phrase.
3. Article 47 directs the State to endeavour; it does not directly jail offenders.

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "Article 47 bans liquor directly" — No, directs State to endeavour through law. Trap: "Public health is secondary" — Article says primary duty.

## Section 21: Article 50 — Separation of Judiciary from Executive

## Section 23: Classification Buckets — Socialist, Gandhian, Liberal

**What:** For easy memory, books divide DPSP into three buckets. This division is **not in the Constitution** — only for study.

**Buckets in simple words:**
- **Socialist / Welfare:** Aim for workers, poor, equality. Articles: 38 (welfare), 39 (economic justice), 39A (legal aid), 41 (work/assistance), 42 (humane work), 43 (living wage), 43A (workers in management), 47 (health).
- **Gandhian:** Based on Gandhi's village, moral ideas. Articles: 40 (panchayats), 43 (cottage industries part), 46 (weaker sections uplift — often counted here too), 47 (prohibition part), 48 (cow protection, agriculture).
- **Liberal-Intellectual:** Modern, thinking-person ideas. Articles: 44 (UCC), 45 (childhood care), 48A (environment), 49 (monuments), 50 (separation of judiciary), 51 (world peace), 43B (cooperatives often put here/modern).

**Why it helps:** AP SI may ask "Which is Gandhian?" If you know bucket, you tick fast.

**Confusion to avoid:** Some Articles appear in two lists (e.g., 43, 46, 47) in different books — do not panic. Learn core: 40,48 = Gandhian; 44,50,51 = Liberal; 39,42,43A = Socialist. 43B = Liberal/modern cooperative.

## Section 24: DPSP vs Fundamental Rights — Difference in One Table

**What:** FR (Part III) are enforceable rights of individuals; DPSP (Part IV) are non-enforceable goals for State.

**Simple comparison:**
- FR: Justiciable (Art 32/226 writs). DPSP: Non-justiciable (Art 37).
- FR: Stops State from doing wrong (negative — "do not discriminate"). DPSP: Asks State to do good (positive — "provide, promote").
- FR: For citizens + some for all persons. DPSP: Duties of State towards all.
- FR: Can be suspended in Emergency (Art 20-21 excepted). DPSP: Always remain as goals.
- Both are fundamental — FR for courts, DPSP for governance. Best rule today: read together harmoniously.

**Why conflict arose:** Land reform and equality laws (DPSP 39(b)-(c)) clashed with FR right to property/equality. Courts first said FR wins (Champakam 1951).

**SI example:** FR Art 22 says produce arrested person in 24 hrs — you MUST obey, court will punish if not. DPSP Art 47 says prohibit liquor — you raid under excise law, but no one can get writ ordering total prohibition tomorrow.

**Confusion to avoid:** Do NOT say DPSP are inferior. Supreme Court in Minerva (1980) said harmony between FR and DPSP is part of basic structure.

**Exam traps:**
1. Trap: "DPSP can be enforced by writ." — No.
2. Trap: "FR are goals, DPSP are rights." — Reversed.

**Practice MCQs:**
1. Main difference is:
a) FR justiciable, DPSP non-justiciable b) Both same c) DPSP in Part III d) FR non-justiciable

## Section 25: Article 31C — Full Story: 25th, 42nd, Minerva, 2024 Revival

**What does it say?**
Article 31C is a shield. It says a law made to fulfil certain DPSP cannot be struck down only because it violates Fundamental Rights Article 14 (equality) or Article 19 (freedoms). It sits just after Fundamental Rights property history — remember it when DPSP vs Fundamental Rights clash. Verified position: narrow shield for Articles 39(b)-(c) only.

**What does it actually mean in simple words?**
In plain words: if government takes over a mine or surplus land for public distribution under a genuine Article 39(b)-(c) law, the owner cannot win only by saying "violates equality or freedom." But the shield works only for genuine 39(b)-(c) laws, not for any law labelled DPSP. Full story: 25th Amendment 1971 added 31C for 39(b)-(c) to save land reforms. Kesavananda Bharati (1973) upheld this original narrow shield. 42nd Amendment 1976 extended it to all DPSP, but Minerva Mills (1980) struck that extension and brought it back to only 39(b)-(c). On 5 Nov 2024, a 9-judge bench in Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra kept the Minerva narrow limit and only clarified the 39(b) test case-by-case. Pending items are never treated as settled.

**Why was it included?**
Land reform and anti-monopoly laws for Articles 39(b)-(c) were being blocked by equality and freedom claims. Parliament added a limited shield so public-purpose distribution could survive, without destroying Fundamental Rights balance.

**Real-life example**
Land-ceiling laws, coal-mine nationalisation laws of the 1970s, and transparent auction rules for mines and spectrum for public revenue — genuine 39(b)-(c) purpose laws that use the shield.

**AP SI relevance**
During sand-mining, quarry or land-grab disputes, you enforce mining, revenue and penal laws. The idea "public resources must serve public" is 39(b)-(c), and the shield explains why such laws survive equality challenges. You protect records, stop illegal extraction and stay neutral on ownership claims pending in court.

**Related Articles**
Article 39(b)-(c) (twin Articles covered); Articles 14 and 19 (only rights barred as sole ground); Article 38 (welfare umbrella); Article 300A (property now legal right after 44th).

**Exam points**
1. Original Article 31C = 25th Amendment 1971 for Article 39(b)-(c) only.
2. 42nd extension to all DPSP was struck in Minerva Mills 1980 — back to only 39(b)-(c) for 44 years and still narrow after 2024.
3. 2024 did not restore wide shield and did not make all private property community resource — needs real case-by-case test of nature, scarcity and community need.
4. 31C never protected violation of Articles 20, 21 etc. — only Articles 14, 19 (and 31 when it existed).

**Common trap + sample MCQ**
Trap: "After Minerva, 31C covers all DPSP" — False, only 39(b)-(c). Trap: "31C added by 42nd" — Added by 25th, only extended by 42nd. Trap: "2024 made all private property community resource or restored all-DPSP shield" — False, kept narrow shield, only clarified 39(b) test.
Sample MCQ: After Minerva Mills 1980, Art 31C covers — a) Only 39(b)-(c) b) All DPSP c) No DPSP d) Only FR. **Answer: (a).**

## Section 26: Key Cases You Must Know

**1. Champakam Dorairajan v State of Madras (1951):** Medical college communal reservation vs FR equality/merit. Supreme Court said: **FR prevails over DPSP** if direct clash. Result: Parliament added 1st Amendment (Art 15(4)) to allow special provisions. *Trap:* This case is FR-wins, not harmony.

**2. Kerala Education Bill Advisory Opinion (1958):** President asked Supreme Court about Kerala school takeover bill. Court advised: try **harmonious construction** — do not treat FR vs DPSP as enemy; interpret to give effect to both as far as possible. Starting point of harmony idea.

**3. Kesavananda Bharati (1973):** Upheld original 31C (39(b)-(c) shield), propounded Basic Structure. Said Parliament can amend but not destroy Constitution.

**4. Minerva Mills (1980):** Struck 42nd extension of 31C; held **harmony between FR and DPSP is basic structure**. Neither can swallow the other. 31C back to 39(b)-(c).

**5. Unni Krishnan / Mohini Jain → RTE:** Right to education read from FR Art 21 + DPSP 41,45 → led to 86th Amendment and Art 21A. Shows DPSP pushing FR growth.

**6. Property Owners Association (2024, 9 judges):** Clarified 39(b) — private property may be community resource only after real test, not automatically. Keeps Minerva narrow shield.

**SI takeaway:** Quote in mains/descriptive: "From Champakam (FR supremacy) to Kerala Bill (harmony) to Minerva (balance is basic structure)."

**Confusion to avoid:** Do NOT say Minerva upheld 42nd wide shield — it struck it. Do NOT present pending/sub-judice issues as settled.

**Exam traps:**
1. Trap: "Kerala Bill case enforced DPSP by writ." — No, advisory harmony rule.
2. Trap: "Minerva made DPSP supreme." — No, made balance supreme.

**Practice MCQs:**
1. "FR prevail over DPSP" was held in:
a) Champakam Dorairajan 1951 b) Minerva Mills c) 2024 Property case d) None
**Answer: (a).**

2. Harmony between FR and DPSP as basic structure was held in:
a) Minerva Mills 1980 b) Champakam c) Golaknath only d) None
**Answer: (a).**

## Section 27: Amendment Spine — 42nd, 44th, 86th, 97th

**42nd Amendment 1976 (Mini-Constitution, Emergency):**
- Amended Art 39(f) (child protection wording strengthened).
- Added Art 39A (legal aid), 43A (workers in management), 48A (environment).
- Extended 31C to all DPSP (later struck in Minerva).
- *Memory:* 42nd = 3 Additions (39A,43A,48A) + 1 change (39f).

**44th Amendment 1978 (Janata correction):**
- Added Art 38(2) (minimise income inequality).
- Deleted right to property as FR (Art 19(1)(f)+31 → shifted to Art 300A legal right). This reduced FR-DPSP clash over property.
- *Memory:* 44th = 38(2) + property out.

**86th Amendment 2002:**
- Rewrote Art 45 (now only 0-6 care).
- Added Art 21A (6-14 education as FR).
- Added Fundamental Duty 51A(k) (parents to educate 6-14).
- *Trap:* If question says "Art 45 education till 14" — that is pre-86th.

**97th Amendment 2011:**
- Added Art 43B (cooperatives) + Part IXB + added "cooperative societies" in Art 19(1)(c).
- *Memory:* 97th = Cooperatives.

**Other link:** 73rd (panchayats — Art 40) and 74th (municipalities) fulfil DPSP but are outside Part IV.

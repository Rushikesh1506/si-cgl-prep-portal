Day-1: Why equality? Articles 14 to 18.

My dear student, before touching any Article, close the book and listen to a story. In British days, at a railway station like yours, there were separate waiting rooms — Europeans here, Indians there. In clubs, Indians were not allowed. In jobs, an Indian could not become an officer however talented he was. The law itself treated people unequally. Our freedom fighters watched this every day and promised: in free India, every person will stand equal before law. That promise is Articles 14 to 18. That is why the Constitution puts equality FIRST among rights — because without it, every other right belongs only to rich and powerful people.

Now, a second problem. Even if government becomes fair, society was unfair. In villages, some people could not draw water from the common well. They could not enter temples. Children could not sit with others in school. This cruelty was done not by government but by society itself. So the makers of the Constitution understood one deep thing: equality needs two medicines. One medicine controls the government. Another medicine controls society. Art 14 controls government. Art 17 attacks society's cruelty directly. Remember this line, it will save you marks: Art 14 stops the STATE from discriminating; Art 17 stops SOCIETY from practising untouchability.

Let us understand Art 14 slowly, because examiners love it. It has two phrases. First phrase, equality before law, came from a British professor named Dicey. Its meaning is simple: nobody is above law. A minister and a railway porter face the same court for the same crime. Second phrase, equal protection of laws, came from America. Its meaning: people in the SAME condition must get the SAME treatment. Example: income tax slab — a man earning five lakhs and a man earning fifty lakhs are NOT in the same condition, so different tax is fair. But two clerks with the same salary and same work must get the same pay — denying one is unfair.

From this comes the golden rule of Art 14. The Constitution bans what is called class legislation. That means picking one person or group for special treatment with NO good reason. Example: a rule saying only houses painted red must pay double tax — that is class legislation, it will be struck down. But the Constitution allows what is called reasonable classification. That means grouping people on a REAL difference, for the PURPOSE of that law. Any valid grouping must pass two questions. First, is the difference real and visible? Doctors and engineers are really different for a medical allowance. Second, does that difference connect to the aim of the law? Medical allowance for doctors connects to health duty. Giving it only to engineers would fail. If either answer is no, the law violates Art 14.

Now meet two judges who changed everything. In 1974, an officer named Royappa was denied promotion without any reason given. Justice Bhagwati said a historic line: equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies. If government acts with no reason, that alone breaks Art 14, even if no grouping is involved. Four years later, Maneka Gandhi's passport was seized by government saying only public interest, no hearing given. The Court said: any procedure that takes away liberty must be fair, just and reasonable. A rule can look fair on paper but if applied unfairly, it still breaks Art 14. So whenever you see a question where government acted without reason or hearing, answer Art 14 violation. That is the Royappa-Maneka blade.

Art 15 is for citizens only. Note this difference carefully, examiner traps it every time: Art 14 is for ALL persons including foreigners, but Arts 15 and 16 are for CITIZENS only. Art 15 bans discrimination on exactly five grounds: religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth. Say them like a prayer: religion, race, caste, sex, birthplace. Two words are NOT in Art 15: descent and residence. They appear only in Art 16 for jobs. If a question mentions descent or residence, answer can never be Art 15.

Art 15 has two faces. First face is the ban. No citizen shall face discrimination in government jobs, and no citizen shall be denied entry to shops, restaurants, hotels, wells, tanks, roads and public resorts maintained by the state. Read that slowly: shops, restaurants and hotels are covered even if privately owned — the owner cannot refuse a person on caste or religion grounds. But wells, tanks and roads are covered only when maintained fully or partly by state money. A private farm pond is not covered. A hotel refusing a Dalit family food is violating Art 15, and for an SI this also means registering a case under the atrocity law. That is your real-life duty, not just exam theory.

Second face of Art 15 is permission: the Constitution ALLOWS special help. 15(3) allows special laws for women and children, like maternity benefits and girls schools. 15(4) allows help for socially backward classes. 15(5), added in 2005, allows reservation in private colleges except minority institutions run under Art 30. 15(6), added in 2019, gives 10 percent to poor among forward classes, called EWS. Old books say these help-clauses are exceptions to equality. That is outdated and the examiner knows it. In the Mandal case of 1992, nine judges said these are NOT exceptions but the real method of equality — because treating unequal people identically keeps them unequal forever. If an option says reservation is an exception to equality, mark it false with full confidence.

Art 16 is also citizens only, but only about government jobs. It repeats the ban with seven grounds — the same five plus descent and residence. Only Parliament, never a state assembly, can fix a residence condition for jobs in a state. Reservations here follow the same Mandal logic: justified, but total must stay within fifty percent except extraordinary situations, and the creamy layer, the rich among backward classes, must be excluded. Your own SI recruitment runs through this Article combined with Art 371D, under which the President's order creates local zones and quotas for Andhra youth. So when you study Art 16, you are studying the legal mother of your own appointment letter.

Art 17 is one line but absolute. Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden and punishable by law. No exception, no reasonable limit, no emergency excuse. In 2020 UPSC asked under which right untouchability falls, and students who memorised headings wrongly marked exploitation. It sits under Right to Equality, Articles 14 to 18. Never forget its address.

Art 18 finishes the story. The state shall give no titles like Raja or Nawab, except military and academic distinctions like Param Vir Chakra or university degrees. No citizen may accept any title from a foreign country without the President's permission. And Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are NOT titles — the Supreme Court said in 1996 they only honour excellence, but you must not use them as a prefix before your name.

Every rule has shields, and examiner asks them. The President and Governors cannot be dragged to court while in office — Art 361. During Emergency, enforcement of most rights can be suspended — Art 359 — but Arts 20 and 21 can never be suspended after the 44th Amendment of 1978. Laws genuinely giving effect to village-resource principles in Art 39(b) and (c) are shielded from Art 14 challenge — Art 31C — but after the Minerva Mills case of 1980, ONLY those two clauses, not all welfare principles. Foreign diplomats and soldiers under special service rules also stand outside Art 14.

Now the history chain, learn it like stations on your route. British Dicey spoke of rule of law. America wrote equal protection in 1868. Our freedom movement demanded equal citizenship. Constitution adopted both phrases in 1950. Early Court built the grouping test. Royappa 1974 added the no-arbitrariness blade. Maneka 1978 demanded fair procedure. The 44th Amendment of 1978 repaired Emergency excesses. Minerva 1980 balanced welfare and rights. Mandal 1992 settled reservations. 103rd Amendment 2019 added EWS, upheld in 2022. Read this chain twice and sequencing questions become free marks.

How to remember all this without fear? Think of one family. Art 14 is the strict father watching every government act. Art 15 is the mother protecting citizens in daily life. Art 16 is the elder brother guarding government jobs. Art 17 is the youngest child with zero tolerance for cruelty. Art 18 is the grandfather throwing old feudal titles out of the house. When a question comes, ask: who is complaining, foreigner or citizen? Foreigner means only Art 14 can help. Citizen in daily life means Art 15. Citizen in jobs means Art 16. Cruelty of untouchability means Art 17. Title or award means Art 18. Then check shields: immunity, emergency, welfare-shield. This five-step walk solves any question in under a minute, even if you forget exact words.

Tomorrow in the exam hall, remember you are not just answering law. You are the future officer who will refuse an arbitrary order, register the atrocity case the village fears to file, and recruit fairly under the same Articles. That is why this lesson is first.

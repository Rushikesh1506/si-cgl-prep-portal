# 🏛️ CASE 15 — In re Berubari Union (1960)
*Why it matters: it is half of the Preamble question. Kesavananda is the other half.*

### 1. Background
**Berubari** was a small area in West Bengal. Under an agreement between Nehru and the Pakistani Prime Minister, part of it was to be **transferred to Pakistan (then East Pakistan)**.

A doubt arose: **can the Government of India simply give away Indian territory?** The President referred the question to the Supreme Court under **Art 143** — the advisory jurisdiction.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. One argument made was that the **Preamble** declares India a "Sovereign Democratic Republic" whose territory belongs to "We, the People", and so territory could not be ceded.
2. To answer that, the Court had to decide **what legal status the Preamble has**.

### 3. What the Court decided
- **The Preamble is NOT a part of the Constitution.** It is a key to open the mind of the makers — useful for **interpretation** where a provision is ambiguous — but it is **not a source of power or of limitation**.
- On the main question: **ceding territory requires a constitutional amendment**, not an ordinary law. (Parliament then passed the **9th Amendment, 1960**.)

### 4. What changed later
In ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**, the Court **reversed the first holding**: the **Preamble IS a part of the Constitution**, and it **can be amended** — but not so as to damage the basic structure.

So far the Preamble has been amended **exactly once**, by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, which added **Socialist, Secular and Integrity**.

### 5. Simple analogy
The preface of a textbook. In 1960 the Court said the preface is not part of the syllabus — it only tells you what the book is about. In 1973 it said no, the preface **is** part of the book, and it can be edited, so long as the editing does not turn it into a different book altogether.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Berubari 1960: Preamble NOT part.** **Kesavananda 1973: Preamble IS part, and amendable.**
- Amended **once only** — the 42nd.
- The date **26 November 1949** printed in the Preamble is the date of **adoption**. Commencement was **26 January 1950** and that date is **not** in the Preamble.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Multi-statement questions combining the Berubari holding with a false claim that the Preamble has been amended "more than once", or that the date in it is the date of commencement.

# 🏛️ CASE 16 — Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005)
*Why it matters: candidates remember half of this case and lose the mark on the other half.*

### 1. Background
**Art 12** defines "the State" for Fundamental Rights purposes. It covers the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, and **"all local or other authorities"** within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Courts developed the **"instrumentality or agency" test** (*R.D. Shetty*, *Ajay Hasia*) to decide whether a body counts as an "other authority": Does the government hold deep financial control? Does it enjoy a monopoly? Does it perform functions close to governmental functions? Is its management State-controlled?

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. The **BCCI** — the Board of Control for Cricket in India — cancelled a television rights contract with Zee Telefilms.
2. Zee filed a **writ petition under Art 32**, which requires the respondent to be "**State**" under Art 12.
3. So the Court had to decide: **is the BCCI "State"?**
4. The BCCI is a **private society**. It receives **no government funding** and the government does not control its management. But it **selects the national team, controls all organised cricket in India, and effectively speaks for India in the sport** — functions that look public.

### 3. What the Court decided — BOTH halves matter
- **The BCCI is NOT "State" under Art 12.** It fails the financial-control and governmental-control limbs. So a **writ under Art 32 does not lie** against it.
- **BUT the BCCI IS amenable to Art 226**, because it discharges **public functions**. **Art 226 runs against "any person or authority"** — it does **not** require the respondent to be "State" under Art 12.

So the aggrieved party is **not** without a remedy — the forum simply shifts from the Supreme Court under Art 32 to a High Court under Art 226.

### 4. Simple analogy
A private club runs the only cricket ground in town and decides who may play. It is not a government body, so you cannot invoke the constitutional guarantee that binds government. **But** because it performs a function the whole town depends on, a court can still review its decisions under a wider power.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Not "State" under Art 12 → no Art 32.** **Public function → Art 226 still available.**
- The underlying rule: **Art 12 governs Art 32. Art 226 does not need Art 12.**
- This is why **Art 226 is wider in scope** than Art 32 — though Art 32 is the **stronger guarantee**, being itself a Fundamental Right.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Statement I / Statement II format: "The BCCI is not amenable to the writ jurisdiction of a High Court" (**false**) paired with "Zee Telefilms held the BCCI is not State under Art 12" (**true**).

# 🏛️ CASE 17 — The Judges Cases and the NJAC (1993 · 1998 · 2015)
*Why it matters: it explains how judges are appointed, and why an amendment was struck down.*

### 1. Background — what the Constitution actually says
**Art 124** says a Supreme Court judge is appointed by the **President after CONSULTATION with** such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President thinks necessary, and that the **Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted** for an appointment other than that of the CJI.

The whole controversy is about one word: **"consultation"**. Does it mean "ask, then decide for yourself", or does it mean "obtain agreement"?

### 2. The sequence, step by step
**First Judges Case (1981) — S.P. Gupta.** The Court held "consultation" does **not** mean concurrence. The **executive had primacy** in appointments.

**Second Judges Case (1993).** A nine-judge bench **reversed** that. "Consultation" was read to mean **concurrence**. The CJI's opinion, formed collectively with senior judges, would have **primacy**. This created the **collegium**.

**Third Judges Case (1998).** A Presidential Reference under **Art 143** asked the Court to clarify the working of the collegium. The Court held the CJI must consult a **collegium of four senior-most judges** of the Supreme Court for SC appointments (two for High Court appointments), and that a recommendation made without that consultation is not binding.

**The 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act (2014).** Parliament created the **National Judicial Appointments Commission** to replace the collegium. Its composition: the **CJI** (chair), the **two senior-most SC judges**, the **Union Law Minister**, and **two eminent persons** selected by a committee of the Prime Minister, the CJI and the Leader of the Opposition. Any **two members could veto** a recommendation.

**Struck down (2015).** A five-judge bench struck down **both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act** by **4:1**, holding that the scheme violated the **independence of the judiciary**, which is part of the **basic structure**. The presence of the Law Minister and the two-member veto were treated as permitting executive interference. The **collegium was revived**.

### 3. Simple analogy
A school must appoint teachers. Version 1: the management decides after "consulting" the head teacher — the head's view can be ignored. Version 2: the head teacher and three seniors decide, and the management can send a name back only once. Version 3: a mixed panel including a management representative, where any two members can block a name. A court says Version 3 lets management control who teaches, which compromises the teachers' independence — so it goes back to Version 2.

### 4. 📌 Key takeaway
- **"Consultation" means CONCURRENCE** — Second Judges Case, 1993.
- **Collegium = CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges** (Third Judges Case, 1998).
- **NJAC / 99th Amendment struck down in 2015** for violating **judicial independence**, a basic feature.
- Note the irony worth remembering: **S.P. Gupta (1981)** is famous for **two** different things — the First Judges Case **and** the expansion of **PIL**.

### 5. 🎯 How it is asked
"Did the NJAC replace the collegium?" → **No** — it was struck down before becoming operational. Any statement saying the collegium was replaced is **false**.

# 🏛️ CASE 18 — How PIL was born: Hussainara Khatoon, S.P. Gupta, Bandhua Mukti Morcha
*Why it matters: it explains how a letter can become a Supreme Court case.*

### 1. Background — the problem PIL solved
Normally, only the person **actually injured** can go to court. That rule is called **locus standi** — "standing to sue".

The difficulty is obvious: the people whose rights are violated most often — undertrial prisoners, bonded labourers, children in hazardous work — are exactly the people who **cannot afford a lawyer, do not know their rights, and cannot reach a court**. A strict locus standi rule leaves them without a remedy.

### 2. The three building blocks

**Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) — the first PIL.**
A lawyer read a newspaper report about the condition of **undertrial prisoners in Bihar**. It revealed that thousands were in jail awaiting trial for periods **longer than the maximum sentence** for the offence they were charged with — some for years, for petty offences.

The Court held:
- **A speedy trial is part of the right to life and personal liberty under Art 21.**
- **Free legal aid** to an indigent accused is an essential ingredient of a fair procedure under Art 21, read with **Art 39A** (a Directive Principle).
- It ordered the **release of thousands** of undertrials.

**S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) — locus standi relaxed.**
The Court held that **any member of the public acting bona fide** may move the court on behalf of a person or class whose rights are violated and who cannot approach the court themselves due to **poverty, disability or a socially or economically disadvantaged position**. It also accepted **epistolary jurisdiction** — treating a **letter** as a writ petition.

**Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) — bonded labour.**
An organisation wrote to the Court about **bonded labourers in stone quarries in Haryana**. The letter was treated as a writ petition under **Art 32**. The Court appointed **commissioners to investigate on the ground**, and held that bonded labour violates **Art 21** and **Art 23**, ordering identification, release and rehabilitation.

### 3. What PIL is NOT
- It is **not** for settling **private disputes** dressed up as public causes.
- Courts now impose **costs for frivolous PILs**, sometimes describing the misuse as "publicity interest litigation".

### 4. Simple analogy
A village well is poisoned. Under the old rule, only someone who has already fallen ill may complain — and the sick are too weak to walk to the office. PIL says: **any honest neighbour may report it on their behalf, and a letter is enough to start the process.**

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Hussainara Khatoon 1979** — first PIL; **speedy trial** and **free legal aid** under Art 21.
- **S.P. Gupta 1981** — **locus standi relaxed**; **epistolary jurisdiction**.
- **Bandhua Mukti Morcha 1984** — bonded labour; commissioners appointed.
- The enforcement mechanism: **a Directive Principle (39A) becomes enforceable when read into a Fundamental Right (Art 21).**

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Assertion–Reason where the Reason is **Art 39A** and the Assertion claims legal aid is **enforceable**. Both are true, but **39A alone cannot explain enforceability** — the missing link is **Art 21**. The answer is "**both true, R does not explain A**".

# 🏛️ CASE 19 — People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) — "the Asiad Workers case"
*Why it matters: it turned Art 23 from a rule about slavery into a rule about wages.*

### 1. Background
Delhi was preparing to host the **1982 Asian Games**. Stadiums, flyovers and hotels were being built at speed, using large numbers of **migrant construction workers** engaged through contractors.

PUDR, a civil liberties organisation, commissioned an investigation. It found workers being paid **below the statutory minimum wage**, women paid less than men, children under 14 employed on sites, and labour laws generally ignored. PUDR wrote a **letter** to Justice Bhagwati, which was treated as a writ petition.

### 2. The legal problem
**Art 23** prohibits "traffic in human beings, **begar** and other similar forms of **forced labour**".

"Begar" historically meant being made to work **without any payment**. Here the workers **were** being paid — just less than the legal minimum. The contractors argued this was, at worst, a violation of the **Minimum Wages Act**, a statutory matter — not a **constitutional** violation, and certainly not "forced labour", since the workers had come voluntarily.

### 3. What the Court decided
The Court rejected that reading:

- **"Forced labour" is not limited to physical force.** Force includes **compulsion arising from hunger and poverty**, from want and destitution.
- A person who works for **less than the minimum wage** is not doing so out of choice. He accepts it because the alternative is starvation. **That economic compulsion is itself "force".**
- Therefore **payment below the statutory minimum wage amounts to FORCED LABOUR and violates Art 23.**
- Art 23 is available **against private persons**, not just the State — so the contractors were directly bound.
- The Court also held that a violation of Art 24 (children under 14 in construction, a hazardous occupation) was made out.

### 4. Why this is enormously important for a police officer
It changes what you look for at a scene. The lay assumption is that bonded labour requires **locks, guards or physical restraint**. After PUDR:

> **The trigger is WAGES and DEBT, not chains.**

A brick kiln where workers are paid below minimum wage against an advance is, on the face of it, a matter attracting Art 23 and the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976**, without any need to prove confinement.

### 5. Simple analogy
A man "agrees" to sell his kidney for a fraction of its value because his child needs surgery today. Technically he consented. But nobody would call it a free choice. The Court applied the same reasoning to wages: **consent given under the pressure of hunger is not consent.**

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Sub-minimum wage = forced labour = Art 23 violation** (PUDR, 1982).
- Art 23 binds **private persons** and protects **all persons**, not only citizens.
- Statutes: **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976**; **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956**; BNS provisions on trafficking.
- **Art 35** is why these are **central** laws: only Parliament may prescribe punishment for a Part III offence.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Multi-statement questions where the discriminating statement is "payment of less than the minimum wage has been held to amount to forced labour" — **true**.

# 🏛️ CASE 20 — M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996) — the Sivakasi case
*Why it matters: it is the leading case on child labour, and it shows what a court does beyond declaring a right.*

### 1. Background
**Sivakasi** in Tamil Nadu is the centre of India's **fireworks and match industry**. A fire at a factory there drew attention to the widespread employment of **children in hazardous manufacturing**.

**Art 24** prohibits the employment of a child **below 14** in a **factory, mine or other hazardous employment**.

### 2. What the Court decided — and the practical scheme it built
The Court went past a simple declaration and issued directions:

- Employers of children in hazardous industries must pay **compensation of ₹20,000 per child** into a **Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund**.
- The **appropriate government** must contribute **₹5,000 per child** if it cannot provide employment to an adult member of the family.
- The child withdrawn from work must be **put into education**, with the fund's income used to support the family.
- A survey of child labour was to be completed and the scheme monitored.

The reasoning tied **Art 24** to **Art 21A / Art 45 (education)** and **Art 39(e)–(f)** (children not to be abused and to be given opportunities to develop in conditions of freedom and dignity). Simply removing a child from a factory without supporting the family, the Court recognised, pushes the family into deeper distress.

### 3. The statute that went further than the Constitution
Understand this distinction clearly, because it is the exam trap:

| Instrument | What it prohibits |
| --- | --- |
| **Art 24** | Child under 14 in a **factory, mine or other hazardous employment** — **not** all work |
| **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016** | **ALL employment of children below 14**, except help in a **family enterprise outside school hours** and the **entertainment industry**; and creates **"adolescents" (14–18)** barred from **hazardous** occupations |

So a statement saying "all employment of children below 14 is prohibited" is **false under Art 24** but **true under the 2016 Act**. Read which instrument the question is asking about.

### 4. Simple analogy
Taking a child out of a factory is like pulling someone out of a river — necessary, but not sufficient. If you leave them on the bank with nothing, they go back in. The compensation fund and the schooling requirement are the towel, the dry clothes and the road home.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **M.C. Mehta (Sivakasi) 1996** — compensation fund, rehabilitation, education.
- **Art 24 = factory, mine, hazardous.** **2016 Act = all work under 14, with two narrow exceptions.**
- **Arts 23 and 24 are horizontal** — they bind private employers directly.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Statement pairs contrasting the **constitutional** ban with the **statutory** ban. The candidate must notice which one is being described.

# 🏛️ CASE 21 — T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002)
*Why it matters: it settles who counts as a "minority" and what Art 30 actually protects.*

### 1. Background
**Art 30(1)** gives "all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to **establish and administer** educational institutions of their choice."

Two questions were unresolved:
1. **Minority in relation to what?** India as a whole, or the State? A community may be a minority nationally but a majority in one State — Sikhs in Punjab, for example.
2. **How far does "administer" go?** Can the State regulate admissions, fees and staff appointments in a minority institution?

### 2. What the Court decided — an eleven-judge bench
- **The unit for determining minority status is the STATE, not the country.** Since States were reorganised on linguistic lines, a religious or linguistic minority must be identified **State-wise**.
- **The right under Art 30 is not absolute.** The State may impose **reasonable regulations** to ensure **academic excellence and proper administration** — qualifications of teachers, standards of education, conditions of service, rules against maladministration.
- But regulation must **not destroy the minority character** of the institution or take away its right to administer.
- The Court also dealt with **unaided private institutions generally**, holding they have autonomy in admissions and fee structure, subject to the bar on **profiteering and capitation fees**.

### 3. The connection to Art 15(5)
The **93rd Amendment (2005)** inserted **Art 15(5)**, enabling reservation in admissions in **private unaided institutions** — but it **expressly excepts minority educational institutions**. That carve-out exists precisely to protect the Art 30 right that T.M.A. Pai had just mapped out.

Note the requirement of **both limbs**: a minority must have **established** the institution **and** administer it. An institution merely administered by a minority, but founded by someone else, does not qualify.

### 4. Simple analogy
A community builds and runs its own school so its language and culture survive. The State may insist the teachers be qualified, the building be safe, and the accounts be honest — that is regulation. What the State may not do is dictate who the school admits and who teaches there to the point that the school is no longer the community's school at all.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Eleven judges, 2002. The unit for minority status is the STATE.**
- **Art 30 needs BOTH limbs: establish AND administer.**
- **Regulation is allowed; destruction of minority character is not.**
- **Art 15(5) excepts minority institutions** (93rd Amendment).

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- "What is the unit for determining a linguistic minority?" → the **State**.
- "Which institutions are excepted from Art 15(5)?" → **minority institutions under Art 30**.

# 🏛️ CASE 22 — Sabarimala (2018) — and why it is NOT settled law
*Why it matters: half the study material in circulation states this wrongly.*

### 1. Background
The **Sabarimala** temple in Kerala is dedicated to Lord Ayyappa. By long-standing custom, **women between the ages of 10 and 50** — that is, of menstruating age — were **not permitted to enter**.

The exclusion was challenged as violating **Arts 14, 15, 17 and 25**.

### 2. The competing arguments
- **For the temple:** the practice is an **essential religious practice** of the Ayyappa devotees, protected by **Art 25**, and the devotees form a **religious denomination** entitled to manage its own affairs under **Art 26**.
- **Against:** the exclusion discriminates on the ground of **sex**; it is not essential to the religion; and **Art 25(2)(b)** expressly empowers the State to throw open **Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus**.

### 3. What the Court decided in 2018
By **4:1**, the Court held the exclusion **unconstitutional**:
- The devotees do **not** constitute a separate religious denomination.
- The exclusion is **not an essential religious practice**.
- It violates the **right of women to worship** and the guarantee of equality.

**Justice Indu Malhotra dissented**, holding that courts should not ordinarily interfere in matters of deep religious sentiment, and that the essential-practices test should not be applied to strike down a community's own beliefs.

### 4. ⚠️ What most notes get wrong — the case is PENDING
Review petitions were filed. In **November 2019**, a five-judge bench, by **3:2**, kept the review petitions pending and **referred a set of larger questions to a NINE-JUDGE bench** — questions about the interplay between Arts 25 and 26, the scope of the essential-practices doctrine, and whether a court may examine matters of faith at all.

Those questions **remain pending**. So:

> **Treating Sabarimala 2018 as settled law is incorrect.** The correct answer is that the 2018 judgment exists, and the larger reference is **unresolved**.

### 5. Simple analogy
A match was played and one side won. The losing side appealed, and rather than simply re-examining that match, the appeal body has referred a broader question — *what the rules of this game even are* — to a bigger panel. Until that panel rules, the result of the original match stands on paper, but the rule book itself is under revision.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **2018, 4:1, entry permitted; Indu Malhotra J dissented.**
- **Referred to nine judges in November 2019 — still pending.**
- The empowering provision is **Art 25(2)(b)** (social welfare and reform; throwing open Hindu institutions), with the explanation extending "Hindus" to **Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists**.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Statement pairs: "Sabarimala 2018 is settled law" (**false**) with "a reference to a nine-judge bench is pending" (**true**).

# 🏛️ CASE 23 — The Maratha reservation case (2021) and the 102nd → 105th chain
*Why it matters: it is a perfect cause-and-effect pair, and it is missing from most older material.*

### 1. Background
The **102nd Amendment (2018)** did two things:
- Gave **constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)** by inserting **Art 338B**.
- Inserted **Art 342A**, providing that the **President**, in consultation with the Governor, shall specify the socially and educationally backward classes for a State, and that **Parliament** may include or exclude classes from that list.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. Maharashtra enacted a law granting reservation to the **Maratha community**, taking total reservation in the State **beyond 50%**.
2. It was challenged. In 2021 a five-judge bench struck down the Maratha quota, holding there were **no extraordinary circumstances** justifying a breach of the **Indra Sawhney 50% ceiling**.
3. But the Court went further on a second question. By a majority, it read **Art 342A** as meaning that after the 102nd Amendment, **only the President (and Parliament) could identify SEBCs** — **States had lost that power**.

### 3. Why that caused a crisis
Every State maintains its own list of backward classes for State-level reservation. If only the Centre could identify them, **State lists would be paralysed**. There was strong cross-party objection.

### 4. The response — the 105th Amendment (2021)
Parliament enacted the **105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021** to **restore the power of States and Union Territories to prepare and maintain their own lists** of socially and educationally backward classes. It clarified that the Central list under Art 342A is for **Central government purposes**, and that States retain their own lists.

### 5. Simple analogy
A head office issues a circular to standardise a form. A court reads the circular as meaning branch offices may no longer maintain their own customer lists. Every branch grinds to a halt. The head office immediately issues a clarification: **the circular concerned only the head-office list — branches keep theirs.** The clarification is the 105th Amendment.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **102nd (2018):** NCBC constitutional status, **Art 338B**, **Art 342A**.
- **Maratha judgment (2021):** struck the quota for breaching 50%; read the 102nd as removing the States' identification power.
- **105th (2021):** **restored** the States' power.
- Remember the general rule this illustrates: **when two amendments on the same Article sit a few years apart, a judgment usually sits between them.**

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Assertion–Reason: "Parliament enacted the 105th Amendment" because "the 102nd had been interpreted as depriving States of the power to identify SEBCs" → **both true, and R correctly explains A**.

# 🏛️ CASE 24 — Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) — the EWS judgment
*Why it matters: it changed the 50% rule you were taught.*

### 1. Background
The **103rd Amendment (2019)** inserted **Arts 15(6) and 16(6)**, enabling reservation of **up to 10%** in education and public employment for the **Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** among citizens **not already covered** by the existing SC, ST and OBC reservations.

Two features made it constitutionally novel:
- It used **economic criteria alone** as the basis of reservation.
- It **excluded** SCs, STs and OBCs from its benefit.
- It took **total reservation past the 50% mark** laid down in *Indra Sawhney*.

### 2. What the Court decided — 3:2, November 2022
The majority **upheld the amendment**, holding:

- **Reservation based solely on economic criteria does not violate the basic structure.** Affirmative action may address economic disadvantage, not only social and educational backwardness.
- **Excluding SC/ST/OBC from the EWS quota does not violate the equality code**, because those groups already receive reservation under separate provisions — so the exclusion avoids double benefit rather than creating discrimination.
- Crucially: **the 50% ceiling is NOT part of the basic structure.** It was a rule of prudence laid down for **Art 16(4)** reservations, not an inviolable constitutional limit. The **EWS 10% therefore operates OUTSIDE the Indra Sawhney ceiling.**

The **two dissenting judges** held that excluding the poor among SCs, STs and OBCs from an economic-criteria quota was itself discriminatory, and that breaching the 50% ceiling would let it become the norm.

### 3. What you must now unlearn
Older material — including the original version of this pack — states flatly that **"reservation can never exceed 50%."** After 2022 that is **wrong**. The accurate position:

> **The 50% ceiling governs Art 16(4) backward-class reservation. It does not bind EWS, and it is not part of the basic structure.**

### 4. Simple analogy
A hall has 100 chairs and a house rule says "at most 50 may be kept aside for special-needs guests." A new category of guest is then created by a separate rule, with its own 10 chairs. Someone objects that the total now exceeds 50. The management replies: the "50" rule was written about **that** category of special-needs guests — this is a **different scheme under a different rule**, so it counts separately.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **103rd Amendment upheld 3:2 in 2022.**
- **50% ceiling ≠ basic structure. EWS sits outside it.**
- Economic criteria **alone** can be a valid basis for affirmative action.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Assertion–Reason where the Assertion says a combined figure above 50% is "**automatically**" unconstitutional. The Reason (Indra Sawhney fixed a ceiling) is **true**; the Assertion is **false**. Answer: **A false, R true**.

# 🏛️ CASE 25 — The Governor's assent saga (2023 → April 2025 → November 2025)
*Why it matters: it is the most recent and most volatile item in the entire Polity syllabus.*

### 1. Background — what Art 200 says
When a State legislature passes a Bill, it goes to the **Governor**, who has **four options**:
1. Give **assent**.
2. **Withhold** assent.
3. **Return** it for reconsideration (not a Money Bill) — and if the House passes it again, the Governor "**shall not withhold assent**".
4. **Reserve** it for the consideration of the **President** (then **Art 201** applies).

The gap in the text: **Art 200 prescribes NO TIME LIMIT.** A Governor may therefore sit on a Bill indefinitely — sometimes called a "pocket veto".

### 2. The sequence, step by step

**Step 1 — Punjab (2023).** The Supreme Court held that a Governor **cannot sit on Bills indefinitely** and thereby frustrate the legislative process. The phrase "as soon as possible" in the first proviso to Art 200 carries real content.

**Step 2 — Tamil Nadu (April 2025).** A large number of Bills had been pending with the Governor for extended periods. The Court went considerably further: it **prescribed timelines** for the Governor and the President to act under Arts 200 and 201, and held that if those timelines were not met, the Bills would be **"deemed to have been assented to"** — the concept of **deemed assent**.

**Step 3 — the Presidential Reference (20 November 2025).** The President referred questions to the Supreme Court under **Art 143**. A **five-judge Constitution Bench** delivered its opinion, holding that:
- Courts **CANNOT judicially prescribe timelines** for the Governor or the President under Arts 200 and 201, those provisions containing no such limits.
- The concept of **"deemed assent" is impermissible** — a court cannot substitute its own act for the constitutional act of assent.
- **However**, prolonged and unexplained inaction is **not beyond scrutiny**. The remedy is a limited one: a court may direct the constitutional authority to **decide**, but cannot decide for it.

### 3. Simple analogy
A file needs the director's signature. The rules say "sign as soon as possible" but give no deadline. The director sits on it for two years. A tribunal first says: you cannot sit on it forever. Then it says: if you do not sign within three months, we will **treat it as signed**. On review, a higher body says: no — **we can order the director to make up his mind, but we cannot forge his signature.**

### 4. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Chain: Punjab 2023 → Tamil Nadu April 2025 (timelines + deemed assent) → Presidential Reference opinion 20 November 2025 (no timelines, no deemed assent, but inaction still reviewable).**
- Any material still teaching **"deemed assent"** as current law is **out of date**.
- Structural point that never changes: **Art 111 (President, central Bills) has THREE options; Art 200 (Governor, State Bills) has FOUR.** The extra one is **reservation for the President** — because there is no higher authority for the President to reserve to.

### 5. ⚠️ Verify before the exam
This is the **fastest-moving item** in the syllabus. Confirm the position from a current-affairs source in the month before your paper.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Chronology sets placing the three stages in order, and statement questions asking what the position "at present" is.

# 🏛️ CASE 26 — I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)
*Why it matters: it closed the last loophole in the basic structure doctrine.*

### 1. Background — what the Ninth Schedule is
The **First Amendment (1951)** created **Art 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule**. Any law placed in that Schedule was **immune from challenge on the ground that it violated Fundamental Rights**.

It was created to protect **land reform laws** from litigation. But over the decades, governments began inserting all kinds of legislation into it — by 2007 the Schedule contained **hundreds of entries**, many having nothing to do with land reform. It had become a **legislative safe-deposit box**.

### 2. The problem
If a law inside the Ninth Schedule cannot be challenged, Parliament could place **any** law there and place it beyond judicial review — effectively bypassing the basic structure doctrine altogether.

### 3. What the Court decided — nine judges, 2007
- Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule **after 24 April 1973** — the date of the **Kesavananda Bharati** judgment — **ARE open to challenge** on the ground that they damage the **basic structure**.
- The test is one of **effect**: the Court will ask whether the law, in substance, abrogates or abridges rights forming part of the basic structure, applying the "rights test" and the "essence of the right" test.
- Laws inserted **before** that date remain protected.

### 4. Simple analogy
A safe-deposit box was provided for one specific purpose, and the bank promised not to inspect its contents. Over the years the customer started storing anything at all in it. The bank eventually says: everything deposited before a stated date stays sealed, but **anything put in after that date can be inspected** — because otherwise the box becomes a way of defeating every rule the bank has.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **The Ninth Schedule is NOT an absolute shield.**
- **Cut-off: 24 April 1973** — the Kesavananda date.
- Nine judges, 2007.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
Assertion–Reason: "A law in the Ninth Schedule is immune from judicial review" (**false after 2007**) with "Art 31B protects Ninth Schedule laws from Part III challenge" (**true as text**). Answer: **A false, R true**.

# 🏛️ TOPIC 27 — PESA 1996: why a separate law was needed for Scheduled Areas
*Not a case, but the most under-explained topic in the pack — and directly relevant to policing in AP.*

### 1. Background — the gap
The **73rd Amendment (1992)** created Part IX and made panchayats constitutional bodies across India.

But **Art 243M** says Part IX does **NOT apply** to:
- **Scheduled Areas** under the **Fifth Schedule** and **tribal areas** under the **Sixth Schedule**;
- the States of **Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram**;
- the **hill areas of Manipur** having a district council;
- the **Darjeeling** area of West Bengal so far as the Gorkha Hill Council is concerned.

**Why exclude them?** Because tribal areas already had their own traditional systems of self-governance, and a uniform panchayat structure imposed from outside could displace them. The exclusion was **protective**, not punitive.

### 2. The problem the exclusion created
The exclusion meant tribal areas had **no constitutional framework of local self-government at all**. So **Art 243M(4)(b)** empowered **Parliament** to extend Part IX to those areas **with exceptions and modifications**.

Parliament did exactly that through the **Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — PESA**, following the recommendations of the **Bhuria Committee**.

### 3. What PESA actually gives — and why it changes policing
The central shift: **the Gram Sabha — the assembly of ALL registered voters of the village — becomes the primary authority**, not the elected Gram Panchayat. Specifically, the Gram Sabha:

- **Approves plans, programmes and projects** for social and economic development, and **identifies beneficiaries** of poverty-alleviation schemes.
- Must be **consulted before land acquisition** in a Scheduled Area, and before the **resettlement or rehabilitation** of displaced persons.
- Has the power to **prevent alienation of tribal land** and to **restore** land unlawfully alienated.
- **Owns minor forest produce.**
- May **regulate or prohibit the sale and consumption of intoxicants**.
- Controls **money lending** to Scheduled Tribes.
- Manages **minor water bodies**, and its recommendation is required for **prospecting licences and mining leases for minor minerals**.

### 4. Why this matters in Andhra Pradesh
AP has **Scheduled Areas** — tracts in **Alluri Sitharama Raju, Parvathipuram Manyam, Eluru, Palnadu, Srikakulam, Anakapalli and Kakinada** districts. An officer posted there is working under a **different local-governance regime** from one posted in a coastal district.

**A practical example.** A contractor begins extracting minor minerals in a Scheduled Area village, producing a State-issued permit. Villagers obstruct the work and a law-and-order situation develops.

- The instinct is to treat this as obstruction and register a case.
- But under PESA, the **Gram Sabha's recommendation is required** for such a lease. The villagers may be asserting a **statutory right**.
- **The first verification is whether the Gram Sabha resolution exists.** Acting without that check risks proceeding against the very statute the State enacted for that area.

### 5. Simple analogy
A national building code is issued for all towns. Heritage districts are **exempted**, to protect their character. But then the heritage districts have **no** building rules at all. So a **special code** is written for them — same objective, adapted to local conditions, with the residents' assembly given the final say. That special code is PESA.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Excluded by Art 243M → restored by PESA, 1996.** One phrase answers every question here.
- **The Gram Sabha, not the Panchayat, is the primary authority** in a Scheduled Area.
- **Land alienation, minor forest produce, minor minerals, intoxicants, money lending** — the five powers to remember.
- **AP has Scheduled Areas**, so this is operational law, not theory.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Assertion–Reason: "Part IX was extended to Scheduled Areas by a separate Act of Parliament" because "Art 243M provides that Part IX shall not apply to them" → **both true, R explains A**.

# 🏛️ TOPIC 28 — The Andhra Pradesh chain: Sri Bagh Pact → Gentlemen's Agreement → Six-Point Formula → Art 371D
*The single most AP-specific sequence in the syllabus, and the one the original pack omitted entirely.*

### 1. Why these agreements exist at all
Every time two regions merged to form a larger unit, the **smaller or less-developed region feared domination** by the bigger one — in jobs, in education, in where the capital and institutions would be located. Each agreement below is an attempt to **reassure the weaker partner before a merger**.

Understand that one idea and the whole sequence becomes logical rather than a list of dates.

### 2. Step 1 — the Sri Bagh Pact (1937)
**Who:** leaders of **coastal Andhra** and **Rayalaseema**.
**Why:** Rayalaseema is drier and was less developed. Its leaders feared that in a combined Andhra province, coastal Andhra — richer, with the deltas — would take everything.
**What was agreed:** a distribution of key institutions — the **capital**, the **High Court** and the **university** were not all to go to one region; Rayalaseema was to get a share, and its irrigation needs were to receive priority.
**Named after:** "Sri Bagh", the Madras residence of Kasu Brahmananda Reddy's contemporary, the publisher **Desiraju Hanumantha Rao**, where the leaders met.

### 3. Step 2 — the Gentlemen's Agreement (1956)
**Who:** leaders of **Andhra State** and of the **Telangana region** of Hyderabad State.
**Why:** Telangana had been under the Nizam and was less developed in education and administration. Its leaders feared that on merger, better-educated candidates from coastal Andhra would take the government jobs and the university seats. The **Mulki Rules** of the old Hyderabad State had reserved jobs for locals, and Telangana wanted that protection to continue.
**What was agreed:** safeguards in **employment, education and the allocation of expenditure**, with a Regional Council for Telangana; a convention that if the Chief Minister came from one region, the Deputy Chief Minister would come from the other.
**Result:** on this assurance, **Andhra Pradesh was formed on 1 November 1956.**

### 4. Step 3 — the agitations (1969 and 1972–73)
**1969 — the Telangana agitation.** Telangana leaders argued the safeguards of the Gentlemen's Agreement had not been honoured, and demanded a separate State.

**1972–73 — the Jai Andhra agitation.** A court ruling upholding the **Mulki Rules** provoked the opposite reaction in coastal Andhra and Rayalaseema, where people now demanded a **separate Andhra**. The State became ungovernable and was placed under **President's Rule in January 1973**.

So within four years the State faced **separatist agitations from both directions** — each side believing the 1956 arrangement had failed it.

### 5. Step 4 — the Six-Point Formula (September 1973)
This was the political settlement that ended the crisis. In substance it provided for:
1. **Accelerated development of backward areas**, with a planning board.
2. **Local-area preference in education and employment**, organised through **local cadres**.
3. A **Central University** in Hyderabad to improve educational facilities.
4. **Abolition of the Mulki Rules and the Regional Committee**, replaced by the new scheme.
5. Constitutional amendment to give the arrangement legal force.
6. An **Administrative Tribunal** to resolve service grievances arising from it.

### 6. Step 5 — the 32nd Amendment (1973) and Article 371D
To implement the Formula, Parliament enacted the **Constitution (Thirty-second Amendment) Act, 1973**, inserting:

- **Art 371D** — empowering the **President**, by **order**, to provide for **equitable opportunities in public employment and education**; to require civil posts to be organised into **local cadres**; to specify **local areas** and provide for **direct recruitment by local area**; and to constitute an **Administrative Tribunal** for the State.
- **Art 371E** — requiring Parliament to provide for a **Central University in Andhra Pradesh** → which became the **University of Hyderabad (1974)**.

The operative instrument is the **AP Public Employment (Organisation of Local Cadres and Regulation of Direct Recruitment) Order, 1975** — universally called **"the Presidential Order"**. It defines who counts as a **local candidate** of a zone and how posts are shared between local and non-local candidates.

**Art 371D survived the 2014 bifurcation** and continues to apply to **both Andhra Pradesh and Telangana**.

### 7. Why this is personal to the candidate
**Art 371D is the reason AP recruitment is organised zone-wise.** When an SI is allotted to a particular zone rather than a statewide merit list, that is Art 371D and the Presidential Order operating. Service disputes arising from it go to the Administrative Tribunal machinery.

### 8. The comparison that is the actual exam question

| Provision | Who acts | By what instrument | About what |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Art 16(3)** | **Parliament** | A **law** | A **residence** requirement for a class of posts |
| **Art 371D** | The **President** | An **order** | **Local cadres** and **local-area** recruitment in AP |
| **Art 16(4)** | The **State** | Law or executive order | Reservation for **backward classes** |

### 9. Simple analogy
Two schools merge. The smaller school's parents worry their children will lose out on seats and prizes. Before agreeing, they sign an understanding on how places will be shared (**Gentlemen's Agreement**). Years later they say the understanding was ignored, and both sides threaten to walk out (**the agitations**). A fresh settlement is negotiated (**Six-Point Formula**) — and this time, instead of a handshake, it is written into the **school's own constitution** so it cannot simply be forgotten (**Art 371D**).

### 10. 📌 Key takeaway
- **1937 Sri Bagh** — coastal Andhra + Rayalaseema.
- **1956 Gentlemen's Agreement** — Andhra + Telangana, **before** the merger of 1 Nov 1956.
- **1969 Telangana agitation; 1972–73 Jai Andhra agitation; President's Rule January 1973.**
- **September 1973 Six-Point Formula → 32nd Amendment → Arts 371D and 371E.**
- **Presidential Order 1975** is the operative document. **371D survived bifurcation.**

### 11. 🎯 How it is asked
- Chronology: the commonest error is placing the **Gentlemen's Agreement after** the formation of AP. It came **before**, as the precondition.
- Cause-and-effect: the **Formula precedes the Amendment**, never the reverse.
- Match-the-following pairing each agreement with the **two regions** involved.

# 🏛️ TOPIC 29 — Potti Sriramulu and the two formations (1953 and 1956)
*The most commonly muddled sequence in AP history.*

### 1. The demand
Telugu-speaking districts of the **Madras Presidency** had sought a separate province since the **first Andhra Mahasabha at Bapatla in May 1913**, presided over by **B.N. Sarma**.

⚠️ **Do not confuse this with the Andhra Maha Sabha of Hyderabad State (Telangana)**, first held at **Jogipet, Medak, in 1930**. Two different bodies, two different regions, two different decades — and examiners swap them deliberately.

### 2. The committees that said no
- **Dar Commission (1948)** — advised **against** reorganising States on a purely linguistic basis, fearing it would harm national unity.
- **JVP Committee (1949)** — **J**awaharlal Nehru, **V**allabhbhai Patel and **P**attabhi Sitaramayya. It broadly agreed with Dar, but conceded that an Andhra province might be formed **if the claim to Madras city were given up**.

### 3. What Potti Sriramulu did
**Potti Sriramulu** was a Gandhian and a freedom fighter. When the demand stalled, he began a **fast unto death on 19 October 1952** for a separate Andhra State.

He fasted for **58 days** and **died on 15 December 1952**.

His death triggered **widespread public grief and rioting** across the Telugu districts. Within days the central government announced its agreement to the formation of a separate State.

### 4. The two formations — keep them apart

| | **Andhra State** | **Andhra Pradesh** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Date** | **1 October 1953** | **1 November 1956** |
| **Caused by** | **Potti Sriramulu's death** and the agitation | The **States Reorganisation Commission** report |
| **Legal basis** | **Andhra State Act, 1953** | **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** + **7th Amendment** |
| **Capital** | **Kurnool** (High Court at Guntur) | **Hyderabad** |
| **Made from** | Telugu districts of the **Madras Presidency** | Andhra State **+ Telugu areas of Hyderabad State** |
| **Significance** | **India's FIRST State formed on a linguistic basis** | First CM **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** |

⚠️ **The trap.** The **States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali, with K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru) was appointed in 1953 and reported in 1955.** Andhra State was formed in **October 1953** — that is, **before the SRC reported**. So a statement saying "Andhra State was formed on the recommendation of the SRC" is **false on the dates alone**. The SRC produced **Andhra Pradesh in 1956**, not Andhra State in 1953.

### 5. Simple analogy
A community demands its own school. A committee studies the idea and says no. A respected elder then goes on hunger strike and dies. The authorities immediately sanction a **temporary school building** (1953). Only three years later does the full review committee report, and a **proper school with all the neighbouring wards merged in** is built (1956). Two different buildings, two different causes.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Fast begins 19 Oct 1952; death 15 Dec 1952; Andhra State 1 Oct 1953, capital Kurnool; India's first linguistic State.**
- **SRC appointed 1953, reported 1955 → Andhra Pradesh, 1 Nov 1956.**
- **1953 came from a DEATH. 1956 came from a COMMISSION.** Never merge the two.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
Multi-statement questions where the false statement attributes Andhra State (1953) to the SRC. It is the highest-yield trap in AP history.

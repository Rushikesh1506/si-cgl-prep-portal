# MODULE D — CASE FILES V2, PART 1 (14 cases, Days 1–6)
Written for a first-time learner. Read each one like a story.

## 1. The girl who was told she cannot join medical college — Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

In 1950, Madras State ran medical and engineering colleges. The government had an old rule, called the Communal Government Order. It divided seats by community. Only a fixed number of seats for Brahmins, a fixed number for non-Brahmins, and fixed numbers for Christians, Muslims, and others.

A young Brahmin woman named Champakam Dorairajan wanted to study medicine. When she asked, she was told she had almost no chance, because only two seats out of fourteen were kept for her community, and those were already full. Students from other communities with fewer marks than her could still get in.

Here is one honest detail the Supreme Court itself recorded. She had not actually filled the admission form. She went to court after asking and learning she would be refused because she was a Brahmin. The State even agreed to keep one seat for her if she won. So the case went ahead.

She asked the court: is the government refusing me only because of my caste?

The State answered: we are only following Article 46. That article tells the State to help weaker sections with education. So we are obeying the Constitution.

The court had to answer a bigger question. When a Directive Principle in Part IV and a Fundamental Right in Part III fight with each other, who wins?

The court said the Fundamental Right wins. Directive Principles are goals. They are important, but Article 37 itself says courts cannot enforce them directly. Fundamental Rights can be enforced through the court under Article 32. So a goal cannot crush a right. The Communal Order broke Article 29(2), which says no citizen can be refused admission to a State-run or State-aided college only because of religion, race, caste, or language. The order was cancelled as void under Article 13.

Parliament did not like this result. Within months it passed the First Amendment in 1951. That amendment put in a new clause, Article 15(4). That clause clearly allows the State to make special rules for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes in education. So today's reservation in colleges exists because she won, and Parliament changed the Constitution in reply.

For your exam, hold two lines. Fundamental Rights beat Directive Principles in 1951. And Article 15(4) came through the First Amendment of 1951 because of this case. This is round one of a long fight. Later rounds are Golaknath, Kesavananda, and Minerva Mills.

## 2. The top officer who was moved out of his chair — E. P. Royappa (decided November 1973, quoted as 1974)

E. P. Royappa was an IAS officer in Tamil Nadu. He reached the highest post in the State service, Chief Secretary. Then the government moved him. First he was made Deputy Chairman of the State Planning Commission. Then he was made Officer on Special Duty. His salary stayed the same, Rs. 3,500, the same as Chief Secretary. But he felt these were side posts with less respect. He felt the Chief Minister moved him out of anger.

He went to the Supreme Court. He said the transfer was done in bad faith, that it was a hidden punishment, and that it broke Articles 14, 16, and 311.

On the facts, he lost. The court said he could not prove bad faith. The new posts carried the same pay and were posts of high responsibility. So his personal complaint failed.

But the reason we still read this case is one idea from Justice Bhagwati, writing for a five-judge bench. Before this, Article 14 was mostly tested by one question: did the government divide people into groups in a reasonable way? That test needs two things, a clear difference between groups and a sensible link to the goal. Royappa showed the gap in that test. Here there was no group at all. One man was simply moved. If Article 14 only asked about groups, a purely unreasonable order against one person would escape.

Bhagwati said equality is a living idea with many sides. It cannot be cribbed, cabined, and confined inside the old box. Then came the famous line: equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies. If State action has no reason, it is unequal by itself, and it breaks Article 14, even if nobody divided anyone into classes.

So now Article 14 has two separate doors. One door is the old grouping test. The other door is the no-arbitrariness test. If the State fails either door, its action can fall. Remember it as: no reason means no equality.

If the exam gives you the line about arbitrariness being the opposite of equality, write Royappa, 1974. And remember the twist examiners love: the man lost, the idea won. Later this idea grew into Maneka Gandhi, into government contract cases, and much later into the manifest arbitrariness test.

## 3. The woman whose passport was taken without telling her why — Maneka Gandhi (1978)

To understand this story, first learn the old rule. In A. K. Gopalan in 1950, the court read Article 21 in a narrow way. Article 21 says nobody can lose life or personal liberty except by procedure established by law. Gopalan said that means: if some law makes some procedure, that is enough. The court will not ask whether the procedure is fair. It also said Articles 14, 19, and 21 live in separate closed rooms.

That old rule was dangerous. A law could say a man can be locked up after a coin toss, and still call it procedure.

Maneka Gandhi got a passport in 1976. In July 1977 the government told her to give it back in public interest, under the Passports Act. She asked: tell me the reasons. The government said: we cannot tell you, it is not in public interest to tell you. She got no hearing, no reasons, and no chance to answer.

She asked the court: is this allowed?

The court changed the law. It said Gopalan's narrow reading is over. The procedure in Article 21 must be right, just, and fair. It must not be fanciful, oppressive, or arbitrary. Only existing on paper is not enough. It must also be reasonable. The court also said Articles 14, 19, and 21 are not separate rooms. A law that takes liberty must pass all three together. Teachers call these three together the Golden Triangle.

The court also read natural justice into Article 21. Normally, before you punish a person, you must tell them why and hear them.

Think of a hostel warden who expels a boy and says the rule book allows me to expel anyone. Under Gopalan, the talk ends there. Under Maneka, the court asks three more questions. Were you told why? Did you get a chance to speak? Is the rule itself sensible? If any answer is no, the action fails.

As a future Sub-Inspector, this is your daily bread. Every arrest, detention, or search takes away liberty for some time. After Maneka, it is not enough to say a section of the BNSS allows it. How you do it must also be fair. Tell the grounds, follow the steps, write the reasons. This same root later grew into the arrest rules in D. K. Basu.

For the exam, chain three names. Gopalan in 1950 was narrow. Maneka in 1978 made it wide and fair. Puttaswamy in 2017 took it to privacy. If an option says Article 21 only needs some procedure to exist, that is the dead Gopalan view. It is a favourite trap.

## 4. The fight over 27 percent jobs — Indra Sawhney, the Mandal case (1992, nine judges)

In 1979 the government set up the Mandal Commission to find socially and educationally backward classes. It gave its report in 1980. It said give 27 percent of central government jobs to Other Backward Classes. The report lay still for ten years. In August 1990 the V. P. Singh government said it will apply the 27 percent quota. The country saw huge protests. Many petitions reached the Supreme Court. The Chief Justice sent them to a special bench of nine judges. On 16 November 1992 the court gave its answer, by 6 to 3.

The main attack was: this quota breaks equality of opportunity in jobs under Article 16(1). The government answered: Article 16(4) allows reservation for backward classes not adequately shown in services.

The court laid down four things you must know.

First, Article 16(4) is not an exception cut out of Article 16(1). It is an emphatic statement of the equality already inside 16(1). Treating unequal people in exactly the same way creates inequality. So reservation is a way to reach real equality, not a break from it. Any option that calls reservation an exception to equality is wrong.

Second, the 50 percent ceiling. Reservation should normally not cross 50 percent of posts or seats. Crossing is allowed only in extraordinary situations. The court gave the example of far-flung people living outside the mainstream.

Third, the creamy layer must be kept out. In every backward class there are some families who have already moved ahead in money and position. If they take all the seats, the truly backward get nothing. So the advanced section must be found and excluded.

Fourth, no reservation in promotions. Reservation is for entry into service, not for moving up later. The court also struck down the extra 10 percent quota for poor among upper groups made by the office memo of September 1991, and said economic criterion alone cannot be the base under Article 16(4) as it then stood.

Parliament did not accept the fourth point. It amended the Constitution. The 77th Amendment in 1995 put in Article 16(4A) to bring back reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The 81st Amendment in 2000 put in Article 16(4B) about carrying forward backlog vacancies without hitting the ceiling in one year. The 85th Amendment in 2001 added consequential seniority to 16(4A).

And the 50 percent story has moved since. In Janhit Abhiyan in 2022, by 3 to 2, the court upheld the 103rd Amendment and its 10 percent EWS quota. The majority said the 50 percent ceiling is not inflexible, it applies to reservations under Articles 15(4), 15(5), and 16(4), and it is not part of the basic structure. The 10 percent EWS quota sits outside that ceiling. So the sentence reservation can never cross 50 percent is no longer correct.

For the exam, anchor everything around nine judges and 1992. Creamy layer means this case. The chain to learn is Royappa 1974, then Sawhney 1992, then the 77th and 85th Amendments, then Janhit Abhiyan 2022.

## 5. The monk who stopped Parliament — Kesavananda Bharati (1973, thirteen judges)

In the 1950s and 1960s there was a long war over land reform. States took big estates to give land to the landless. Landowners went to court using the right to property, which was then a Fundamental Right. Parliament kept amending the Constitution to save those land laws.

Underneath lay one question. Can Parliament change anything in the Constitution, without any limit?

First the court said yes in Shankari Prasad in 1951 and Sajjan Singh in 1965. Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights. Then in Golaknath in 1967, by 6 to 5, the court said no. An amendment is law under Article 13(2), so it cannot take away Fundamental Rights. Parliament hit back with the 24th Amendment in 1971. It said an amendment under Article 368 is not law under Article 13, and Parliament can amend any part.

Then came Kesavananda Bharati. He was the head of a mutt, a religious house, in Kerala. The Kerala government wanted to take its land under land reform laws. He challenged it. During the case Parliament also passed the 24th, 25th, and 29th Amendments, and he challenged those too. The case grew from one man's land into the full question of Parliament's power.

The court called thirteen judges, the largest bench ever. They heard arguments for 68 days. The judgment ran over 700 pages, with eleven separate opinions. The result was 7 to 6.

The middle path won. Golaknath was overruled. Parliament CAN amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights. BUT Parliament cannot damage or destroy the basic structure of the Constitution.

The court did not give a closed list. That was deliberate. Later cases named features like supremacy of the Constitution, rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review, federalism, secularism, free and fair elections, independence of the judiciary, and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

The court also said the Preamble IS a part of the Constitution, overruling Berubari in 1960 on that point.

Think of a building. The owner can paint it, change windows, add a floor, fix pipes. He cannot remove the foundation and pillars, or it stops being that building. Parliament is the owner. The basic structure is the foundation.

One date matters separately. The judgment came on 24 April 1973. That date became a cut-off. In I. R. Coelho in 2007 the court said any law put in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested for breaking the basic structure. So the Ninth Schedule is not a full shield.

For the exam, learn 13 judges, 7 to 6, 1973. Basic structure was born here. It overruled Golaknath. It made the Preamble a part. And if an option says the court gave a full and final list of basic features, that is wrong. The chain is Shankari Prasad 1951, Sajjan Singh 1965, Golaknath 1967, 24th Amendment 1971, Kesavananda 1973.

## 6. The mill that checked the Emergency amendment — Minerva Mills (1980)

Minerva Mills was a textile company in Karnataka. The central government took over its management and then nationalised sick textile units under a 1974 law. The owners challenged the takeover.

But the case became big because of the 42nd Amendment of 1976. That amendment did two things. It changed Article 368 to say no amendment can be questioned in any court on any ground, and there is no limit to Parliament's amending power. In simple words, it tried to kill the basic structure rule by amendment. It also widened Article 31C, so that any law made to carry out ANY Directive Principle would be safe from attack under Articles 14 and 19.

The court asked: can Parliament use a limited amending power to remove the limit itself?

The answer was no. Both provisions were struck down. A limited power cannot be used to make itself unlimited. Judicial review is part of the basic structure, so a clause that stops courts from checking amendments is void. The widened Article 31C was also struck down. The shield went back to the pre-42nd position. Only laws that truly carry out Article 39(b) and 39(c) get protection.

Then came the line that defines the case. The balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is itself part of the basic structure. Neither can fully crush the other. The court said the Constitution stands on the bedrock of that balance. Later writers call Parts III and IV two wheels of a chariot. Break one and the other loses meaning.

The story did not end in 1980. In November 2024 a nine-judge bench in Property Owners Association against the State of Maharashtra spoke again. It said the unamended Article 31C survives. That is, the pre-42nd shield for 39(b) and 39(c) laws is still alive. But it narrowed Article 39(b). Earlier views in Ranganatha Reddy and Sanjeev Coke had suggested almost all private property could be treated as material resources of the community for the State to redistribute. The 2024 majority rejected that wide reading. Not every private building is community material. The court said you must look at the nature of the resource, its scarcity, its effect on public welfare, and what happens if it stays packed in few hands.

For the exam, remember Minerva Mills 1980 cut back the 42nd Amendment and brought 31C back to only 39(b) and 39(c). Any option that says a law for any Directive Principle is safe from Articles 14 and 19 is the dead version. And FR-DPSP balance itself is basic structure.

## 7. Are Bharat Ratna and Padma awards banned titles? — Balaji Raghavan (decided December 1995, quoted as 1996)

Article 18(1) says the State shall not give any title, except a military or academic distinction. The reason is history. Under British rule, titles like Rai Bahadur and Khan Bahadur were given to make a separate superior class. A republic of equals should not make such ranks.

But India also gives National Awards: Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, and Padma Shri. They were formally started by Presidential notifications in January 1954. Are they titles banned by Article 18?

Petitions said yes. They said these awards make ranks among citizens and must be stopped.

The Supreme Court said no. The awards are NOT titles under Article 18(1). They recognise exceptional work. So they are valid. But they must NOT be used as a prefix or suffix to the name. Nobody can write Padma Shri so-and-so as if it were a rank. If a person misuses it that way, they can lose the award by the procedure in Regulation 10 of the notifications. The court also said awards must be given with care, and suggested a high-level committee to advise on selection, because in recent years too many awards had gone to less deserving people.

Think of an Employee of the Year plaque in a company. Giving the plaque is fine. Trouble starts only if the winner signs letters as Employee-of-the-Year Ramesh, as if it were a higher post. Recognition is allowed. Rank is not.

Separately, learn the small Articles correctly, because notes mix them. Article 18(2) says a citizen cannot take a foreign title at all. There is no permission route for a citizen. Article 18(3) is different. It is about a non-citizen who holds an office of profit under the State. That person needs the President's consent to take a foreign title. So if an option says a citizen needs the President's okay for a foreign title, that is wrong. The citizen is fully barred. Consent belongs to 18(3).

If the exam asks whether national awards are titles, answer no, Balaji Raghavan, 1996, but no prefix or suffix use.

## 8. What can a police officer force an accused to give? — Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961) and Selvi (2010)

Article 20(3) says no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. In daily words, the right against self-incrimination.

For a future officer the question is practical. Can I take the accused's fingerprints, handwriting sample, or blood sample?

In State of Bombay against Kathi Kalu Oghad in 1961, an eleven-judge bench drew the line. To be a witness means to give testimony. That means sharing personal knowledge about the case, by speaking or by producing a paper that speaks your mind. It does NOT mean only giving body material for matching.

So these are outside Article 20(3) and can lawfully be taken: fingerprints, palm prints, footprints, specimen handwriting and signature, blood, hair, semen and DNA samples, photos, and identification parades.

These ARE protected: a confession pulled out by force, or forcing the accused to produce a paper that itself tells his personal knowledge.

Later, in Selvi against the State of Karnataka in 2010, the court dealt with narco-analysis, polygraph lie-detector, and brain-mapping BEAP tests. It said doing these tests WITHOUT consent breaks Article 20(3) and Article 21, because they pull out what is inside the person's mind. The results of a forced test cannot be used as evidence. If a person freely agrees, with a lawyer, with consent recorded before a magistrate, and with safeguards, then the test may be done, but even then the result by itself is not a confession. Only new material truly found later with its help can be used under Section 27 of the Evidence Act.

Think of a teacher who suspects copying. Asking the boy to write one line so handwriting can be matched is allowed. The writing already exists in his hand. Forcing him to say yes, I copied is not allowed. That is pulling testimony from his mind. The whole rule in one image is: the body may be measured, the mind may not be forced.

The commonest false statement in all of Part III is: forcing an accused to give specimen handwriting breaks Article 20(3). It does not. Also remember Article 20(3) needs two things together: the person is accused of an offence, and there is compulsion. Selvi also made clear the protection reaches the investigation stage, not only the courtroom.

## 9. When courts were shut during Emergency — ADM Jabalpur (1976)

In June 1975 a National Emergency was declared on the ground of internal disturbance. Many political opponents were locked up without trial under MISA, the Maintenance of Internal Security Act. The government also passed an order under Article 359 stopping people from moving courts to enforce Articles 14, 21, and 22.

Detained people filed habeas corpus petitions. That remedy asks the court to call for the body and ask why the person is held. Several High Courts sided with the detenus. They said even in Emergency a court can check whether a detention is lawful. The government appealed to the Supreme Court.

The question was stark. In an Emergency, can a detained person go to court at all against an illegal detention? In open court the Attorney General accepted that on the government's logic, even a wrongful killing by the State would have no remedy while the Emergency lasted.

A five-judge bench ruled 4 to 1 for the government. The majority said once the Article 359 order was in force, nobody had standing to file a writ under Article 21. No habeas corpus in any circumstance. Article 21 was treated as the only home of life and liberty, so closing it left nothing behind.

Justice H. R. Khanna stood alone. He said life and liberty do not exist only because the Constitution gives them. The State has no power to take life or liberty without lawful authority, even in Emergency. His dissent cost him the Chief Justice post. He was superseded and he resigned.

History judged the majority harshly. Parliament answered through the 44th Amendment in 1978. Articles 20 and 21 can NEVER be suspended, in any Emergency. This directly undoes ADM Jabalpur's effect. Internal disturbance was removed and replaced by armed rebellion as a ground for National Emergency, raising the bar. And the automatic shutting of Article 19 under Article 358 was limited to Emergency on war or external aggression.

Finally, in Puttaswamy in 2017, a nine-judge bench expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur and called the majority view seriously flawed.

Think of a building where the fire alarm is switched off for a drill. The management says since the alarm is off, nobody can report a fire, and if people burn there is no remedy. Common sense says the right not to burn does not depend on the alarm. Khanna's dissent was that common sense, and the 44th Amendment wrote it into the Constitution.

For the exam, chain three points. ADM Jabalpur 1976, 4 to 1, Khanna dissented. 44th Amendment 1978 made 20 and 21 non-suspendable. Puttaswamy 2017 overruled it. If an option says Article 21 can be suspended today, answer no.

## 10. The case that made privacy a Fundamental Right — Puttaswamy (2017, nine judges)

The Aadhaar scheme collected body data, fingerprints and eye scans, from residents. People challenged it as an attack on privacy.

The government's reply was technical. It said India has no Fundamental Right to privacy at all. It pointed to two old cases, M. P. Sharma in 1954 by eight judges and Kharak Singh in 1962 by six judges, which had said privacy is not a guaranteed right.

Because those benches were so large, a small bench could not overrule them. So a nine-judge bench was formed only to settle this one question. On 24 August 2017 it gave a unanimous verdict.

It said privacy IS a Fundamental Right. It lives as an intrinsic part of life and personal liberty under Article 21, and of the freedoms in Part III generally. M. P. Sharma and Kharak Singh were overruled to the extent they said otherwise.

Privacy is not absolute. Any limit must pass three tests. First legality, there must be a law. Second a legitimate State aim. Third proportionality, the means must fit the goal and go no further than needed.

The court also expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur.

For policing this is not abstract. After this case, every act of watching, phone tapping, opening a phone or laptop, taking body data, or keeping CCTV footage needs a legal base and must be proportionate. This is also the background for how the BSA 2023 treats electronic records.

Think of it this way. Privacy was always a room inside the house of Article 21. It just had no nameplate. Puttaswamy did not build a new room. It confirmed the room was always there and put a name on it. And it added: this door can be opened only with a valid key, for a good reason, and only as wide as needed.

If the exam asks in which case and year privacy became a Fundamental Right, write Puttaswamy, 2017. If an option says privacy is absolute, that is the trap. It is not.

## 11. The case that tells you how to make an arrest — D. K. Basu (decided December 1996, quoted as 1997)

D. K. Basu headed a legal aid group. He wrote a letter to the Chief Justice of India about news of deaths in police custody and torture in lock-ups. The Supreme Court treated that letter itself as a writ petition. Lawyers call this epistolary jurisdiction.

The court laid down binding rules for every arrest. Learn their substance, because examiners ask them as stories.

The police who arrest and question must wear clear name tags and their details must be written in a register. At the time of arrest the officer must make a memo of arrest, with date and time, signed by at least one witness who is a family member or a respectable local person, and also signed by the arrested person. The arrested person has the right to have one friend, relative, or well-wisher told as soon as possible where he is held. If that person lives outside the district, the police must send the time, place, and venue of custody through the Legal Aid Organisation and the local police station within 8 to 12 hours. The arrested person must be told about this right at once. An entry must be made in the diary at the detention place, naming who was informed and which officers hold custody. If the arrested person asks, he must be examined for injuries at arrest time, and the Inspection Memo must be signed by both sides, with a copy to him. During custody he must get a medical check every 48 hours by a doctor from an approved panel. Copies of all papers must go to the Magistrate for record. He may meet his lawyer during questioning, though not through every minute of it. And every district and State must have a police control room that gets arrest details within 12 hours and puts them on a notice board.

These rules were later written into law, first in the Criminal Procedure Code and now carried into the BNSS, which also asks designated officers to keep and display arrest information.

Think of a hospital admitting a patient. Form, time stamp, witness sign, relative informed, health check on arrival, and a record the family can see. That does not stop treatment. It only makes every step checkable. D. K. Basu does the same for custody, so illegal detention has nowhere to hide.

If the exam asks which case gave arrest guidelines, write D. K. Basu, 1997. In a story question where there is no memo and no relative informed, the violation is this case, rooted in Articles 21 and 22.

## 12. The case that stopped Delhi from removing States at will — Bommai (1994, nine judges)

Article 356 lets the President take over a State when its government cannot run as per the Constitution. People call it President's Rule. Between 1950 and 1994 it was used more than a hundred times, often for politics: to remove an opposition government, or on a Governor's bare note that the Chief Minister lost majority. Courts usually called it a political question beyond review.

S. R. Bommai was Chief Minister of Karnataka. In 1989 the Governor wrote that he lost majority, based on letters from legislators withdrawing support. Bommai said let me prove majority on the floor of the House. He even offered to advance the session date. The Governor refused, sent a report, and the President dismissed the government and dissolved the Assembly. Similar dismissals in other States, including those after the Babri Masjid demolition in 1992, were heard together by nine judges. Judgment came on 11 March 1994.

The court laid down rules that still bind.

A proclamation under Article 356 can be checked by courts. It is not immune as politics. Majority must be tested by voting in the Assembly. The Assembly is the only forum. The Governor's personal satisfaction or counting letters in his office is not enough. Courts can look at the material and strike down a mala fide proclamation based on irrelevant or extraneous grounds. The court looks not at whether the President was right, but whether there was relevant material at all. If the proclamation is bad, courts can bring back the dissolved Assembly and the dismissed ministry. And secularism is part of the basic structure, so a State government acting against secular principles can be a valid ground for Article 356.

The court added a practical shield. The Assembly should not be dissolved before Parliament approves the proclamation. Otherwise by the time the court finds fault, the Assembly is already gone and the remedy is empty.

Think of a club where the patron removes the president on a note saying he lost support. The president says call a meeting and count hands. The patron refuses. Bommai says support is proved by hands raised in the room, not by letters in the patron's office, and a wrong removal can be undone.

The Sarkaria Commission in 1988 and the Punchhi Commission in 2010 had already advised that 356 be a last resort. Bommai turned that advice into enforceable law. Use of Article 356 fell sharply after 1994.

If the exam gives a story where a Governor advises 356 without a floor test, the answer is it is challengeable. If an option says 356 is a political question beyond review, that was true before 1994, not now. That time-shift is a favourite trap.

## 13. The professor who counted 256 ordinances — D. C. Wadhwa (decided December 1986, quoted as 1987)

When the legislature is not sitting and something urgent comes, the President under Article 123 or a Governor under Article 213 can issue an ordinance. It works like a law. But it is meant to be temporary. It must be placed before the legislature when it meets again, and it stops working six weeks after reassembly unless the legislature passes it as an Act.

Dr. D. C. Wadhwa taught economics. He studied Bihar ordinances. Between 1967 and 1981 Bihar had issued 256 ordinances and kept them alive by issuing them again and again, some for up to fourteen years. As the six-week period came near, the government let the ordinance lapse and at once issued the same one again, without ever placing it for proper passing. On some days dozens were reissued together.

He challenged the practice and three live ordinances.

The court said re-promulgation without placing the ordinance before the legislature is a fraud on the Constitution and a subversion of democracy. Ordinance power is an emergency power for urgent need, not a parallel way to rule because the executive finds the legislature inconvenient. The court accepted a rare exception. If the legislature truly had too much work in one session and could not take up the Bill, a reissue might be justified. But a system like Bihar's is unconstitutional.

A later seven-judge bench in Krishna Kumar Singh in 2017 went further. Placing the ordinance before the legislature is a mandatory duty, not a formality. Not placing it is itself an abuse. Re-promulgation to bypass the house is impermissible and an effort to overreach the legislature. Satisfaction of the President or Governor on need for immediate action can also be checked by courts in cases of fraud or abuse, though courts will not weigh sufficiency of material lightly.

Think of a school where the principal can issue a temporary notice when the committee is not meeting, valid till its next sitting. If the principal lets each notice lapse and instantly reissues the same notice for fourteen years, never putting it to the committee, he has not used an emergency power. He has replaced the committee with himself.

For the exam, remember Wadhwa 1987 means re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution, with the memorable figure 256 ordinances. An ordinance lasts six weeks from reassembly, not till the next session. That wording difference is the trap.

## 14. Can courts check a Governor's pardon? — Epuru Sudhakar (2006, an Andhra Pradesh case)

Pardon power sounds like pure mercy. Article 72 gives the President power to pardon, commute, remit, respite, or reprieve. It covers court martial cases and death sentences. Article 161 gives a Governor similar power for offences against State laws. But it cannot cover a court martial, and it cannot pardon a death sentence, though State law may allow suspension, remission, or commutation of death in some settings. For long it was argued courts cannot touch these orders at all.

In Andhra Pradesh a convict in a political killing case got the rest of his sentence remitted by the Governor. The order was challenged. The charge was that it rested on political and irrelevant grounds, including calling the convict a good political worker, and on factually wrong material.

The question was: can a court look at a clemency order at all?

The Supreme Court said yes, though on limited grounds. An order under Article 72 or 161 can be set aside if it was passed without application of mind, or mala fide in bad faith, or on wholly irrelevant or extraneous grounds, or by hiding or ignoring material facts. Power is wide but not absolute. Rule of law is the base of review. The Governor's order in this case was set aside, with liberty to reconsider properly. The court also confirmed the power is used on the advice of the Council of Ministers, not by the Governor's personal choice alone.

Think of a principal who can waive a student's punishment. The power is real and wide. But if she waives it because the boy's relative is her friend, or because someone gave her a false report, the waiver can be cancelled. Not because she had no power, but because she used it for the wrong reason or on wrong facts.

For the exam, learn three lines. Clemency is reviewable on limited grounds. Article 72 can pardon death and covers court martial. Article 161 can do neither. And it runs on ministerial advice. Because this is an Andhra Pradesh case, expect it in State papers. If an option says pardon power is fully beyond courts, that is wrong after Epuru Sudhakar, 2006.

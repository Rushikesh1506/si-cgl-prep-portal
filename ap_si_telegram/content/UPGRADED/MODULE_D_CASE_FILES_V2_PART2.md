# MODULE D — Case Files Part 2, Rewritten for a First-Time Learner (Cases 15-26 + Topics 27-29)

Dear friend, this file tells you the human story behind each case. For every case remember four things: who was fighting, what actually happened, what the judges said in simple words, and why a Sub-Inspector must care. Exam traps are at the end of each story.

## Case 15 — Berubari: Can India give away a piece of land? (1960)

Imagine a small village called Berubari in West Bengal. After Partition, India and Pakistan (then East Pakistan, now Bangladesh) signed an agreement — the Nehru-Noon Agreement of 1958. Under it, half of Berubari was to go to Pakistan.

People asked: can the government just give away Indian land like that? The President asked the Supreme Court for advice under Article 143. This is called advisory opinion, not a fight between two parties.

One argument was: the Preamble says "We, the People" and declares India a Sovereign Democratic Republic, so our territory is sacred and cannot be given away.

To answer, the Court had to decide what the Preamble itself is.

What the Court said in 1960:
- The Preamble is only a key to understand the minds of the makers. It helps when a rule is unclear, but it is NOT a part of the Constitution. It gives no power and puts no limit.
- Giving away Indian territory needs a constitutional amendment under Article 368, not an ordinary law under Article 3. Parliament then passed the 9th Amendment in 1960 to do it.

What changed later: In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Court changed its mind on the first point. It said the Preamble IS a part of the Constitution, and it can be amended, but its basic character cannot be destroyed. So far the Preamble was amended only once, by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, which added three words: Socialist, Secular and Integrity.

The date 26 November 1949 written in the Preamble is the date of adoption. India started following the Constitution on 26 January 1950. That second date is NOT in the Preamble.

Why an SI cares: when you see a question on cession of territory, remember amendment is needed. And when you see Preamble questions, remember 1960 said No, 1973 said Yes.

Exam trap: "Preamble was amended many times" — false, only once. "The date in the Preamble is the date of commencement" — false, it is adoption.

## Case 16 — Zee Telefilms: Is BCCI the Government? (2005)

Zee Telefilms is a TV company. BCCI is the Board that runs cricket in India. In 2004 BCCI invited bids for TV rights, accepted Zee's bid of about 260 million US dollars, took Rs 92.5 crores deposit, and then cancelled the deal. Zee went to the Supreme Court under Article 32 and said BCCI acted arbitrarily and broke Article 14.

But Article 32 only works if the other side is "State" under Article 12. Article 12 says State means Government of India, Parliament, State governments and legislatures, and "all local or other authorities". Courts made a test — the instrumentality test from R.D. Shetty and Ajay Hasia cases: does government give money, control management, give monopoly, do the body do government-like work?

BCCI is a private society registered under the Tamil Nadu Societies Registration Act. Government gives it no money and does not control it. But BCCI selects India's team and controls all cricket in India. It looks public.

What the Court said, and you must remember both halves:
- BCCI is NOT State under Article 12. So a case under Article 32 does not lie against it. The petition was not maintainable on that ground.
- BUT BCCI does public functions — selecting the national team, controlling players and the game. So a High Court CAN hear a case against it under Article 226, because Article 226 runs against "any person or authority", not only State.

Simple meaning: Article 12 controls Article 32. Article 226 does not need Article 12. Article 226 is wider, though Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and so a stronger guarantee.

Why an SI cares: when a private body does a public job — a private school, a sports body, a contractor running a public utility — a citizen may still get a writ in High Court even if Supreme Court Article 32 is closed.

Exam trap: Statement "BCCI is not amenable even to High Court writs" is false. Statement "Zee Telefilms held BCCI is not State under Article 12" is true.

## Case 17 — Who appoints judges? Three Judges Cases and NJAC (1981, 1993, 1998, 2015)

Article 124 says the President appoints Supreme Court judges "after consultation" with judges, and the Chief Justice must always be consulted. The whole fight is over one word: consultation. Does it mean just ask, or does it mean agree?

First Judges Case — S.P. Gupta, decided 30 December 1981 (reported as AIR 1982 SC 149). The Court said consultation does NOT mean concurrence. The executive had primacy. Government could decide after asking.

Second Judges Case — 1993, nine judges. The Court reversed it. Consultation means concurrence. The Chief Justice's view, formed together with senior judges, has primacy. This created the collegium system.

Third Judges Case — 1998, a Presidential Reference under Article 143. The Court explained how collegium works: the CJI must consult four senior-most judges for Supreme Court appointments (two for High Court appointments). A recommendation without that consultation is not binding.

Then Parliament tried to change the system. By the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act in 2014, it made the National Judicial Appointments Commission: the CJI as chair, two senior-most Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons chosen by a committee of the Prime Minister, the CJI and the Leader of Opposition. Any two members could veto a name.

In October 2015 a five-judge bench struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act by 4 to 1. Reason: it broke the independence of the judiciary, which is part of the basic structure. The Law Minister's presence and the two-member veto let the executive interfere. The collegium came back.

Note: S.P. Gupta (1981) is famous for two different reasons — First Judges Case on appointments, and also for starting PIL by relaxing locus standi.

Why an SI cares: you will see questions like "Did NJAC replace the collegium?" Answer is No, it was struck down before it could work.

Exam trap: collegium = CJI plus four senior-most judges for Supreme Court matters. NJAC had a veto rule. Independence of judiciary is basic structure.

## Case 18 — How a letter became a case: birth of PIL (1979, 1981, 1984)

Normally only the injured person can go to court. This is called locus standi. But undertrial prisoners, bonded labourers and poor children cannot hire lawyers or reach Delhi. So their rights had no remedy.

Three steps built the answer:

1. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, 1979 — the first PIL. A lawyer read a newspaper report about Bihar jails. Thousands of undertrial prisoners were inside for longer than the maximum punishment for their alleged offence, some for years for small offences. The Court, led by Justice P.N. Bhagwati, through orders in 1979-1980, said speedy trial is part of right to life under Article 21. Free legal aid to a poor accused is part of fair procedure under Article 21 read with Article 39A (a Directive Principle). It ordered release of thousands. This is treated as India's first PIL.

2. S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981. The Court said any member of the public acting honestly can move the court for people who cannot come themselves because of poverty, disability or social disadvantage. It also accepted epistolary jurisdiction — a letter can be treated as a writ petition.

3. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, decided 16 December 1983 (reported 1984 AIR 802). An organisation wrote about bonded labourers in stone quarries in Haryana. The letter was treated as a petition under Article 32. The Court sent commissioners to the ground, held bonded labour violates Article 21 and Article 23, and ordered identification, release and rehabilitation.

What PIL is not: it is not for private fights dressed as public causes. Courts now fine people for frivolous PILs, sometimes called publicity interest litigation.

The big legal trick to remember: a Directive Principle like Article 39A (free legal aid) becomes enforceable when it is read into a Fundamental Right like Article 21.

Why an SI cares: you will meet undertrial prisoners and bonded labourers. Delay itself is a rights violation. Legal aid is not charity, it is a constitutional duty.

Exam trap: Assertion says legal aid is enforceable, Reason cites Article 39A alone. Both statements true, but Reason alone does not explain enforceability — you need Article 21. So answer is both true, R does not explain A.

## Case 19 — The Asiad workers: less than minimum wage is forced labour (1982)

Delhi was building stadiums, flyovers and hotels for the 1982 Asian Games. Migrant workers came through contractors called jamadars. The People's Union for Democratic Rights (PUDR) investigated and found: wages below the minimum (Rs 9.25 per day at that time, women got about Rs 7), women paid less, children below 14 on sites, labour laws ignored. PUDR wrote a letter to Justice Bhagwati. It became a writ petition. Judgment came on 18 September 1982, reported as (1982) 3 SCC 235, AIR 1982 SC 1473.

Contractors said: we are paying something, workers came on their own, so this is at most a Minimum Wages Act issue, not forced labour under Article 23. Article 23 bans traffic in human beings, begar and other forced labour. Begar once meant work with no pay.

What the Court said:
- Force does not mean only physical force. Hunger, poverty and destitution are also force.
- If a person works for less than minimum wage, he does so because the alternative is starvation. That economic compulsion is force.
- So payment below minimum wage IS forced labour and violates Article 23.
- Article 23 works against private persons also, not only government. So contractors are directly bound. It protects all persons, not only citizens.
- Children on construction sites also violated Article 24.

Why an SI cares, in one line: look for wages and debt, not chains. A brick kiln where workers get less than minimum wage against an advance can attract Article 23 and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976, even without locks or guards. Other related laws: Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956, and now BNS provisions on trafficking. Only Parliament can make punishment for Part III offences — that is Article 35, which is why these are central laws.

Exam trap: "Payment of less than minimum wage amounts to forced labour" — true, after PUDR 1982.

## Case 20 — Sivakasi children: what a court does beyond declaring rights (1996)

Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu makes fireworks and matches. After a fire, attention fell on children working in hazardous factories. M.C. Mehta filed a PIL under Article 32. Judgment came on 10 December 1996, reported as (1996) 6 SCC 756, AIR 1997 SC 699.

Article 24 says no child below 14 shall work in a factory, mine or other hazardous employment. Note: it does NOT ban all work, only factory, mine or hazardous work.

The Court did more than declare. It made a scheme:
- Employer of a child in hazardous work must pay Rs 20,000 per child into a Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund (district-wise or area-wise, kept in a high-yield account, income also part of fund).
- The government must give a job to an adult member of that family if possible; if it cannot, it must contribute Rs 5,000 per child to the fund. [Note on figures: textbooks uniformly say 20,000 employer plus 5,000 government; one government compilation prints 25,000 in one place — treat 20,000/5,000 as exam answer.]
- The child must be withdrawn and put into education, with fund income supporting the family.
- Survey of child labour to be completed, inspectors under the Child Labour Act to enforce.

Reasoning linked Article 24 with Article 21A / Article 45 (education) and Article 39(e)-(f) (children must not be abused, must grow in freedom and dignity). Just pulling a child out without supporting the family pushes the family deeper into trouble.

The statute went further than the Constitution. Remember this table:

- Article 24: only under-14 in factory, mine or hazardous work.
- Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 (in force from 1 September 2016): bans ALL employment of children below 14, except help in family enterprise outside school hours and work in entertainment industry; creates a new group called adolescents (14 to 18) who cannot do hazardous work; makes violation cognizable with jail up to two years and fine up to Rs 50,000.

So "all employment below 14 is banned" is false under Article 24 but true under the 2016 Act. Always check which law the question asks about.

Articles 23 and 24 bind private employers directly. They are called horizontal rights.

Exam trap: pair contrasting constitutional ban vs statutory ban. Read carefully.

## Case 21 — Who is a minority? T.M.A. Pai (2002)

Article 30(1) says all minorities, whether based on religion or language, can establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

Two doubts: minority compared to what — whole India or the State? Sikhs are a minority in India but a majority in Punjab. And how far does "administer" go — can government fix fees, admissions, teacher qualifications?

An eleven-judge bench decided on 31 October 2002, reported as (2002) 8 SCC 481.

What it said:
- The unit to decide minority status is the STATE, not the whole country, because States were reorganised on language lines.
- Article 30 needs BOTH limbs: the minority must have established AND must administer the institution. Only administering is not enough.
- The right is not absolute. Government can make reasonable rules for excellence — qualified teachers, standards, service conditions, honest accounts, no profiteering, no capitation fee.
- But rules must not destroy the minority character or make the right an illusion.
- The judgment also said unaided private institutions have autonomy in admissions and fees, subject to merit, transparency and no profiteering.

Link to Article 15(5): the 93rd Amendment in 2005 added Article 15(5) allowing reservation in private unaided institutions, but it expressly leaves out minority institutions under Article 30. That exception protects what T.M.A. Pai mapped.

Simple picture: a community builds a school to save its language. Government can say teachers must be qualified and building must be safe. It cannot take over admissions and staff fully so that it is no longer the community's school.

Exam trap: "Unit for linguistic minority?" Answer: State. "Which institutions are outside Article 15(5)?" Answer: minority institutions under Article 30.

## Case 22 — Sabarimala: why this case is NOT settled — it is PENDING (2018 onwards)

Sabarimala temple in Kerala is for Lord Ayyappa. Custom barred women aged 10 to 50 (menstruating age) from entering.

Challenge said it breaks Articles 14, 15, 17 and 25. Temple side said it is an essential religious practice protected by Article 25, and Ayyappa devotees are a religious denomination under Article 26 with right to manage. They also relied on Article 25(2)(b), which lets the State throw open Hindu public temples to all classes — but they said this custom is essential, so it survives.

On 28 September 2018, a five-judge bench held by 4 to 1 that the exclusion is unconstitutional. Devotees are not a separate denomination, the bar is not an essential religious practice, it denies women's right to worship and equality. Rule 3(b) of the Kerala Hindu Places of Public Worship Rules, 1965, which allowed exclusion by custom, was struck down. Justice Indu Malhotra dissented, saying courts should not lightly interfere in deep faith and the essential-practices test should not be used to strike down a community's own belief.

Most notes stop here and say settled. That is wrong.

Review petitions were filed — more than 50. On 14 November 2019, a five-judge bench by 3 to 2 kept reviews pending and referred larger questions to a bigger bench: how Articles 25 and 26 fit together, what is the scope of essential practices, can courts test faith at all. On 13 January 2020 a nine-judge bench led by then CJI S.A. Bobde started, and on 10 February 2020 it held the reference is maintainable. Then Covid stopped hearings.

Update verified in 2026: after almost six years, a new nine-judge bench headed by Chief Justice Surya Kant (with Justices B.V. Nagarathna, M.M. Sundresh, Ahsanuddin Amanullah, Aravind Kumar, Augustine George Masih, Prasanna Varale, R. Mahadevan and Joymalya Bagchi) was notified on 4 April 2026. As per causelist it was to hear from 7 April 2026 onwards, with petitioners, opponents and rejoinder in April. Reports in April 2026 say hearings were on. As of the date of this file, no final nine-judge verdict has been reported as settled law. So the correct position for exam is: 2018 judgment exists and is technically on paper, but the larger reference is unresolved and PENDING.

Also remember Article 25(2)(b) explanation: "Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.

Why an SI cares: Sabarimala-type duties involve faith, crowds and gender. Do not quote 2018 as final in answers or on duty. Say "2018 (4:1) plus reference pending before nine judges".

Exam trap: "Sabarimala 2018 is settled law" — false. "Reference to nine judges is pending" — true.

## Case 23 — Maratha quota and why two amendments came close together (2021, 102nd and 105th)

The 102nd Amendment in 2018 did two things: gave constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes by adding Article 338B, and added Article 342A saying the President, in consultation with Governor, notifies socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs), and Parliament can change that list.

Maharashtra made a law giving Marathas reservation, taking total reservation in the State beyond 50% (about 12% in jobs and 13% in education, total going to 64-65%).

Challenge reached a five-judge bench: Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil case, judgment on 5 May 2021. It said:
- Marathas were not shown as SEBC needing this quota, and there were no extraordinary circumstances to breach the 50% ceiling from Indra Sawhney (1992). So the Maratha quota was struck down as unconstitutional.
- By majority of 3 to 2 (Justices Rao, Gupta and Bhat for majority; Justices Bhushan and Nazeer dissenting on this point), it read Article 342A to mean only the President and Parliament could identify SEBCs — States had lost that power.

That created a crisis. Every State has its own backward-classes list for State jobs and colleges. If only Delhi could make lists, State lists would freeze. All parties objected.

Answer: Parliament passed the 105th Amendment in 2021 (in force 19 August 2021). It clarified that States and Union Territories keep power to prepare and maintain their own SEBC lists for State purposes. The Central list under Article 342A is for Central purposes.

Simple rule to remember: when two amendments on the same Article come within a few years, usually a judgment sits between them. Here 102nd (2018) — judgment (May 2021) — 105th (2021).

Exam trap: Assertion "Parliament passed 105th" because Reason "102nd was read as taking away States' power" — both true, and Reason correctly explains Assertion.

## Case 24 — EWS quota: the 50% rule changed (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022)

The 103rd Amendment in 2019 added Articles 15(6) and 16(6). It allowed up to 10% reservation in education and government jobs for Economically Weaker Sections among citizens NOT already covered by SC, ST and OBC quotas.

It was new in three ways: only economic criteria, SC/ST/OBC excluded from it, and total reservation would cross the 50% ceiling from Indra Sawhney.

On 7 November 2022 a five-judge bench in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India upheld it by 3 to 2. Majority: Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, Bela Trivedi and J.B. Pardiwala. Dissent: CJI U.U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat. Four separate judgments were pronounced.

Majority said:
- Reservation only on economic criteria does not break basic structure. Affirmative action can help economic disadvantage also.
- Excluding SC/ST/OBC is not discrimination, because they already have their own quotas — this avoids double benefit.
- Crucially, the 50% ceiling is NOT part of basic structure. It was a rule of prudence for Article 16(4) backward-class quotas, not an unbreakable limit. EWS 10% sits OUTSIDE that ceiling.
- The amendment can also apply to private unaided institutions under Article 15(6) without breaking basic structure.

Dissent said excluding the poor among SC/ST/OBC from an economic quota is discriminatory, and breaching 50% will become routine and lead to compartmentalisation.

What you must unlearn: old books said "reservation can never cross 50%". After 2022 that is wrong. Correct line: the 50% ceiling governs Article 16(4) backward-class reservation; it does not bind EWS, and it is not basic structure.

Exam trap: Assertion "any total above 50% is automatically unconstitutional" is false now; Reason "Indra Sawhney fixed a ceiling" is true. So A false, R true.

## Case 25 — Governor and Bills: a fast-moving, EVOLVING story (2023, April 2025, November 2025) — read as flux, not final

Article 200 is about what a Governor does when a State Assembly passes a Bill. Textually it gives choices: give assent, withhold assent, return for reconsideration (except Money Bills — and if House passes again, Governor "shall not withhold assent"), or reserve for President (then Article 201 applies). Article 111 for the President's assent to Central Bills has three options; Article 200 has this extra option of reservation for President because there is no higher authority for the President to send to. Article 200 says "as soon as possible" but gives NO fixed days. That silence caused the fight. A Governor sitting silent is sometimes called pocket veto.

Three stages you must know in order, and you must present them as unsettled flux:

Stage 1 — Punjab, November 2023. Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, with Justices Pardiwala and Manoj Misra, on Punjab government's plea against Governor Banwarilal Purohit over four Bills. It said a Governor cannot sit indefinitely and kill law-making. Withholding without sending back to House violates Article 200. Real power lies with elected representatives. If Governor withholds, he must send back "as soon as possible" with a message.

Stage 2 — Tamil Nadu, 8 April 2025 (full reasons released 11 April 2025). Bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan, on Tamil Nadu government's plea against Governor R.N. Ravi over 12 Bills (mostly on university Vice-Chancellors) sent between January 2020 and April 2023. Governor withheld 10 without message, House re-passed them on 18 November 2023, Governor then reserved them for President on 28 November 2023. Court held reservation illegal, invoked Article 142 to declare the 10 Bills "deemed to have been assented to" on the date of re-presentation, and fixed timelines: broadly one to three months for Governor (e.g. one month to withhold on Cabinet advice, three months if against advice, one month to assent after re-passage) and timelines for President under Article 201. It said no pocket veto or absolute veto exists. Tamil Nadu notified the 10 as law on 12 April 2025.

Stage 3 — Presidential Reference opinion, 20 November 2025. President Droupadi Murmu referred 14 questions under Article 143 on 13 May 2025 (Special Reference No. 1 of 2025). A five-judge Constitution Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai (unanimous opinion) advised:
- Courts CANNOT impose one-size-fits-all timelines on Governor or President under Articles 200 and 201. Those Articles preserve elasticity.
- "Deemed assent" is alien to the Constitution. A court cannot use Article 142 to substitute itself for Governor or President and forge assent.
- However, prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction is NOT beyond scrutiny. Court can issue limited mandamus asking the authority to decide within reasonable time, without judging merits.
- On options: opinion describes three constitutional options — assent, withhold-cum-return with reasons, or reserve for President (four only in the sense that return is not available for Money Bills). Withholding simpliciter without returning frustrates dialogue and federalism.
- Governor has discretion to choose among options and is not strictly bound by Cabinet advice on that choice (otherwise reservation would become meaningless), but cannot sit forever. Governor enjoys personal immunity under Article 361, but the office's inaction can still be directed to decide.
- President's function under Article 201 is not justiciable on merits, no timelines, and President need not seek Supreme Court advice under Article 143 every time.

So April 2025 and November 2025 point in different directions on timelines and deemed assent. The only point both agree on: Governor cannot sit indefinitely. Because this is EVOLVING, any material teaching "deemed assent" as current settled law is out of date, and any material teaching "Governor can sit forever" is also wrong. For exam, learn the chronology and the words "limited mandamus within reasonable time" as the November 2025 position.

Why an SI cares: Bills on universities, police, local bodies affect your daily work. Knowing who must decide, and that courts can prod but not sign, helps you read government orders correctly.

Exam trap: chronology order Punjab 2023 → Tamil Nadu April 2025 → Reference November 2025. And "at present" questions must be answered with caution: say position is in flux, November 2025 advisory says no judicial timelines, no deemed assent, but inaction reviewable.

## Case 26 — I.R. Coelho: closing the Ninth Schedule escape route (2007)

The First Amendment in 1951 made Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule. Any law put in that Schedule could not be challenged for violating Fundamental Rights. Aim was to protect land reforms. Over time governments put all kinds of laws inside — by 2007 there were 284 entries, about 218 added after 1973, many with no link to land reform. It became a safe box.

Problem: if anything inside is fully safe, Parliament could put any law there and escape basic structure review.

A nine-judge bench decided unanimously on 11 January 2007 (citation (2007) 2 SCC 1), Chief Justice Y.K. Sabharwal delivering. Reference had come from a five-judge order in 1999 building on Waman Rao (1981).

What it said:
- Laws put in the Ninth Schedule AFTER 24 April 1973 — the date of Kesavananda Bharati judgment — ARE open to challenge if they damage the basic structure.
- Court will apply effect test: does the law in substance abrogate rights forming part of basic structure, using rights test and essence-of-right test (Articles 14, 19, 21 and principles behind them).
- Laws inserted BEFORE that date stay protected.
- Validity of Article 31B itself was not struck down; only its misuse after the basic-structure date is checked. Power to amend Ninth Schedule comes from Article 368 and must respect its limits.

Simple picture: a bank gave you a sealed box for land papers. You started putting everything inside. Bank now says: boxes sealed before 24 April 1973 stay sealed; anything put after can be opened and checked.

Exam trap: "A Ninth Schedule law is immune from review" — false after 2007. "Article 31B protects Ninth Schedule laws from Part III challenge" — true as text. So A false, R true. Cut-off date 24 April 1973 is the heart.

## Topic 27 — PESA 1996: why tribal areas needed a separate panchayat law

The 73rd Amendment in 1992 made panchayats constitutional (Part IX). But Article 243M says Part IX does NOT apply to Scheduled Areas under Fifth Schedule and tribal areas under Sixth Schedule, to Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram, hill areas of Manipur with district councils, and Darjeeling Gorkha areas. Why? Because tribal areas had their own traditional self-rule. Imposing a uniform panchayat could destroy it. Exclusion was protective.

But then tribal areas had NO constitutional local-government framework. So Article 243M(4)(b) allowed Parliament to extend Part IX there with exceptions and changes.

Parliament did that through the Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — PESA, passed 24 December 1996, based on the Bhuria Committee (Dileep Singh Bhuria, appointed June 1994, report January 1995).

Big shift: the Gram Sabha — all registered voters of the village — becomes the boss, not the elected Gram Panchayat. Powers you must remember:
- Approves development plans and picks beneficiaries of poverty schemes; panchayat must get utilisation certificate from Gram Sabha.
- Must be consulted before land acquisition in Scheduled Areas and before resettlement/rehabilitation.
- Can stop transfer of tribal land and get back land taken wrongly.
- Owns minor forest produce.
- Can regulate or stop sale and use of intoxicants.
- Controls money-lending to Scheduled Tribes.
- Manages minor water bodies; its recommendation is needed for prospecting licences and mining leases for minor minerals; manages village markets.

Why this matters in Andhra Pradesh: AP has Scheduled Areas in Alluri Sitharama Raju, Parvathipuram Manyam, Eluru, Palnadu, Srikakulam, Anakapalli and Kakinada districts (tribal pockets). If a contractor with a State permit digs minor minerals and villagers stop work, do not rush to file obstruction case. First ask: is there a Gram Sabha resolution? Villagers may be using a statutory right. Acting without that check can violate the very law made for that area.

Exam trap in one line: Excluded by Article 243M, restored by PESA 1996. Gram Sabha, not Panchayat, is primary. Land, minor forest produce, minor minerals, intoxicants, money-lending — these five words answer most questions.

## Topic 28 — Andhra chain: Sri Bagh → Gentlemen's Agreement → Six Points → Article 371D

One idea explains all: when a smaller, less-developed region merges with a bigger one, it fears losing jobs, college seats, capital and courts. Each agreement below is a promise to calm that fear before merger.

Step 1 — Sri Bagh Pact, 16 November 1937 (ratified December 1937). Who: leaders of coastal Andhra (Circars) and Rayalaseema. Why: Rayalaseema was dry and less developed, feared coastal deltas would take everything in a combined Andhra province. What: share of institutions — capital, High Court and university not all in one region; Rayalaseema gets a share and irrigation priority; later Andhra State in 1953 put capital at Kurnool under this spirit. Drafted by Pattabhi Sitaramayya. Met at Sri Bagh, the house of Kashinathuni Nageswara Rao, founder of Andhra Patrika and Amrutanjan, in Madras. [Correction: some notes wrongly name Desiraju Hanumantha Rao's house — verified house is Kashinathuni Nageswara Rao's Sri Bagh.]

Step 2 — Gentlemen's Agreement, 20 February 1956. Who: leaders of Andhra State and Telangana region of Hyderabad State. Why: Telangana was under the Nizam, less developed in education and administration, feared coastal Andhra candidates would take jobs and seats. Old Hyderabad Mulki Rules reserved jobs for locals, Telangana wanted protection to continue. What: safeguards in jobs, education, spending, a Regional Council for Telangana, convention that if CM is from one region Deputy CM from the other. On this promise, Andhra Pradesh was formed on 1 November 1956.

Step 3 — Agitations. 1969 Telangana agitation: safeguards not honoured, demand for separate State. 1972-73 Jai Andhra agitation: after a court upheld Mulki Rules, coastal Andhra and Rayalaseema demanded separate Andhra. State became ungovernable, President's Rule in January 1973.

Step 4 — Six-Point Formula, 21 September 1973. Political settlement: faster development of backward areas with planning board; local-area preference in education and jobs through local cadres; a Central University at Hyderabad; abolish Mulki Rules and Regional Committee and replace with new scheme; amend Constitution; make an Administrative Tribunal for service disputes.

Step 5 — 32nd Amendment in 1973 (passed 1974) and Articles 371D and 371E. Article 371D lets the President by ORDER provide equitable opportunities in public employment and education in AP, organise civil posts into local cadres, specify local areas, provide direct recruitment by local area, and make an Administrative Tribunal. Article 371E asks Parliament to make a Central University in AP — that became University of Hyderabad (1974). The working rule is the AP Public Employment (Organisation of Local Cadres and Regulation of Direct Recruitment) Order, 1975 — called the Presidential Order. It defines who is a local candidate of a zone and how local vs non-local posts are shared. Article 371D survived the 2014 bifurcation and applies to both AP and Telangana.

Why personal for you: when an SI is posted zone-wise, not by State merit, that is Article 371D and the Presidential Order at work. Service fights go to Tribunal machinery.

Compare for exam:
- Article 16(3): Parliament by LAW can require residence for a class of posts.
- Article 371D: President by ORDER makes local cadres and local-area recruitment in AP.
- Article 16(4): State provides reservation for backward classes.

Exam traps: Gentlemen's Agreement came BEFORE formation of AP (20 Feb 1956 before 1 Nov 1956), not after. Formula came before Amendment, never reverse. Match each pact with its two regions.

## Topic 29 — Potti Sriramulu and the two birthdays: 1953 and 1956

Demand is old. Telugu districts of Madras Presidency wanted a separate province since the first Andhra Mahasabha at Bapatla in May 1913, presided by B.N. Sarma. Do not mix this with the Andhra Maha Sabha of Hyderabad State (Telangana), first held at Jogipet, Medak, in 1930. Two different bodies, regions, decades — examiners swap them.

Committees said no:
- Dar Commission (Linguistic Provinces Commission, S.K. Dar, report 10 December 1948): against states purely on language, feared harm to unity; suggested reorganisation on contiguity, finance, administration.
- JVP Committee (Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, report 1 April 1949): agreed broadly, said time not suitable, but Andhra province could be formed IF claim to Madras city is given up. Telugus would not give up Madras, so deadlock continued. A Partition Committee under Kumaraswami Raja in late 1949 also failed on Madras city.

What Potti Sriramulu did: Gandhian freedom fighter (born 16 March 1901, jailed 1930 and 1942). He started fast unto death on 19 October 1952 at Bulusu Sambamurti's house in Madras for a separate Andhra State. He fasted 58 days and died on the night of 15 December 1952. His death caused grief and riots across Telugu districts. On 19 December 1952 Prime Minister Nehru announced a separate Andhra State (without Madras city).

Two formations — never mix:
- Andhra State, 1 October 1953, by Andhra State Act 1953 (assented 14 September 1953). Made from Telugu districts of Madras State (11 districts). Capital Kurnool (High Court at Guntur). First CM Tanguturi Prakasam. Significance: first State of independent India formed on linguistic basis (Odisha before Independence was for Odia speakers). Cause: Sriramulu's death and agitation.
- Andhra Pradesh, 1 November 1956, by States Reorganisation Act 1956 plus 7th Amendment, on report of States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali chair, with K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru, appointed 1953, reported 1955). Made from Andhra State plus Telugu areas of Hyderabad State. Capital Hyderabad. First CM Neelam Sanjiva Reddy. Cause: Commission, plus Gentlemen's Agreement.

Trap in one line: 1953 came from a DEATH, 1956 came from a COMMISSION. Andhra State in October 1953 came BEFORE the SRC reported in 1955, so "Andhra State was formed on SRC recommendation" is false on dates alone. SRC gave Andhra Pradesh in 1956.

## Hard rules you must not get wrong in exam hall

- Sabarimala review is PENDING before nine judges. Never call 2018 settled.
- Governor-assent law is EVOLVING. Punjab 2023 said cannot sit forever. Tamil Nadu April 2025 added timelines plus deemed assent. Presidential Reference November 2025 says no judicial timelines, no deemed assent, but inaction can be pushed by limited mandamus. Present both 2025 steps as flux.
- 106th Amendment (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023, Articles 330A, 332A, 239AA, Article 334A) gives one-third reservation for women in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, but works ONLY after the first census after commencement plus delimitation. It is not yet operative on its own. It lasts 15 years from commencement, seats rotate after each delimitation. It does not apply to Rajya Sabha or Legislative Councils.
- Lok Sabha maximum is 550 (530 from States plus 20 from Union Territories under Article 81; 552 before removal of two Anglo-Indian nominated seats by 104th Amendment in January 2020; present elected strength 543).
- Andhra Pradesh has 26 districts since 4 April 2022 (13 old plus 13 new notified 26 January 2022).
- Polavaram town/mandal and Polavaram Project are in Eluru district (Polavaram Assembly No. 67, Eluru district; Eluru district formed 4 April 2022). Note: in November 2025 the State announced proposals for new districts including a Polavaram district with headquarters Rampachodavaram — that is a proposal, not the exam answer; for this syllabus answer remains Eluru district.

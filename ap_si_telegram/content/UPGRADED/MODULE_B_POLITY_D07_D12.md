# 📚 DAY 7 — PARLIAMENT AND THE STATE LEGISLATURE

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** Parliament = **President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha**. The President is **part of Parliament** though he sits in neither House — a favourite one-mark question.

**Strength — get the numbers exactly right.**

⚠️ **Correction to the original pack**, which stated "LS max 552 … 2 Anglo-Indian REMOVED by 104th" in the same breath.

- **Lok Sabha maximum today = 550**: **530 from States + 20 from Union Territories**. The figure **552** was the **pre-2020** ceiling, which included **2 nominated Anglo-Indians**; that nomination **ended on 25 January 2020** under the **104th Amendment**. **Present actual strength = 543.**
- **Rajya Sabha maximum = 250**: **238 representatives of States and UTs + 12 nominated** by the President from literature, science, art and social service. **Present actual strength = 245.**

**Terms.** Lok Sabha **5 years**, extendable by **1 year at a time during a National Emergency**, and that extension **cannot continue beyond 6 months after the Emergency ends**. The Rajya Sabha is a **permanent body that is never dissolved**; **one-third retire every second year**, so each member serves **6 years**.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Money Bill | A Bill dealing **only** with the matters in Art 110(1)(a)–(g) | **Speaker certifies**; RS has **14 days**, recommendations only |
| Financial Bill | Contains money matters **plus** other matters | Needs RS concurrence; **not** a Money Bill |
| Joint sitting (Art 108) | Both Houses meet to break a deadlock | **Never** for a Money Bill or a Constitutional Amendment Bill |
| Quorum | Minimum members needed to conduct business | **One-tenth** of the total membership of the House |
| Effective majority | Majority of the **present strength** of the House, excluding vacancies | Removal of the **Speaker**, **Deputy Speaker**, **RS Chairman** |
| Adjournment sine die | Terminating a sitting with no fixed date to resume | Done by the **presiding officer**, not the President |
| Prorogation | Ending a **session** | Done by the **President** |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Sessions.** **Art 85** — six months may not elapse between the **last sitting of one session** and the **first sitting of the next**. Note what this does **not** say: there is **no constitutional requirement of a minimum number of sittings**, and **no requirement of three sessions a year** — three sessions (Budget, Monsoon, Winter) is **convention**, not law.

**Money Bill — where questions are built.**
- A Money Bill can be introduced **only in the Lok Sabha**, only on the **President's recommendation**.
- The **Speaker's certificate** that a Bill is a Money Bill is declared **final** by Art 110(3).
- The Rajya Sabha **cannot reject or amend** it; it must return it within **14 days**, and its recommendations are **not binding**. If the Rajya Sabha does not return it in 14 days, it is **deemed passed by both Houses**.
- **No joint sitting** for a Money Bill — there can be no deadlock to break.
- On the finality of the Speaker's certificate: *Aadhaar* (**2018**) proceeded on the majority view upholding the certification, but the correctness of that approach was **referred to a larger bench in *Rojer Mathew* (2019)** and **remains pending**. So "the Speaker's certificate is wholly beyond review" is the **safe textual answer**, while the litigation is the nuance.

**Joint sitting (Art 108).** Summoned by the **President**, presided over by the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha** (not the Vice-President), decided by a **simple majority of those present and voting**. Because the Lok Sabha is larger, it effectively prevails. **Held only three times: 1961 (Dowry Prohibition Bill), 1978 (Banking Service Commission Repeal Bill), 2002 (Prevention of Terrorism Bill).**

**Committees.**
- **Public Accounts Committee — 22 members (15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha)**, examines the **CAG's** reports; the Chairman is appointed by the Speaker and has been drawn from the **Opposition by convention since 1967**. Ministers cannot be members.
- **Estimates Committee — 30 members, all from the Lok Sabha**, no Rajya Sabha representation. Known as the "continuous economy committee".
- **Committee on Public Undertakings — 22 members (15 LS + 7 RS)**.

The exam pattern is a **number swap**: PAC 22 / Estimates 30 / CoPU 22, and **Estimates is the Lok Sabha-only one**.

**Privileges (Arts 105 / 194).** Freedom of speech in the House; immunity from proceedings in any court for anything **said or any vote given**; immunity for **publication under the authority of the House**. Privileges have **never been codified** — the Constitution says they shall be as the House may define **by law**, and until then as they were. Note: an MP or MLA enjoys **freedom from arrest in civil cases only**, for **40 days before and 40 days after** a session and during it — **not in criminal cases or preventive detention**. That last point is a direct police-duty fact.

**State Legislature (Arts 168–212).** Legislative **Assembly** maximum **500**, minimum **60** (with named exceptions). A Legislative **Council** cannot exceed **one-third of the Assembly's strength** and cannot be fewer than **40**.

**Art 169 — creation or abolition of a Legislative Council.** Parliament may do it by **law**, but only **after the Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by a special majority** — a majority of the **total membership** of the Assembly **and** not less than **two-thirds of members present and voting**. Such a law is **not deemed a constitutional amendment** under Art 368 and is passed like an ordinary law.

⚠️ **The AP fact the original pack omits.** The **AP Legislative Council** was **abolished in 1985**, **revived in 2007**, and on **27 January 2020 the AP Legislative Assembly passed a resolution to abolish it again. Parliament never enacted the required law, so the Council continues to exist.** Its strength is **58**; the AP Legislative Assembly has **175** seats. **This is the perfect Art 169 question: the Assembly resolution is only a precondition — abolition happens only when Parliament legislates.**

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI holds a warrant against a sitting MLA in a criminal case. The MLA's staff assert legislative privilege because the Assembly is in session.

- **Freedom from arrest under Arts 105/194 applies to civil cases only.** It does **not** extend to **criminal cases** or to **preventive detention**.
- The arrest is therefore lawful. What the officer **must** do is **intimate the presiding officer** — the Speaker or Chairman — of the arrest, the reason and the place of detention, as required by the House's rules.
- Privilege protects **what is said and voted inside the House**; it does not create immunity from the ordinary criminal law outside it.

**The transferable lesson:** legislative privilege is about **speech and civil process**, never about criminal immunity. An officer who accepts the claim at face value commits a dereliction; one who arrests without informing the presiding officer commits a breach of privilege.

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

## 6️⃣ 📝 ADVANCED QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

Consider the following statements regarding the Lok Sabha:

1. Its maximum strength under the Constitution is at present 552.
2. Of its members, not more than 20 may represent the Union Territories.
3. Its term may be extended by one year at a time during a National Emergency.
4. Any extension of its term cannot continue beyond six months after the Emergency ceases.

Which are correct?

A) 2, 3 and 4 only
B) 1, 2 and 3 only
C) 1, 3 and 4 only
D) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** 2, 3 and 4 are each exact constitutional statements — 20 UT seats, a one-year-at-a-time extension during an Emergency, and the six-month tail after it ends.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 1** — the error carried in the original pack. **552 was the pre-2020 ceiling** (530 + 20 + **2 Anglo-Indians**). Since the **104th Amendment** ended Anglo-Indian nomination on **25 January 2020**, the maximum is **550**. The words "**at present**" in the statement are what make it false.
- **Option D** is where almost every candidate taught from older material lands.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** A number that was correct for seventy years and changed in 2020. Examiners love these, because every older book and note still prints the old figure.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **530 + 20 = 550.** Reconstruct it from the components instead of recalling a total. If an option gives **552**, it is quoting a ceiling that no longer exists.

### Q2 — Match the following
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

| List I (Committee) | List II (Composition) |
| --- | --- |
| A. Public Accounts Committee | 1. 30 members, all from the Lok Sabha |
| B. Estimates Committee | 2. 22 members, 15 from LS and 7 from RS |
| C. Committee on Public Undertakings | 3. Members of both Houses, chaired by the Vice-President |
| D. Business Advisory Committee | 4. Constituted separately in each House by its presiding officer |

A) A-2, B-1, C-2, D-4
B) A-1, B-2, C-2, D-4
C) A-2, B-1, C-4, D-3
D) A-2, B-3, C-1, D-4

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** The PAC and the CoPU share the same composition — **22 (15 + 7)**. The **Estimates Committee is the Lok Sabha-only one with 30 members**. The Business Advisory Committee exists **separately in each House**, constituted by that House's presiding officer.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** swaps the PAC and the Estimates Committee — the single most common confusion in the topic.
- **C)** and **D)** introduce entry 3, which is fiction: **no parliamentary financial committee is chaired by the Vice-President**.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Two committees deliberately share one composition entry, so a candidate who assumes each List II item is used **exactly once** rejects the correct answer. **Match questions do not promise one-to-one mapping.**

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **"Estimates is the odd one out — 30, Lok Sabha only."** Fix that pairing first, and every option except the right one collapses.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A State Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by the required special majority for the abolition of its Legislative Council. Three years later, the Council continues to function.

Which of the following explains this correctly?

A) The resolution is invalid unless ratified by half the State legislatures
B) The Council is abolished only when Parliament makes a law to that effect, and Parliament has not done so
C) The resolution required the Governor's prior assent, which was not obtained
D) A Legislative Council once created cannot be abolished

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** **Art 169** makes the Assembly's special-majority resolution a **precondition**, not the operative act. **Abolition takes effect only on a law made by Parliament**, and Parliament is under **no obligation** to enact it. This is precisely the position in **Andhra Pradesh**, where the Assembly resolved to abolish the Council on **27 January 2020** and the Council **still functions**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** imports the **Art 368 ratification** requirement for federal amendments. An Art 169 law is expressly **not** deemed a constitutional amendment.
- **C)** invents a Governor's-assent requirement that Art 169 does not contain.
- **D)** factually wrong, and AP itself disproves it — the Council was **abolished in 1985 and revived in 2007**.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Confusing a **precondition** with the **operative act**. Candidates learn "the Assembly can abolish its Council" and never learn that the Assembly can only **ask**.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Assembly proposes, Parliament disposes.** For Art 169 in both directions — creation and abolition — **Parliament's law is the operative step**.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Lok Sabha maximum is 550 (530 + 20), not 552.** Actual strength 543; Rajya Sabha 250 max, 245 actual.
2. **PAC 22, CoPU 22, Estimates 30 and Lok Sabha-only.**
3. **Freedom from arrest covers civil cases only** — never criminal, never preventive detention.
4. **Art 169: the Assembly's resolution is a precondition; Parliament's law is the act.** AP's Council survives a 2020 abolition resolution.

# 📚 DAY 8 — JUDICIARY, CAG AND PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** India has a **single integrated judiciary**: one pyramid from the district courts through the High Courts to the Supreme Court, applying **both** central and State law. This is unlike the USA, where federal and State court systems run in parallel.

**Supreme Court (Arts 124–147).** Sanctioned strength **34 — the Chief Justice plus 33 judges**. Judges hold office until **65**; **High Court judges until 62**.

**Appointment** is by the **collegium**, a judicial construct rather than a constitutional text: the **Second Judges Case (1993)** and the **Third Judges Case (1998)** read "consultation" in Art 124 to mean **concurrence**. The **99th Amendment** and the **NJAC Act** attempted to replace it and were **struck down in 2015** for violating **judicial independence**, a basic feature.

**Removal (Art 124(4))** — the ground is **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**; the process requires a motion in either House with **100 Lok Sabha** or **50 Rajya Sabha** members' signatures, an inquiry under the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**, and passage in **both Houses by a special majority in the same session**. **No judge has ever been removed**; the closest was **Justice V. Ramaswami (1993)**, where the motion failed in the Lok Sabha.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original jurisdiction | A case starting in that court | **Art 131** — Centre–State and inter-State disputes |
| Writ jurisdiction | Power to issue writs | **Art 32** (SC, **FRs only**) and **Art 226** (HC, **wider**) |
| Advisory jurisdiction | The President seeks the Court's opinion | **Art 143** — opinion is **not binding** |
| Special Leave Petition | Discretionary appeal from any court or tribunal | **Art 136** — the widest appellate power |
| Curative petition | A last remedy after a review is dismissed | *Rupa Ashok Hurra* (2002) |
| Locus standi | The standing to bring a case | **Relaxed** in PIL |
| Epistolary jurisdiction | Treating a letter as a writ petition | *S.P. Gupta* (1981) |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Art 32 vs Art 226 — the distinction that generates the most questions.**

| | **Art 32 (Supreme Court)** | **Art 226 (High Court)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is it itself a Fundamental Right? | **YES** — it is in Part III | **No** — it is a constitutional right |
| For what | **Fundamental Rights only** | **FRs and any other legal right** |
| Can it be refused? | The SC **cannot** refuse where an FR is violated | The HC has **discretion** |
| Suspension | Can be suspended under **Art 359** | Also affected, but the scope differs |
| Territorial reach | All India | Its own territorial jurisdiction, **plus** wherever the cause of action arises (Art 226(2)) |
| Against whom | The "State" under Art 12 | **Any person or authority** |

Ambedkar called **Art 32 the "heart and soul of the Constitution"**. The sharper point for an exam is that **Art 226 is wider in scope but weaker in guarantee**: the High Court may decline relief, the Supreme Court may not.

**The five writs.**
- **Habeas Corpus** — "produce the body". Against **the State and private persons**. The only writ available against a private individual, which makes it the standard remedy against illegal detention.
- **Mandamus** — "we command" a public authority to do its duty. **Not** available against a private person, the President or a Governor, or to enforce a purely discretionary or contractual duty.
- **Prohibition** — issued **to a lower court or tribunal** to stop exceeding jurisdiction. Preventive; **not** against administrative or legislative bodies.
- **Certiorari** — quashes an order already passed. Curative; and since *A.K. Kraipak*, it **is** available against administrative authorities acting judicially.
- **Quo Warranto** — "by what authority" a person holds a **public office of a substantive character**. **Any person may apply**, not merely an aggrieved one.

**CAG (Arts 148–151).** Appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal; term **6 years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier**; removable **in the same manner and on the same grounds as a Supreme Court judge**. He audits the accounts of the Union and the States, and his reports go to the **President (laid before Parliament, then to the PAC)** and to the **Governor (laid before the State legislature)**. **He is not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or of any State** after retirement — an independence safeguard that is frequently asked. Ambedkar considered the CAG the **most important officer under the Constitution**.

Pair it with the exam's standard contrast: **the Finance Commission (Art 280) is constitutional; NITI Aayog is an executive body created by a Cabinet resolution in 2015**, exactly as the Planning Commission was in 1950.

**PIL.** Built by *Hussainara Khatoon* (undertrials), *S.P. Gupta* (1981, relaxed locus and epistolary jurisdiction) and *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (bonded labour). PIL rests on **Arts 32 and 226** and is a device for those who cannot reach a court themselves. It is **not** available to settle private disputes, and courts now impose **costs for frivolous PILs**.

**AP High Court.** Established at **Amaravati on 1 January 2019** by Presidential order under **Section 30 of the AP Reorganisation Act, 2014**; until then AP and Telangana shared a common High Court at Hyderabad.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

A person's relative alleges that he has been picked up by the police and is not shown in any station register.

- The remedy is a **writ of habeas corpus** under **Art 226** before the **AP High Court at Amaravati**, or under **Art 32** before the Supreme Court.
- Habeas corpus is the **one writ that lies against a private person as well**, so it works even if the detention is by someone other than the State.
- It is **not suspendable in the way ADM Jabalpur once allowed**: after the **44th Amendment**, **Arts 20 and 21 survive every emergency**, and *ADM Jabalpur* was **expressly overruled in Puttaswamy (2017)**.
- Operationally, the answer to a habeas petition is the **station records** — which is exactly why the BNSS requires arrest registers, information to a relative, and display of arrest information.

**The transferable lesson:** habeas corpus is the mechanism by which an SI's record-keeping becomes constitutional compliance. **An arrest not in the register is an arrest the State cannot defend.**

## 6️⃣ 📝 ADVANCED QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

Consider the following statements:

1. The jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 is wider than that of the Supreme Court under Article 32.
2. The right to move the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right.
3. A High Court may refuse relief under Article 226 in its discretion.
4. A writ of habeas corpus may be issued against a private individual.

Which are correct?

A) 1, 2 and 3 only
B) 2, 3 and 4 only
C) 1, 2, 3 and 4
D) 1 and 4 only

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** All four are correct, and together they form the complete comparison. Art 226 covers **FRs plus other legal rights** and runs against **any person or authority**; **Art 32 is itself an FR** and so the Supreme Court **cannot decline** where an FR is shown; the High Court **retains discretion**; and **habeas corpus is the one writ that lies against a private person**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** drops statement 4 — for candidates who have absorbed "writs run only against the State".
- **B)** drops statement 1 — for candidates who assume the Supreme Court must be "wider" because it is the higher court.
- **D)** drops the two nuanced statements and keeps only the memorised pair.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The intuition that **higher court = wider power**. It is the **opposite** here: the High Court has **wider subject-matter jurisdiction**, while the Supreme Court has a **stronger guarantee**. Both propositions are true at once, which is why an "all of the above" answer feels wrong and is right.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **"226 is wider; 32 is stronger."** Any option built on "32 is wider" is out immediately.

### Q2 — Assertion & Reason
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

**Assertion (A):** The Comptroller and Auditor General is not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or of any State after ceasing to hold office.

**Reason (R):** The CAG can be removed from office only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a judge of the Supreme Court.

A) Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A
B) Both A and R are true, but R does not correctly explain A
C) A is true, but R is false
D) A is false, but R is true

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** Both statements are true. But they are **two separate independence safeguards**. R protects the CAG **during** tenure by making removal difficult; A protects the office **after** tenure by removing any incentive to favour the government in the hope of a post-retirement appointment. **The manner of removal does not cause the post-retirement bar.**

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** the intuitive pick. Both statements concern "independence", so the candidate assumes a causal link. They are **parallel**, not causal.
- **C)** and **D)** each deny a true statement; both are accurate propositions of constitutional law.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Two facts from the **same theme** presented as cause and effect. This is the standard high-level Assertion–Reason construction: the test is not whether you know the facts but whether you can tell **correlation from explanation**.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Ask literally: **"Does R cause A?"** Not "are they both about independence" — **does one produce the other**. If they merely share a theme, the answer is **B**.

**Difficulty:** 🟡 Level 2 — Competitive

| List I (Writ) | List II (Function) |
| --- | --- |
| A. Mandamus | 1. Quashes an order already made by a lower authority |
| B. Certiorari | 2. Commands a public authority to perform its legal duty |
| C. Quo Warranto | 3. Restrains a lower court from exceeding its jurisdiction |
| D. Prohibition | 4. Questions a person's title to a public office |

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** swaps Mandamus and Certiorari.
- **C)** and **D)** swap Certiorari and Prohibition — the genuinely difficult pair, since both are directed at lower courts.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Art 226 is wider in scope; Art 32 is stronger as a guarantee** and is itself a Fundamental Right.
2. **Habeas corpus is the only writ against a private person**, and it survives every emergency after the 44th Amendment.
3. **CAG: 6 years or age 65, removed like a Supreme Court judge, barred from further office.**
4. **Prohibition stops proceedings before; certiorari quashes an order after.**

# 📚 DAY 9 — LOCAL GOVERNMENT, PESA AND ANDHRA PRADESH

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** Before 1992, local bodies existed only at the pleasure of State governments — created, superseded and left unelected at will. The **73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992** made them **constitutional institutions**.

- **73rd Amendment** — rural. Inserted **Part IX (Arts 243–243-O)** and the **Eleventh Schedule** with **29 subjects**. Came into force **24 April 1993**, now observed as **National Panchayati Raj Day**.
- **74th Amendment** — urban. Inserted **Part IXA (Arts 243P–243ZG)** and the **Twelfth Schedule** with **18 subjects**. Came into force **1 June 1993**.

Both were **passed in 1992 and commenced in 1993** — a date pair examiners exploit.

**Advanced — compulsory versus voluntary provisions.** This is the distinction the whole topic turns on, and the original pack does not draw it.

**Compulsory** (a State has no choice): a **three-tier structure** (with the exemption below); **direct elections** to all seats at every level; a **five-year term**; **fresh elections within six months** of a dissolution; reservation of seats for **SCs and STs in proportion to population**; reservation of **not less than one-third of seats and of chairperson posts for women**; a **State Election Commission** (Art 243K); a **State Finance Commission every five years** (Art 243-I); and the **minimum age of 21** to contest.

**Voluntary** (left to the State): reservation for **backward classes**; devolution of the **29 / 18 subjects** — the Schedules are a **menu, not a mandate**; granting financial powers; and the composition of the District Planning Committee beyond the constitutional minimum.

**This is why "the 73rd Amendment transferred 29 subjects to panchayats" is false.** It **authorised** the State legislature to endow panchayats with such powers as may be necessary — the Eleventh Schedule is a list of what **may** be devolved.

**Exemption from three tiers:** a State with a population **not exceeding twenty lakhs** need not constitute the **intermediate** (block) level.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gram Sabha | The body of **all registered voters** of a village | Art 243(b) — the foundation of PESA |
| Gram Panchayat | The **elected** body at village level | Distinct from the Gram Sabha |
| State Election Commission | Conducts panchayat and municipal elections | **Art 243K** — distinct from the ECI (Art 324) |
| State Finance Commission | Reviews the financial position of local bodies every 5 years | Art 243-I (rural), Art 243Y (urban) |
| Nagar Panchayat | Urban body for an area **in transition** from rural to urban | Smallest of the three urban bodies |
| Scheduled Area | An area notified under the **Fifth Schedule** | Part IX does **not** apply directly — hence PESA |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**The three urban bodies (Art 243Q):** **Nagar Panchayat** for a **transitional area**; **Municipal Council** for a **smaller urban area**; **Municipal Corporation** for a **larger urban area**. The Governor decides the classification, having regard to population, density, revenue, employment in non-agricultural activities and economic importance.

**Committees under the 74th:** **Wards Committees** are **compulsory** in municipalities with a population of **three lakhs or more** (Art 243S). The **District Planning Committee** (Art 243ZD) consolidates the plans of panchayats and municipalities in a district — **at least four-fifths of its members are elected** by and from the elected members of the district panchayat and municipalities. The **Metropolitan Planning Committee** (Art 243ZE) is for areas with a population of **ten lakhs or more**.

**Art 243-O and Art 243ZG — the bar on courts.** No court may question the validity of a law relating to the **delimitation of constituencies or the allotment of seats**, and **no election to a panchayat or municipality may be questioned except by an election petition** presented as State law provides. This mirrors **Art 329** for parliamentary and State-legislature elections. For an SI on election duty the consequence is direct: **grievances go to the election tribunal, not to a writ court, and not to the police station.**

⚠️ **GAP FILLED — Art 243M and PESA, 1996.**

**Part IX does not apply to:**
- The **Scheduled Areas** under the **Fifth Schedule** and the **tribal areas** under the **Sixth Schedule**;
- The States of **Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram**;
- The **hill areas of Manipur** with an existing district council;
- The **Darjeeling** area of West Bengal, so far as the Gorkha Hill Council is concerned.

Art 243M also empowers **Parliament** to extend Part IX to the Scheduled and tribal areas with exceptions and modifications — and that is exactly what the **Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — PESA** did.

**What PESA gives the Gram Sabha in a Scheduled Area**, and why it matters to an SI posted there:
- The **Gram Sabha**, not the Gram Panchayat, is the primary authority — it approves plans and identifies beneficiaries.
- **Mandatory consultation** before **land acquisition** and before **resettlement** of displaced persons.
- **Prevention of alienation of tribal land** and power to **restore** unlawfully alienated land.
- **Ownership of minor forest produce.**
- **Regulation or prohibition of the sale and consumption of intoxicants.**
- Control over **money lending** to Scheduled Tribes, and over **minor water bodies** and **minor minerals**.

**Andhra Pradesh has Scheduled Areas** — tracts in **Alluri Sitharama Raju, Parvathipuram Manyam, Eluru, Palnadu, Srikakulam, Anakapalli and Kakinada** districts. The **AP Panchayat Raj Act, 1994** is the conformity legislation, and AP has issued PESA-related rules for these areas.

**Andhra Pradesh specifics.**
- Three tiers: **Gram Panchayat → Mandal Parishad → Zilla Parishad**. AP uses **mandals**, not blocks, following the 1985–86 mandal reorganisation.
- **MPTC** (Mandal Parishad Territorial Constituency) and **ZPTC** (Zilla Parishad Territorial Constituency) members are **directly elected**; the **Mandal Parishad President and Zilla Parishad Chairperson are indirectly elected** from among them. The **Sarpanch is directly elected**.
- **AP provides 50% reservation for women** in panchayat raj bodies by **State law** — above the constitutional floor of one-third.
- The **AP State Election Commission** conducts these polls.
- AP has **26 districts** following the reorganisation of **4 April 2022** (from 13).

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

A mining contractor begins extracting minor minerals in a village inside a **Fifth Schedule Scheduled Area** of Alluri Sitharama Raju district, producing a State-issued permit. Villagers obstruct the work and a law-and-order situation develops.

- The instinct is to treat this as pure obstruction. **Under PESA, the Gram Sabha's recommendation is required for the grant of prospecting licences or mining leases for minor minerals in a Scheduled Area**, and the Gram Sabha has statutory competence over land alienation and minor forest produce.
- So the villagers may be asserting a **statutory right**, not merely obstructing. The **first verification is whether the Gram Sabha resolution exists**.
- An SI who registers a case without that check risks acting against the very statute the State enacted to protect the area — and creates a situation that will not survive scrutiny.

**The transferable lesson:** in a Scheduled Area, **the ordinary panchayat framework is not the operative law**. PESA is. Knowing which legal regime applies to the ground under your feet is the difference between lawful and unlawful policing.

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

Consider the following statements regarding the 73rd Amendment:

1. It transferred the 29 subjects listed in the Eleventh Schedule to the panchayats.
2. Reservation of not less than one-third of the seats for women is a compulsory provision.
3. Reservation for backward classes is left to the discretion of the State legislature.
4. A State with a population not exceeding twenty lakhs need not constitute panchayats at the intermediate level.

Which are correct?

A) 2, 3 and 4 only
B) 1, 2 and 3 only
C) 1, 2 and 4 only
D) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** 2, 3 and 4 are exact. The women's reservation floor is **compulsory**; backward-class reservation is **voluntary**; and the twenty-lakh exemption applies to the **intermediate** tier alone.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 1** — the trap, and the verb is the giveaway. The Amendment did **not "transfer"** anything. It **empowered State legislatures** to endow panchayats with such powers as may be necessary. The Eleventh Schedule is a **menu of what may be devolved**, which is why devolution varies enormously between States.
- **Option D** is where a candidate taught the standard summary line lands.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** A **verb-level** trap. "Transferred", "vested" and "conferred" imply automatic devolution; the constitutional word is **"may by law endow"**. Actual devolution is a **State policy choice**, which is the whole reason State devolution indices differ.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **The Constitution created the structure; the State decides the powers.** Any statement claiming the 73rd or 74th **gave subjects** to local bodies is false.

### Q2 — Assertion & Reason
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

**Assertion (A):** The provisions of Part IX of the Constitution were extended to the Scheduled Areas by a separate Act of Parliament.

**Reason (R):** Article 243M provides that Part IX shall not apply to the Scheduled Areas referred to in the Fifth Schedule.

A) Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A
B) Both A and R are true, but R does not correctly explain A
C) A is true, but R is false
D) A is false, but R is true

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** Both are true and the relationship is genuinely causal. **Art 243M excludes** the Scheduled Areas from Part IX, and it is **because of that exclusion** that **Parliament enacted PESA, 1996** to extend Part IX there with modifications. R states the precise reason A was necessary.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** would be right only if the exclusion and the extension were unrelated. They are directly connected: **Art 243M(4)(b) itself authorises Parliament to make the extension.**
- **C)** denies the exclusion, which is the plain text of Art 243M.
- **D)** denies PESA, which exists.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Most candidates have never met **Art 243M**, so they cannot evaluate R and guess at the relationship. The question rewards knowing **why PESA exists**, not merely that it does.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **"Excluded by 243M, restored by PESA."** That one phrase answers every question in this sub-topic.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A defeated candidate in a Gram Panchayat election in Andhra Pradesh alleges that the poll was vitiated by irregularities in counting, and files a writ petition in the High Court seeking a fresh election.

Which of the following is the correct position?

A) The writ is maintainable, since the right to contest is a Fundamental Right
B) The writ is not maintainable; an election can be questioned only by an election petition to the authority prescribed by State law
C) The writ is maintainable only if the State Election Commission declines to act
D) The dispute lies before the Election Commission of India, which supervises all elections in the country

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** **Art 243-O** bars courts from entertaining a challenge to a panchayat election otherwise than **by an election petition** presented to the authority and in the manner provided by State law. This mirrors **Art 329** for parliamentary and Assembly elections.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** the right to contest an election is **neither a Fundamental Right nor a common-law right** — it is a **statutory right**. A well-established proposition that candidates routinely get wrong.
- **C)** invents a conditional gateway that Art 243-O does not contain. The bar is not contingent on the SEC's conduct.
- **D)** confuses the two commissions. **Panchayat and municipal elections are conducted by the State Election Commission under Art 243K**, not by the **ECI under Art 324**. This is the single most common institutional confusion in local government.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Option D targets the **SEC/ECI** confusion; option A targets the belief that voting and contesting are Fundamental Rights. Both misconceptions are widespread and both are directly relevant to an officer on election duty.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Local body poll → State Election Commission → election petition.** No step in that chain involves the ECI or a writ court.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Passed 1992, in force 1993** — 73rd on 24 April, 74th on 1 June.
2. **The Schedules are a menu.** The 73rd/74th created structures; **devolution of subjects is a State choice**.
3. **Excluded by Art 243M, restored by PESA 1996** — and **AP has Scheduled Areas**, so PESA is operative field law there.
4. **SEC (Art 243K) runs local polls; the ECI (Art 324) does not. Disputes go by election petition (Art 243-O), not by writ.**

# 📚 DAY 10 — THE AMENDMENT SPINE

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation — Art 368 and the three procedures.** Candidates memorise amendment numbers and never learn that amendments come in **three different procedural classes**.

**Class 1 — by a simple majority**, outside Art 368: creating or abolishing a State Legislative Council (**Art 169**), altering State boundaries or names (**Arts 2–4**), citizenship, salaries, quorum, the Fifth and Sixth Schedules. **These are not counted as constitutional amendments.**

**Class 2 — by a special majority** under Art 368: a majority of the **total membership** of each House **and** two-thirds of members **present and voting**. This covers Fundamental Rights, DPSP and everything not in Class 1 or 3.

**Class 3 — special majority plus ratification by at least half the State legislatures**: the election of the President, the extent of Union and State executive power, the Supreme Court and High Courts, the distribution of legislative powers, any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule, the representation of States in Parliament, and **Art 368 itself**. Ratification requires a **simple majority** in the State legislatures, and there is **no time limit** within which States must ratify.

Note two things that catch candidates: **the President must give assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill — the 24th Amendment made assent mandatory**, and **there is no provision for a joint sitting** on an Amendment Bill.

| Basic structure | The core that no amendment may damage | Kesavananda 1973 |
| Anti-defection | Disqualification for defecting from a party | **Tenth Schedule**, 52nd Amendment |

**The 106th Amendment in detail** — the highest-probability amendment question for a 2026 cycle:
- Reserves **one-third of the seats** in the **Lok Sabha**, the **State Legislative Assemblies** and the **Delhi Assembly** for women.
- The reservation applies **within** the seats already reserved for **SCs and STs** as well.
- It takes effect **only after the first Census conducted after its commencement, and the delimitation exercise that follows**. It is therefore **enacted but not yet operative**.
- It will operate for **15 years**, extendable by Parliament.
- It does **not** cover the **Rajya Sabha** or **State Legislative Councils** — a very common trap.

**The 102nd and 105th together** are a cause-and-effect pair: the 102nd inserted Art 342A, which was read in the **Maratha reservation** judgment (2021) as taking away the States' power to identify SEBCs; the **105th** was enacted to **restore** it. Examiners set this as an Assertion–Reason.

**The Ninth Schedule.** Created by the **1st Amendment** to protect land-reform laws from judicial review under **Art 31B**. **I.R. Coelho (2007)** held that any law placed in the Ninth Schedule **after 24 April 1973** — the date of *Kesavananda* — **is open to challenge on basic-structure grounds**. The Ninth Schedule is therefore **not** an absolute shield.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI is assigned to election duty. Being asked why a particular constituency shows reserved seats, the officer should be able to place the constitutional basis: **Arts 330 and 332** reserve seats for SCs and STs in the Lok Sabha and Assemblies; the **104th Amendment** extended that reservation to **25 January 2030** and simultaneously **ended Anglo-Indian nomination**; and the **106th Amendment** will add a **one-third reservation for women** — **but only after the next Census and delimitation**, so it is **not yet reflected on any ballot**.

**The transferable lesson:** "the law has been passed" and "the law is in operation" are different states. The 106th Amendment is **enacted but not operative**; the 44th Amendment's preventive-detention change is **enacted but never notified**. An exam option that treats enactment as operation is a trap, and an officer who does the same misapplies the law.

# 📚 DAY 11 — RIGHTS AGAINST EXPLOITATION, FREEDOM OF RELIGION, CULTURAL AND EDUCATIONAL RIGHTS, CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDIES (Arts 23–35)

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** This block contains the Fundamental Rights that an SI enforces most directly, because **Arts 23 and 24 create criminal liability for private persons**.

**Art 23 — traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour** are prohibited and any contravention is an **offence punishable in accordance with law**.
- It is available against **the State and private persons** — **horizontal**.
- It protects **citizens and non-citizens** alike.
- **Exception in 23(2):** the State may impose **compulsory service for public purposes** — such as military or social service — provided it makes **no discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class**. Note the word "**only**".
- ***People's Union for Democratic Rights* (1982)** — the Asiad Workers case: **payment of less than the minimum wage amounts to "forced labour" under Art 23.** This is the expansion that makes Art 23 operationally significant.
- Implementing statutes: the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976**, the **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**, and BNS provisions on trafficking and bonded labour.

**Art 24 — no child below fourteen** shall be employed in any **factory, mine or other hazardous employment**.
- Note precisely what Art 24 does **not** say: it does **not** ban all child labour. Non-hazardous work is outside Art 24.
- The **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016** went further than the Constitution: it prohibits the employment of children **below 14 in all occupations**, with narrow exceptions for **family enterprises outside school hours** and the **entertainment industry**, and it creates a new category of **"adolescents" (14–18)** barred from **hazardous** occupations.
- ***M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996)** — the Sivakasi case — laid down directions on the rehabilitation of child workers.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Begar | Forced labour without payment | Art 23 |
| Bonded labour | Working to repay a debt, without real freedom to leave | Art 23 + the 1976 Act |
| Denomination | A religious sect with a common faith, organisation and name | Art 26 |
| Propagate | To transmit or spread one's faith | Art 25 — **does not include a right to convert by force or fraud** |
| Minority institution | An institution established **and** administered by a minority | Art 30 — **both limbs required** |
| Epistolary jurisdiction | A letter treated as a writ petition | PIL under Arts 32/226 |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Art 25 — freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion.** Available to **all persons**, including non-citizens. It is subject to **public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III**.

**Art 25(2)** preserves two State powers that are constantly tested:
- **25(2)(a)** — regulating or restricting any **economic, financial, political or other secular activity** associated with religious practice.
- **25(2)(b)** — providing for **social welfare and reform**, and **throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus**. The explanation extends "Hindus" to **Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists** for this purpose.

**The essential religious practices doctrine.** Only practices **essential** to a religion get Art 25 protection; the court decides what is essential. **Sabarimala (2018)** applied it 4:1 to permit the entry of women aged 10–50. ⚠️ **The review was referred to a nine-judge bench in November 2019 and the questions remain pending.** Teach it as **unsettled**.

**Art 26 — rights of a religious denomination**: to establish and maintain institutions, manage its own affairs **in matters of religion**, own and acquire property, and administer such property in accordance with law. The crucial contrast with Art 25: **Art 26 is a right of a collective body**, and while management of **religious** affairs is protected, **administration of property is subject to law**.

**Art 27 — no person may be compelled to pay any tax the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion.** The distinction the examiner uses is **tax versus fee**: a **fee** charged for services rendered — for pilgrim facilities, for regulation of a religious endowment — is **not** barred, because a fee is a payment for a service, not a compulsory exaction for the promotion of religion.

**Art 28 — religious instruction, in four situations:**

| Type of institution | Religious instruction |
| --- | --- |
| **Wholly maintained** out of State funds | **Completely prohibited** |
| **Administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust** requiring it | **Permitted** |
| **Recognised** by the State | Permitted, **with consent** |
| **Aided** by State funds | Permitted, **with consent** |

For a minor, consent is that of the **guardian**. This four-way table is the question; a two-way version of it is the trap.

**Art 29 — protection of the interests of minorities.** 29(1) protects any **section of citizens** having a distinct language, script or culture — note that it is **not confined to minorities** despite the marginal heading. 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to a State-maintained or State-aided institution **on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them** — and that protection is for **any citizen**.

**Art 30 — the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.** Both limbs are required: **established AND administered**. ***T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (2002, 11 judges)** held that the unit for determining a linguistic or religious minority is the **State**, not the country. **Art 30(1A)** requires that compensation on compulsory acquisition of a minority institution's property must not restrict or abrogate the right. **Art 15(5)** expressly **excludes minority institutions** — the 93rd Amendment's carve-out.

**Arts 32–35 — remedies.**
- **Art 32** — the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Part III rights, itself a **Fundamental Right**. **32(2)** the power to issue the five writs; **32(4)** the right may **not** be suspended except as the Constitution provides — that is, under **Art 359**, and **never for Arts 20 and 21** after the 44th Amendment.
- **Art 33** — **Parliament** may **restrict or abrogate** the application of Fundamental Rights to the **armed forces, forces charged with the maintenance of public order, intelligence organisations and telecommunication systems** set up for them. It is **Parliament alone** — a State legislature cannot do it. **For a police aspirant this is the single most personally relevant Article in Part III**: it is the constitutional basis of restrictions on police personnel forming associations or unions.
- **Art 34** — restriction on rights while **martial law** is in force; Parliament may indemnify acts done in that period.
- **Art 35** — **Parliament alone**, and not a State legislature, may make laws prescribing **punishment** for acts declared offences under Part III (such as Arts 17, 23 and 24) and for the matters in Arts 16(3), 32(3) and 33/34. This is why the **Protection of Civil Rights Act** and the **Bonded Labour Act** are **central** legislation.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI receives information that labourers at a brick kiln are being kept against their will to work off advances taken from the owner, and that several appear to be under fourteen.

The constitutional and statutory frame stacks as follows:
- **Art 23** prohibits **begar and bonded labour** and applies **directly against the private owner**; the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** provides for release and rehabilitation, and the District Magistrate is the releasing authority.
- **Art 24** plus the **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016** cover the children — and note that the 2016 Act bars **all** employment below 14, which is **wider** than Art 24's hazardous-employment limit.
- Payment below the **minimum wage** is itself **forced labour** under **PUDR (1982)**, so the offence does not depend on proving physical confinement.
- **Art 35** explains why the SI applies **central** statutes here: Parliament alone can prescribe punishment for the breach of Part III rights.

**The transferable lesson:** Arts 23 and 24 are among the very few Fundamental Rights an SI enforces **as criminal law against private persons**. The operational trigger is **not chains — it is wages and debt**.

- **Statement 2** — false. Art 23 uses no citizenship limitation and protects **all persons**, which matters directly in trafficking cases involving foreign nationals.
- **Option D** drops statement 4 — for candidates who know the text but not the case law that gives Art 23 its operational reach.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Statement 4 is the discriminator. Without *PUDR*, an officer thinks bonded labour requires proof of physical restraint. With it, **underpayment alone** suffices.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Arts 23 and 24 protect "persons" and bind private hands.** If an option restricts either to citizens, strike it.

### Q2 — Match the following
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

| List I (Type of educational institution) | List II (Position on religious instruction under Art 28) |
| --- | --- |
| A. Wholly maintained out of State funds | 1. Permitted with the consent of the person or guardian |
| B. Administered by the State but established under a trust requiring such instruction | 2. Completely prohibited |
| C. Recognised by the State | 3. Permitted without any requirement of consent |
| D. Receiving aid out of State funds | 4. Permitted with the consent of the person or guardian |

A) A-2, B-3, C-1, D-4
B) A-2, B-1, C-3, D-4
C) A-3, B-2, C-1, D-4
D) A-2, B-4, C-3, D-1

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** The four-way scheme of Art 28: **wholly State-maintained → prohibited**; **State-administered but established under a trust or endowment requiring instruction → permitted, and no consent requirement applies**; **recognised → consent**; **aided → consent**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** and **D)** misplace the trust-established institution, which is the **only** one where instruction is permitted **without** consent.
- **C)** inverts the first entry — the wholly State-funded institution is the **one absolute prohibition** in Art 28, and any option that permits instruction there is wrong at a glance.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Most candidates carry a **two-way** version of Art 28 — "State-funded no, aided with consent" — and cannot place the **trust-established** institution at all. It is the **exception to the exception**, and that is why it is set.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Anchor the two extremes: **wholly funded = never; trust-established = freely.** The remaining two (recognised and aided) both need **consent**, so they are interchangeable in effect and cannot be the discriminator.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A State government enacts a law prescribing imprisonment for the practice of untouchability within that State.

Which of the following is the correct constitutional position?

A) The law is valid, as public order and police are State subjects
B) The law is open to challenge, since Article 35 vests the power to prescribe punishment for offences under Part III exclusively in Parliament
C) The law is valid, as Article 17 permits any legislature to punish untouchability
D) The law is invalid, because untouchability can only be dealt with by the courts and not by legislation

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** **Art 35** reserves to **Parliament alone** the power to make laws prescribing **punishment** for acts declared to be offences under Part III — which is precisely why the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** and the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** are **central** statutes.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** true as a general proposition — public order and police **are** State subjects under List II — but **Art 35 is a specific carve-out that overrides the general distribution** for this purpose. The classic "correct general rule, wrong specific application" distractor.
- **C)** misreads Art 17. It says untouchability is "**an offence punishable in accordance with law**" — it does not say **which** legislature makes that law. Art 35 answers that.
- **D)** absurd on its face; Art 17 expressly contemplates punishment by law.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Option A is engineered for a police aspirant, who knows that **police and public order are State subjects** and will reason from that familiar rule straight past the specific provision. **A specific constitutional provision defeats a general distribution of powers.**

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **"Punishment for a Part III offence = Parliament, via Art 35."** Arts 17, 23 and 24 are the Part III offences, and all three are enforced by **central** law.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Arts 23 and 24 bind private persons and protect all persons**, not only citizens.
2. **Sub-minimum wage is forced labour** (*PUDR*, 1982) — the operational trigger is **wages and debt**, not chains.
3. **Art 28 has four situations, not two.** Only the **trust-established** institution permits instruction **without consent**.
4. **Art 33 is the police officer's own Article**, and **Art 35 puts punishment for Part III offences in Parliament's hands alone**.

## 3️⃣ 🧠 The Seven-Step Method for an Unseen Question

**Step 1 — Identify the rights-holder.** Is the stem about a **person** or a **citizen**? Arts 14, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 28 → **all persons**. Arts 15, 16, 19, 29, 30 → **citizens**. If the stem names a foreigner or a company, every citizen-only option dies at once.

**Step 2 — Hunt the absolute word.** *Always, never, all, only, any, automatically, fully, immediately.* In constitutional law almost every rule has an exception, so an absolute is usually planted. **Exceptions to the exception: Art 17 and the non-suspendability of Arts 20 and 21 really are absolute.**

**Step 3 — Check the tense.** Has the position **changed**? The high-frequency changes: LS maximum **552 → 550** (2020); the **50% ceiling after Janhit Abhiyan**; **31C after Property Owners Association**; the **Governor's assent after November 2025**; the **106th Amendment not yet operative**; the **44th Amendment's 2-month rule never notified**.

**Step 4 — Separate enactment from operation.** "Passed" is not "in force". Two live examples sit in this syllabus.

**Step 5 — Name the institution precisely.** ECI or **SEC**? Finance Commission or **NITI Aayog**? Parliament or the **State legislature**? Judicial or **Executive** Magistrate? President or **Governor**? Most distractors are built by moving a correct fact to the **wrong institution**.

**Step 6 — For Assertion–Reason, ask whether R causes A.** Do not ask whether both are true and related in theme. **Two true facts on the same subject that do not stand in a causal relationship give answer B**, and that is where the marks are.

**Step 7 — For chronology, anchor on two dates you are certain of** and place the rest relative to them. Reliable anchors: **1950** commencement · **1973** Kesavananda, and Art 371D · **1976** the 42nd · **1978** the 44th · **1992** Mandal, and the 73rd/74th · **1994** Bommai · **2002** the 86th · **2016** GST · **2019** EWS · **2020** the 104th · **2023** the 106th · **2024** the new criminal codes in force.

## 4️⃣ 🎯 The Distractor Taxonomy

Every well-made distractor in this exam is one of six kinds. Learn to name them:

1. **Number swap** — the right fact with the wrong figure (552 for 550; 2 months for 3).
2. **Institution swap** — the right function with the wrong body (ECI for SEC; NITI for the Finance Commission).
3. **Stage transplant** — a genuine requirement moved to a different stage (the EC certificate demanded at a 356 proclamation instead of at extension).
4. **Period transplant** — historically correct, wrong era (356 is a non-justiciable political question — true before 1994).
5. **Absolute word** — a true rule stated without its exception ("Art 19 is automatically suspended on any 352").
6. **Cause–effect reversal** — the amendment placed before the judgment that provoked it.

When two options look equally defensible, ask **which of these six a setter would have built**, and the planted one becomes visible.

## 5️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Read the rights-holder first, the absolute word second, the date third.** Most questions fall to those three passes.
2. **Enactment is not operation.** The 106th Amendment and the 44th's preventive-detention clause both prove it.
3. **In Assertion–Reason, the answer is usually B** when both statements are true and share a theme without a causal link.
4. **Name the six distractor types.** Once you can label the trap, you stop falling into it.

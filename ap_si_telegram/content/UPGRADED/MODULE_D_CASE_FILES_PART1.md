# MODULE D — CASE FILES, PART 1 (Days 1–6)
**Written for a candidate with zero legal background.** Every case is broken into: what the situation was, what happened step by step, what the Court decided, a plain analogy, and the exam takeaway.

**How to read a case name.** *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* means Maneka Gandhi (the person complaining) sued the Union of India (the government). The "v." just means "against". The year is when the Supreme Court decided it. You do **not** need to remember citations or judges' names — only **the facts, the rule, and the year**.

# 🏛️ CASE 1 — State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)
*Why it matters: the very first constitutional amendment happened because of this case.*

### 1. Background
In 1950, Madras State ran medical and engineering colleges. Seats were allotted by a **Communal Government Order** — a fixed quota for each community: so many seats for Brahmins, so many for non-Brahmins, so many for Christians, Muslims, and so on.

Champakam Dorairajan was a woman who wanted to study medicine. She found she could not get in, because the seats reserved for her community were already full — even though her marks were higher than candidates admitted from other communities.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. She went to court saying: the government is denying me admission **because of my caste**.
2. The State's defence was: we are following **Article 46**, a Directive Principle that tells the State to promote the educational interests of weaker sections. So we are obeying the Constitution.
3. The Supreme Court had to decide: when a **Directive Principle** (Part IV) clashes with a **Fundamental Right** (Part III), which one wins?

### 3. What the Court decided
**Fundamental Rights win.** Directive Principles are important goals, but they **cannot override** a Fundamental Right. The Communal G.O. violated **Art 29(2)** (no denial of admission to a State institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language), so it was struck down.

### 4. What happened next — the important part
Parliament did not accept this outcome. Within months it passed the **First Constitutional Amendment, 1951**, which inserted **Art 15(4)** — expressly allowing the State to make special provision for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs.

**So the legal basis of reservation in education exists because Champakam Dorairajan won her case.**

### 5. Simple analogy
Imagine a school rule book has two sections. Section A says "no student may be refused entry because of their family background." Section B says "the school should help weaker students." A principal refuses a student, pointing at Section B. The court says: Section A is a **right**, Section B is only a **goal** — the right wins. So the school management rewrites the rule book to add an exception. That rewrite is the First Amendment.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **FR beats DPSP** (this was the position in 1951).
- Parliament's reply was the **1st Amendment (1951) inserting Art 15(4)**.
- This is **Round 1** of the FR-vs-DPSP fight. Rounds 2–4 are Golaknath, Kesavananda and Minerva Mills.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
"Which amendment was enacted as a consequence of the Champakam Dorairajan judgment?" → **First Amendment, 1951.** Also asked in reverse: "Art 15(4) was inserted by which amendment and why?"

# 🏛️ CASE 2 — E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974)
*Why it matters: it changed Art 14 from a rule about classification into a rule about fairness.*

### 1. Background
E.P. Royappa was a senior **IAS officer** in Tamil Nadu. He held the post of **Chief Secretary** — the topmost civil service post in a State.

He was then moved out of that post and placed in two other positions that he considered **lower in status**, even though his pay was not reduced. He believed the transfer was made for political reasons, because he had displeased the government.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. Royappa challenged the transfer, saying it was **arbitrary** — done for no proper reason.
2. Under the old Art 14 test, the question would have been: *did the government classify officers, and was the classification reasonable?* But here there was **no classification at all**. One man was simply moved.
3. That exposed a hole. If the only test was "reasonable classification", then a purely whimsical decision affecting one person escaped Art 14 entirely.

### 3. What the Court decided
Justice **Bhagwati** said equality is a **dynamic concept** with many aspects — it "cannot be cribbed, cabined and confined" within traditional limits. And then the famous line:

> **Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies.**

Meaning: **if State action is arbitrary, it is automatically unequal, and therefore violates Art 14** — even if no classification is involved.

(On the facts, Royappa actually **lost**, because he could not prove the transfer was mala fide. But the *principle* the Court laid down became far more important than the result.)

### 4. Simple analogy
A teacher has a rule: "students who miss three classes lose marks." That rule is fine — it classifies fairly. But suppose the teacher, with no rule at all, simply decides one morning to cut marks from one student she dislikes. There is no classification to examine. Yet everyone can see it is unfair. Royappa says: **that unfairness is itself a violation of equality.**

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- Art 14 now has **two independent tests**: the old **classification test** (intelligible differentia + rational nexus) and the new **arbitrariness test**.
- **Either one** being failed is enough to strike down State action.
- One-line memory: **no reason = no equality.**

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Equality is the antithesis of arbitrariness" → **Royappa, 1974.**
- Scenario questions where a **valid rule is applied arbitrarily** — the answer is still an Art 14 violation.

# 🏛️ CASE 3 — Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)
*Why it matters: this is the single most important case in Indian constitutional law for a police exam.*

### 1. Background
First understand the **old** position. In ***A.K. Gopalan* (1950)**, the Supreme Court had read Art 21 very narrowly. Art 21 says a person may not be deprived of life or personal liberty "except according to **procedure established by law**". Gopalan held this meant: *as long as there is some law laying down some procedure, that is enough.* The Court would not ask whether the procedure was **fair**. It also held that Arts 14, 19 and 21 were separate, watertight compartments.

The practical result: if Parliament passed a law saying "a person may be detained after a coin toss", that would be a "procedure established by law", and Art 21 would be satisfied. Clearly unsatisfactory.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. **Maneka Gandhi** was issued a passport in 1976.
2. In July 1977 the government ordered her to **surrender the passport** "in the public interest".
3. She asked for the reasons. The government **refused to give any**, saying it was not in the public interest to disclose them.
4. She had **no hearing**, **no reasons**, and **no chance to defend herself**.
5. She challenged the impounding before the Supreme Court.

### 3. What the Court decided
The Court overruled the Gopalan approach and held:

- **The procedure under Art 21 must be "right, just and fair" — not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive.** A procedure that merely *exists* is not enough; it must be **reasonable**.
- **Arts 14, 19 and 21 are not watertight compartments.** A law taking away personal liberty must satisfy **all three** together. This interlocking set is called the **"Golden Triangle"**.
- Natural justice — chiefly the right to be **heard** — is read into Art 21.

In effect, India imported the substance of "**due process**" even though the Constitution's text says "procedure established by law".

### 4. Simple analogy
A hostel warden expels a student and says, "the hostel rulebook allows me to expel anyone." Under the old Gopalan view, that ends the matter — a rule exists. Under Maneka, the court asks three more questions: *Was the student told why? Was he given a chance to explain? Is the rule itself reasonable?* If the answer to any is no, the expulsion fails — even though a rule existed.

### 5. Why a police officer must know this
Every arrest, every detention, every search deprives someone of liberty. After Maneka, it is **not enough** that a section of the BNSS permits the action. The **way** it is carried out must also be fair — grounds communicated, procedure followed, reasons recorded. This is the constitutional root of the **D.K. Basu** arrest guidelines.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Procedure must be fair, just and reasonable** — not merely existing.
- **Golden Triangle = Arts 14 + 19 + 21**, read together.
- Chain to memorise: **Gopalan (1950, narrow) → Maneka (1978, wide) → Puttaswamy (2017, privacy).**

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Which case introduced the fair, just and reasonable test under Art 21?" → **Maneka, 1978.**
- Trap: an option saying Art 21 requires only that *some* procedure exists — that is the **overruled Gopalan** position, a classic "period transplant" distractor.

# 🏛️ CASE 4 — Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) — "the Mandal case"
*Why it matters: every rule you know about reservation comes from here.*

### 1. Background
In 1979 the government appointed the **Mandal Commission** to identify India's socially and educationally backward classes. It reported in 1980, recommending **27% reservation in central government jobs for Other Backward Classes (OBCs)**.

The report sat unused for a decade. In **1990**, the V.P. Singh government announced it would implement the 27% quota. The announcement triggered enormous nationwide protests. Many petitions were filed, and the Supreme Court heard them with a **nine-judge bench** — one of the largest ever assembled for this subject.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. The 27% OBC quota was challenged mainly on the ground that it violated **equality of opportunity** in public employment under **Art 16(1)**.
2. The government's defence rested on **Art 16(4)**, which permits reservation for "any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in the services".
3. The core question: **is Art 16(4) an exception carved out of Art 16(1), or is it a part of what equality actually means?**

### 3. What the Court decided — four holdings you must know

**(a) 16(4) is NOT an exception.** It is an **"emphatic statement" of the equality already promised in 16(1)**. Treating unequals equally produces inequality; so reservation is a **method of achieving real equality**, not a departure from it.

> This is why any exam option describing reservation as an "exception to equality" is **wrong**.

**(b) The 50% ceiling.** Reservation should ordinarily **not exceed 50%** of the available seats or posts. Crossing it is permissible only in **extraordinary situations** — the Court gave the example of remote, far-flung populations outside the mainstream.

**(c) Creamy layer must be excluded.** Within a backward class there are people who have already advanced socially and economically. They corner the benefits meant for the genuinely backward. These advanced individuals — the **"creamy layer"** — must be **identified and excluded**.

**(d) No reservation in promotions.** Reservation applies at the stage of **entry into service**, not in promotions.

### 4. What happened afterwards — the part most notes skip
Parliament disagreed with holding (d) and amended the Constitution:
- **77th Amendment (1995)** → inserted **Art 16(4A)**, restoring reservation in **promotion** for SCs and STs.
- **81st Amendment (2000)** → inserted **Art 16(4B)** on carrying forward **backlog vacancies**.
- **85th Amendment (2001)** → added **consequential seniority** to 16(4A).

⚠️ **And the 50% ceiling has since moved.** In ***Janhit Abhiyan* (2022)** the Court held the 50% ceiling is **not part of the basic structure**, and the **10% EWS quota sits outside it**. So "reservation can never exceed 50%" is **no longer a correct statement**.

### 5. Simple analogy
A school gives extra coaching to students who have had no schooling before. Someone objects: "that is unfair to the rest." The court replies: fairness does not mean giving everyone the same thing — it means **giving each what they need to compete on level terms**. But the court adds three safeguards: don't give away more than half the coaching slots; don't let the children of already well-off families take the slots; and this help is for **getting in**, not for **moving up** later.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **16(4) is a facet of equality, not an exception.**
- **50% ceiling** (for backward-class reservation) + **creamy layer excluded** + **no promotion quota** (later reversed by amendment).
- **Nine judges, 1992.** Anchor every reservation-related date around it.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- Assertion–Reason on the 50% ceiling — watch for the words "automatically" or "never".
- "Creamy layer" → Indra Sawhney, 1992.
- Chronology: **Royappa 1974 → Indra Sawhney 1992 → 77th/85th Amendments → Janhit Abhiyan 2022.**

# 🏛️ CASE 5 — Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)
*Why it matters: the longest and most important judgment in Indian history. It decided what Parliament can never do.*

### 1. Background — the fight that led here
There was a running battle through the 1950s and 60s over **land reform**. State governments wanted to take over large estates and redistribute the land. Landowners went to court citing the **right to property**, which was then a Fundamental Right. Parliament kept amending the Constitution to get around the judgments.

The question underneath it all: **does Parliament have unlimited power to amend the Constitution?**

- In ***Shankari Prasad* (1951)** and ***Sajjan Singh* (1965)**, the Court said yes — Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights.
- In ***Golaknath* (1967)**, the Court reversed itself and said **no** — Parliament **cannot** amend Fundamental Rights at all, because an amendment is a "law" under Art 13(2).
- Parliament struck back with the **24th Amendment (1971)**, declaring that an amendment under Art 368 is **not "law"** for Art 13, and that Parliament **can** amend any part of the Constitution.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. **Kesavananda Bharati** was the head of a **mutt (religious institution) in Kerala**. The Kerala government sought to acquire its land under land-reform legislation.
2. He challenged the acquisition. The case grew far beyond his land into a full examination of Parliament's amending power.
3. The Supreme Court assembled a **thirteen-judge bench** — the largest ever — and heard arguments for **68 days**.
4. The judgment ran to over 700 pages, and the Court split **7 to 6**.

### 3. What the Court decided — the "basic structure" doctrine
The majority found a middle path between the two extremes:

- **Golaknath was overruled.** Parliament **CAN** amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
- **BUT** Parliament **cannot damage or destroy the "basic structure" of the Constitution.**

The Court deliberately did **not** give a closed list of what the basic structure contains. Over later cases, features recognised as basic include: **supremacy of the Constitution, rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review, federalism, secularism, free and fair elections, the independence of the judiciary, and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.**

The Court also held that **the Preamble IS a part of the Constitution** — overruling *Berubari* (1960) on that point.

### 4. Simple analogy
Think of a building. The owner may repaint the walls, change the windows, add a floor, redo the plumbing — the building is his to modify. What he may **not** do is remove the **foundation and the load-bearing pillars**, because then it stops being that building at all. Parliament is the owner; the basic structure is the foundation and the pillars.

### 5. Why the date 24 April 1973 matters separately
That is the date of the judgment, and it became a **legal cut-off** in its own right. In ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** the Court held that any law placed in the **Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973** can be challenged for violating the basic structure. So the Ninth Schedule — long treated as an impenetrable shield — is **not absolute**.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **13 judges, 7:6, 1973.** Parliament may amend, but **not destroy the basic structure**.
- It **overruled Golaknath** (1967) and confirmed the **Preamble is part** of the Constitution.
- **24 April 1973** is the Ninth Schedule cut-off used in *I.R. Coelho*.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Which case propounded the basic structure doctrine?" → Kesavananda, 1973.
- Trap: options claiming the Court gave an **exhaustive list** of basic features — it deliberately did not.
- Chronology: **Shankari Prasad 1951 → Sajjan Singh 1965 → Golaknath 1967 → 24th Amendment 1971 → Kesavananda 1973.**

# 🏛️ CASE 6 — Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)
*Why it matters: it is the case that fixed the limits of the 42nd Amendment.*

### 1. Background
**Minerva Mills** was a textile company in Karnataka. The central government took it over under the **Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974**, on the footing that it was a sick unit.

Meanwhile, the **42nd Amendment (1976)** had made two changes that mattered enormously:

- It amended **Art 368** to say that **no constitutional amendment could be questioned in any court on any ground**, and that there was **no limit** on Parliament's amending power. In plain terms: it tried to **abolish the basic structure doctrine by amendment**.
- It widened **Art 31C**, so that a law made to give effect to **ANY** Directive Principle would be immune from challenge under **Arts 14 and 19**.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. The owners challenged the takeover.
2. The challenge widened into an attack on those two parts of the 42nd Amendment.
3. The Court had to answer: **can Parliament use its amending power to remove the very limit on its amending power?**

### 3. What the Court decided
**Both provisions were struck down.**

- **On Art 368:** Parliament's amending power is itself **limited**. A limited power cannot be used to make itself unlimited. **Judicial review is part of the basic structure**, so a clause barring courts from examining amendments is void.
- **On Art 31C:** the widened version was struck down. The shield reverted to the **pre-42nd position — only laws giving effect to Art 39(b) and 39(c)** are protected.
- The Court then gave the line that defines the case: **the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is itself part of the basic structure.** Neither may be given absolute primacy over the other. *"The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV."*

### 4. Simple analogy
A society's rulebook says "the committee may amend these rules, but never abolish the annual election." The committee then passes an amendment saying "the committee's amendments can never be questioned, and there are no limits." A court would say: you were given a **limited** power. You cannot use a limited power to hand yourself an unlimited one. That is exactly the Minerva reasoning.

### 5. ⚠️ Update you must carry — Property Owners Association (November 2024)
For forty years, the story ended at Minerva. It no longer does. A **nine-judge bench** in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*** held:

- The **unamended Art 31C survives** — that is, the pre-42nd shield for Arts 39(b)–(c) laws is still in force.
- **Art 39(b) was narrowed.** Earlier judgments (*Ranganatha Reddy*, *Sanjeev Coke*) had suggested that **all** private property could be treated as a "material resource of the community", which the State could redistribute. The 2024 bench rejected that broad reading: **not every privately owned resource qualifies.** Whether something is a "material resource of the community" depends on factors such as its nature, scarcity, and the consequences of concentrating it in private hands.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Minerva 1980 struck down the 42nd's attempt to make amendments unreviewable and to widen Art 31C.**
- **31C protects only Arts 39(b) and 39(c).** Any option saying "all DPSP laws" is wrong.
- **FR–DPSP balance = basic structure.**
- **2024 update:** unamended 31C survives; **39(b) is narrower** than previously thought.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- "The 42nd Amendment's widened Art 31C was cut back by which case?" → Minerva Mills, 1980.
- Multi-statement trap: "a law giving effect to **any** Directive Principle is immune from Arts 14 and 19" — that is the **struck-down** version.

# 🏛️ CASE 7 — Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India (1996)
*Why it matters: it answers whether Bharat Ratna is a banned "title".*

### 1. Background
**Art 18(1)** says the State shall not confer any **title**, except a military or academic distinction. The purpose is historical: under British rule, titles such as *Rai Bahadur* and *Khan Bahadur* were handed out to create a class of people formally superior to others. A republic of equals should not have such ranks.

But India also gives **National Awards** — Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan and Padma Shri. Are those "titles" banned by Art 18?

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. Petitions argued that the National Awards were exactly the kind of hereditary-sounding distinction Art 18 meant to abolish, and should be scrapped.
2. The Supreme Court examined the **purpose** of Art 18: to prevent the creation of **artificial social ranks**.
3. It distinguished between **recognising achievement** and **conferring status**.

### 3. What the Court decided
- The National Awards are **NOT "titles"** within Art 18(1). They are **awards recognising exceptional work**, and so they are **constitutionally valid**.
- **BUT** they must **not be used as a prefix or suffix** to the recipient's name. A person may not style himself "Padma Shri So-and-so" as though it were a rank.
- The Court also observed that the awards should be conferred with care, and suggested a high-level committee to advise on selection.

### 4. Simple analogy
A company gives an "Employee of the Year" plaque. That is recognition of work — perfectly fine. It becomes a problem only if the winner starts signing official letters as "Employee-of-the-Year Ramesh", as if it were a designation placing him above colleagues. Recognition, yes. Rank, no.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Awards are valid; titles are banned.** Bharat Ratna and the Padma awards survive Art 18.
- **No prefix, no suffix.**
- Separately remember **Art 18(2)**: a **citizen may not accept a foreign title at all**. It is **18(3)** — covering a **non-citizen holding an office of profit** under the State — that requires the **President's consent**. Most notes blur these two.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Are national awards titles under Art 18?" → No (Balaji Raghavan, 1996), but no prefix use.
- Trap: an option saying a **citizen** needs the President's consent for a foreign title. A citizen is barred **outright**; consent belongs to 18(3).

# 🏛️ CASE 8 — Selvi / Kathi Kalu Oghad: what Art 20(3) really protects
*Why it matters: this decides what an investigating officer may lawfully take from an accused.*

### 1. Background
**Art 20(3)** says no person **accused of an offence** shall be **compelled to be a witness against himself**. The everyday phrase is "right against self-incrimination".

The practical question for a police officer: does that stop me from taking the accused's **fingerprints**, a **handwriting sample**, or a **blood sample**?

### 2. What the Courts decided — the governing distinction

In ***State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961)**, an eleven-judge bench drew the line:

- **"To be a witness" means to furnish TESTIMONY** — to convey **personal knowledge** about the facts of the case, by speech or by producing a document.
- **It does NOT mean merely giving some material for comparison.**

So the following are **outside** Art 20(3) and may lawfully be taken:
- **Fingerprints, palm prints, footprints**
- **Specimen handwriting and signature**
- **Blood, hair, semen and DNA samples**
- **Photographs and identification parades**

The following **are** protected:
- A **confession or statement** extracted under compulsion
- Being **forced to produce a document** that itself conveys the accused's personal knowledge

Later, in ***Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010)**, the Court held that **narco-analysis, polygraph (lie detector) and brain-mapping tests conducted WITHOUT consent violate Art 20(3)** and Art 21, because they extract the contents of a person's mind.

### 3. Simple analogy
A teacher suspects a student copied an answer sheet.

- **Allowed:** asking the student to write a line so the handwriting can be compared. The handwriting is **physical evidence that exists anyway** — the student is not telling the teacher anything.
- **Not allowed:** forcing the student to say "yes, I copied." That is **testimony pulled out of his mind**.

That is the whole of Art 20(3) in one image: **the body may be measured; the mind may not be forced.**

### 4. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Art 20(3) protects the mouth, not the body.**
- Fingerprints, handwriting, blood and DNA → **outside** the protection.
- Narco-analysis, polygraph and brain mapping **without consent** → **inside** the protection (*Selvi*, 2010).
- Art 20(3) applies only to a person **accused of an offence**, and only against **compulsion**.

### 5. 🎯 How it is asked
The commonest false statement in the whole of Part III: "compelling an accused to give a specimen handwriting violates Art 20(3)." It **does not**.

# 🏛️ CASE 9 — ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) — "the Habeas Corpus case"
*Why it matters: the Supreme Court's most criticised judgment, and the reason the 44th Amendment exists.*

### 1. Background
A **National Emergency** was proclaimed in June 1975 on the ground of **internal disturbance**. Large numbers of political opponents were detained without trial under the **Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA)**.

The government also issued an order under **Art 359** suspending the right to move courts for the enforcement of **Arts 14, 21 and 22**.

Detainees filed **habeas corpus** petitions — the remedy that asks a court to "produce the body" and justify a detention. Several **High Courts ruled in the detainees' favour**, holding that even during an emergency a court could examine whether a detention was lawful. The government appealed to the Supreme Court.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. The question put to the Court was stark: **during an Emergency, can a detained person approach a court at all to challenge an illegal detention?**
2. The Attorney General accepted, in open court, that on the government's argument even a **wrongful killing** by the State would leave no remedy while the Emergency lasted.
3. A five-judge bench decided **4 to 1** in favour of the government.

### 3. What the Court decided
The majority held that once the Art 359 order was in force, **no person had any locus standi to move a writ petition under Art 21** — no habeas corpus, whatever the circumstances. Art 21 was treated as the **sole repository** of the right to life and liberty, so suspending access to it left nothing behind.

**Justice H.R. Khanna dissented alone.** He held that the right to life and liberty does not owe its existence solely to the Constitution, and that the State has no power to deprive a person of life or liberty without lawful authority even during an emergency. His dissent cost him the Chief Justiceship — he was superseded and resigned.

### 4. What happened afterwards
The judgment is regarded as a failure of the Court to protect citizens. Parliament responded through the **44th Amendment (1978)**:

- **Arts 20 and 21 can NEVER be suspended**, in any emergency. This directly reverses the practical effect of ADM Jabalpur.
- **"Internal disturbance" was replaced by "armed rebellion"** as a ground for a National Emergency, raising the threshold.
- Art 358's automatic suspension of **Art 19** was confined to emergencies declared on **war or external aggression**.

Finally, in ***Puttaswamy* (2017)**, a nine-judge bench **expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur**, calling the majority view seriously flawed.

### 5. Simple analogy
Imagine a building's fire alarm is switched off during a security drill. The management then argues: since the alarm is off, no one may report a fire, and if the building burns there is no remedy. Common sense says the **right not to be burned alive** does not depend on the alarm being switched on. Justice Khanna's dissent was exactly that point — and the 44th Amendment wrote it into the Constitution.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **ADM Jabalpur (1976): 4:1, habeas corpus unavailable during the Emergency. Khanna J dissented.**
- **44th Amendment (1978) reversed it** — Arts 20 and 21 are non-suspendable.
- **Puttaswamy (2017) expressly overruled it.**
- Chronology: **ADM 1976 → 44th Amendment 1978 → overruled 2017.**

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- Assertion–Reason and statement questions asking whether Art 21 can be suspended today → **no**.
- Chronology sets placing ADM, the 44th, Maneka and Puttaswamy in order.

# 🏛️ CASE 10 — K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) — the Privacy judgment
*Why it matters: it created a new Fundamental Right without any amendment.*

### 1. Background
The **Aadhaar** scheme collected biometric data — fingerprints and iris scans — from residents. Challenges were filed arguing this invaded privacy.

The government's answer was a technical one: **there is no Fundamental Right to privacy in India at all.** It relied on two old judgments, ***M.P. Sharma* (1954)** and ***Kharak Singh* (1962)**, decided by benches of **eight and six judges**, which had said privacy was not a guaranteed right.

Because those benches were so large, a smaller bench could not overrule them. So a **nine-judge bench** was constituted purely to settle the question.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. The narrow question was: **is privacy a Fundamental Right?**
2. The nine judges heard the matter and delivered a **unanimous** verdict.

### 3. What the Court decided
- **Privacy IS a Fundamental Right**, protected as an intrinsic part of the **right to life and personal liberty under Art 21**, and of the freedoms in **Part III** generally.
- ***M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* were overruled** to the extent they said otherwise.
- Privacy is **not absolute**. A restriction must satisfy a three-part test: **(i) legality** — there must be a law; **(ii) legitimate State aim**; **(iii) proportionality** — the means must be proportionate to the objective.
- The Court also **expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur (1976)**.

### 4. Why it matters to a police officer
Privacy is not an abstraction in policing. It governs **surveillance, phone interception, search of digital devices, collection of biometric data, and the handling of CCTV footage**. After Puttaswamy, each of these needs a **legal basis** and must be **proportionate**. This is also the backdrop against which the **BSA 2023** treats electronic records.

### 5. Simple analogy
Privacy was always in the house — it simply had no nameplate on the door. Puttaswamy did not build a new room; it confirmed the room was always there, inside Art 21, and put a name on it. And it added: this door can be opened, but only with a **valid key (a law)**, for a **good reason**, and only **as wide as necessary**.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Nine judges, unanimous, 2017. Privacy is part of Art 21.**
- Test: **legality + legitimate aim + proportionality.**
- It **overruled** *M.P. Sharma*, *Kharak Singh* **and ADM Jabalpur**.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Privacy was recognised as a Fundamental Right in which case and year?" → Puttaswamy, 2017.
- Trap: options saying privacy is **absolute** — it is not.

# 🏛️ CASE 11 — D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997)
*Why it matters: this is the case that governs how you make an arrest.*

### 1. Background
D.K. Basu was the Executive Chairman of a legal aid organisation. He wrote a **letter** to the Chief Justice of India drawing attention to news reports of **deaths in police custody and lockup torture**.

The Supreme Court treated that **letter as a writ petition** — an example of **epistolary jurisdiction** — and took up the matter.

### 2. What the Court decided — the eleven requirements
The Court laid down guidelines binding on all arresting authorities. In substance:

1. Police personnel carrying out an arrest must wear **accurate, visible and clear identification** with name tags, and particulars of all personnel handling interrogation must be **recorded in a register**.
2. A **memo of arrest** must be prepared at the time of arrest, **attested by at least one witness** — a family member or a respectable person of the locality — and **countersigned by the arrestee**, with date and time.
3. The arrestee is entitled to have **one friend, relative or well-wisher informed** of the arrest and the place of detention **as soon as practicable**.
4. Where the next friend lives outside the district, the **time, place of arrest and venue of custody must be notified by the police through the Legal Aid Organisation and the local police station** within 8 to 12 hours.
5. The arrestee must be **made aware of this right** to have someone informed.
6. An **entry in the diary** at the place of detention must record the arrest, who was informed, and the names of the officers in whose custody the arrestee is.
7. On request, the arrestee must be **examined for injuries at the time of arrest**, and the **Inspection Memo** signed by both the arrestee and the officer.
8. A **medical examination every 48 hours** during custody by a doctor from an approved panel.
9. **Copies of all documents** to be sent to the **Magistrate** for record.
10. The arrestee may **meet his lawyer during interrogation**, though not throughout.
11. A **police control room** must be provided with arrest information, to be **displayed on a notice board**.

### 3. Where this lives today
These requirements were later written into statute — first as sections in the CrPC (41B, 41C, 41D, 50A), and now carried into the **BNSS**, which additionally requires designated police officers to maintain and **display information about arrested persons**.

### 4. Simple analogy
Think of a hospital admitting a patient. There is a form, a time stamp, a witness signature, a relative informed, a medical check on arrival, and a record the family can inspect. None of that stops treatment; it simply makes the process **auditable**. D.K. Basu does the same for custody: every step leaves a trace, so illegal detention has nowhere to hide.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **D.K. Basu (1997)** grew out of a **letter** treated as a writ petition.
- Core: **arrest memo attested by a witness, relative informed, medical examination, entries in the diary, documents to the Magistrate.**
- It is the operational face of **Arts 21 and 22**, now embedded in the **BNSS**.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- Directly, as "which case laid down arrest guidelines?"
- Indirectly, as a scenario: an arrest with no memo and no relative informed — identify the violation.

# 🏛️ CASE 12 — S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)
*Why it matters: it ended the casual dismissal of State governments.*

### 1. Background
**Art 356** allows the President to take over a State's administration where the government of the State "cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution" — commonly called **President's Rule**.

Between 1950 and 1994 it was used **over a hundred times**, very often for plainly political reasons: to remove an opposition government, or on a Governor's bare assertion that a Chief Minister had lost his majority. Courts had generally treated the matter as a **political question** beyond review.

**S.R. Bommai** was the Chief Minister of **Karnataka**. In 1989 his government was dismissed after the Governor reported that he had lost majority support, relying on letters from legislators withdrawing support. Bommai **offered to prove his majority on the floor of the House. The Governor did not allow it** and recommended President's Rule.

Several similar dismissals — including those of State governments following the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992 — were heard together by a **nine-judge bench**.

### 2. What the Court decided — the four holdings
**(a) A proclamation under Art 356 is JUSTICIABLE.** Courts can examine it. It is not immune as a political question.

**(b) The floor test is the test of majority.** Whether a government commands a majority must be decided by **voting in the Assembly**, not by the Governor's **subjective satisfaction** or by counting letters. *"The Assembly is the only forum that can determine the majority."*

**(c) Courts can review the material and strike down a mala fide proclamation.** The Court cannot examine the *correctness* of the President's satisfaction, but it can ask whether there was **relevant material** at all, and whether the action was **mala fide** or based on wholly extraneous considerations.

**(d) Courts can RESTORE a dismissed government.** If the proclamation is invalid, the Court may revive the dissolved Assembly and reinstate the Ministry.

**(e) Secularism is part of the basic structure.** A State government acting against secular principles can be a valid ground for action under Art 356.

### 3. One practical consequence
The Court added that the **Assembly should not be dissolved until Parliament approves** the proclamation. Otherwise, by the time a court finds the proclamation bad, the Assembly is already gone and the remedy is worthless.

### 4. Simple analogy
A club president is removed because the patron writes a note saying "he has lost the members' support." The president says, "call a meeting and let us vote." The patron refuses and removes him anyway. Bommai says: **support is proved by counting hands in the room, not by reading letters in the patron's office** — and if the removal was wrong, the president goes back in.

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Nine judges, 1994. 356 is justiciable; floor test; restoration possible; secularism is basic structure.**
- The **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and **Punchhi Commission (2010)** had recommended 356 be a last resort; **Bommai turned that advice into enforceable law.**
- Use of Art 356 fell sharply after 1994.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- Scenario: a Governor recommends 356 without a floor test → the proclamation is **challengeable**.
- Trap: "a proclamation under Art 356 is a political question and not reviewable" — correct **before 1994**, wrong now. This is a **period transplant** distractor.

# 🏛️ CASE 13 — D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987)
*Why it matters: it stopped governments from governing permanently by ordinance.*

### 1. Background — what an ordinance is
When the legislature is **not in session** and something urgent arises, the **President (Art 123)** or a **Governor (Art 213)** may issue an **ordinance**. It has the same force as a law.

But it is meant to be **temporary**. It must be laid before the legislature when it reassembles, and it **ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly** unless the legislature passes it as an Act.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. **Dr D.C. Wadhwa**, a professor of economics, studied ordinances issued in **Bihar**.
2. He found an extraordinary pattern: between **1967 and 1981, Bihar had promulgated 256 ordinances, and kept them alive by re-promulgating them again and again** — some for as long as **fourteen years**.
3. The mechanism was simple: as the six-week period approached, the government would let the ordinance lapse and immediately issue an identical new one, without ever placing it before the legislature for proper enactment.
4. He challenged the practice.

### 3. What the Court decided
- **Re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature is a FRAUD on the Constitution** and a **subversion of the democratic process**.
- The ordinance power is an **emergency power** to meet an urgent situation — **not a parallel source of legislation** to be used because the executive finds the legislature inconvenient.
- The Court accepted that re-promulgation might be justified in a rare case where the legislature simply had too much business to take up the matter — but a **systematic practice** of the Bihar kind is unconstitutional.

### 4. The follow-up — Krishna Kumar Singh (2017)
A seven-judge bench went further:
- **Laying the ordinance before the legislature is a mandatory constitutional obligation**, not a formality. Failure to do so is an **abuse**.
- **Re-promulgation is constitutionally impermissible**, representing an "**effort to overreach the legislative body**".

### 5. Simple analogy
A school rule allows the principal to issue a **temporary notice** when the managing committee is not meeting, valid until the committee's next sitting. A principal who lets each notice expire and instantly reissues the same notice — for fourteen years, never once putting it to the committee — has not used an emergency power. He has **replaced the committee with himself**.

### 6. 📌 Key takeaway
- **D.C. Wadhwa 1987: re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution.** The Bihar figure — **256 ordinances**, some alive 14 years — is the memorable fact.
- An ordinance **ceases six weeks after reassembly**.
- **Krishna Kumar Singh 2017:** failure to lay it before the House is itself an abuse.

### 7. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Re-promulgation of ordinances was held unconstitutional in which case?" → D.C. Wadhwa, 1987.
- Trap: an option saying an ordinance lasts "until the next session" — it lasts **six weeks from reassembly**.

# 🏛️ CASE 14 — Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006)
*Why it matters: an Andhra Pradesh case, which makes it doubly likely in an APSLPRB paper.*

### 1. Background — what clemency means
- **Art 72** gives the **President** the power to **pardon, commute, remit, respite or reprieve** any sentence. It covers **court martial** cases and **death sentences**.
- **Art 161** gives a **Governor** similar powers for offences against State laws — but it **cannot cover a court martial, and cannot pardon a death sentence**.

For a long time it was argued that these powers were **entirely beyond the reach of courts**, being acts of grace.

### 2. What happened, step by step
1. A convict in Andhra Pradesh, serving a sentence in a case involving a political killing, had the **remainder of his sentence remitted by the Governor**.
2. The order was challenged on the ground that it had been passed on **irrelevant and politically motivated grounds** — including a claim that the convict was a "good political worker", and on material that was factually wrong.
3. The question: **can a court examine a clemency order at all?**

### 3. What the Court decided
The clemency power is **subject to judicial review**, though on **limited grounds**. An order under Art 72 or 161 can be set aside if it is shown to have been passed:

- **without application of mind**;
- **mala fide** — in bad faith;
- on **wholly irrelevant or extraneous considerations**;
- by **suppressing or ignoring material facts**.

The Court said clearly: the power is **not absolute or unfettered**, and **rule of law is the basis of judicial review** of such orders. The Governor's order in the case was **set aside**.

The Court also confirmed that the power is exercised on the **advice of the Council of Ministers**, not by the Governor personally at his own discretion.

### 4. Simple analogy
A principal has the power to waive a student's punishment. That power is real and wide. But if she waives it because the student's relative is a friend, or because someone handed her a false report about the incident, the waiver can be set aside — **not because she lacked the power, but because she used it for the wrong reason, or on wrong facts.**

### 5. 📌 Key takeaway
- **Clemency is reviewable on limited grounds** — mala fide, irrelevant considerations, non-application of mind, suppression of facts.
- **Art 72 (President): can pardon a death sentence and covers court martial. Art 161 (Governor): can do NEITHER.**
- Exercised on **ministerial advice**, not personal discretion.
- **This is an AP case** — expect it in State papers.

### 6. 🎯 How it is asked
- "Is the pardoning power justiciable?" → Yes, on limited grounds (Epuru Sudhakar, 2006).
- The constant Art 72 vs Art 161 comparison.

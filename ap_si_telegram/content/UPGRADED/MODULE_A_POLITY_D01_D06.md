# 📚 DAY 1 — RIGHT TO EQUALITY (Arts 14–18)

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** Article 14 carries two borrowed ideas welded into one sentence.

- **"Equality before law"** — from Britain (Dicey). A **negative** idea: *nobody is above the law*. No special privilege for rank or wealth.
- **"Equal protection of the laws"** — from the US 14th Amendment. A **positive** idea: *the law must treat equals equally* — which means unequals may lawfully be treated **differently**.

Those two pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the whole topic. If everyone must be treated identically, no tax slab, no age limit and no reservation could survive. So the Constitution permits difference — but only **principled** difference.

**Advanced.** The courts built this in three historical layers, and knowing the layers is what lets you answer an unseen question:

**Layer 1 — The classification test (1950s–60s).** A law may classify people if it passes **both** limbs:
- **Intelligible differentia** — a real, identifiable difference between those inside the group and those left out.
- **Rational nexus** — that difference must connect to the **object the law is trying to achieve**.

Both are compulsory. A law can clear the first and still die on the second: "only candidates born on a Monday may apply" has a perfectly intelligible differentia (birth day is knowable) but **no nexus** to a recruitment purpose.

**Layer 2 — The arbitrariness test (Royappa 1974, Maneka 1978).** Justice Bhagwati added a second, independent blade: **arbitrariness is by itself a violation of Art 14**, even where the classification looks clean. So a fair-looking rule **applied arbitrarily** still breaches Art 14. This is why "the rule was valid, so the order is valid" is always a wrong option.

**Layer 3 — Manifest arbitrariness (Shayara Bano 2017).** Extended the arbitrariness blade to strike down **legislation** itself, not merely executive action.

⚠️ **Correction to original:** the pack defines "State" under Art 12 as including "courts". The judiciary is State only in its **administrative and rule-making** capacity. **Judicial orders cannot be attacked as FR violations** (*Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar*, 1967; *A.R. Antulay*, 1988).

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class legislation | Singling out one person or group for favour or burden with no real reason | **Forbidden** — the thing Art 14 actually bans |
| Reasonable classification | Grouping people on a genuine difference connected to the law's purpose | **Permitted** — needs both limbs |
| Intelligible differentia | The difference is real and identifiable | Limb 1 |
| Rational nexus | The difference actually serves the law's object | Limb 2 — the limb candidates forget |
| Manifest arbitrariness | A law made capriciously, with no determining principle | Shayara Bano 2017 — applies to **laws**, not just orders |
| Horizontal right | A right enforceable against **private persons**, not just the State | Arts 15(2), 17, 23, 24 |
| Creamy layer | The advanced section within a backward class, excluded from quota | Indra Sawhney 1992 |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Art 14 — who it protects.** "Any **person**" — citizens, foreigners, and **juristic persons** (companies, societies) in appropriate cases. Contrast Arts 15 and 16, which say "**citizen**". This one word decides a whole family of questions.

**Art 15 — exactly five grounds: Religion, Race, Caste, Sex, Place of birth (RRCSP).** Descent and residence are **not** here — they appear only in Art 16.

Sub-clauses worth precision:
- **15(1)** — binds the **State** only.
- **15(2)** — ⚠️ **two limbs, and the original pack merges them.**
  - **15(2)(a)** shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment — **no funding condition**, applies to **private** establishments. **Horizontal.**
  - **15(2)(b)** wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads, places of public resort — **only if wholly or partly maintained out of State funds** or dedicated to the public.
- **15(3)** special provision for **women and children** — an enabling power, not an exception to be justified.
- **15(4)** SEBC/SC/ST advancement (1st Amendment, 1951, after *Champakam Dorairajan*).
- **15(5)** admissions in private unaided institutions (93rd, 2005) — **expressly excludes minority institutions** protected by Art 30.
- **15(6)** EWS, up to 10% (103rd, 2019).

**Art 16 — public employment, citizens only. Seven grounds** — the five of Art 15 **plus descent and residence**.
- **16(3)** — a **residence requirement** for a State post can be prescribed by **Parliament alone**. A State legislature cannot do it.
- **16(4)** reservation for backward classes; **16(4A)** reservation in **promotion** with consequential seniority for SC/ST (77th and 85th Amendments); **16(4B)** carry-forward of backlog vacancies (81st); **16(6)** EWS.

**Indra Sawhney (Mandal), 1992, 9 judges** — four holdings:
1. 16(4) is **not an exception** to 16(1); it is a **facet of real equality**. Any option calling reservation an "exception" is false.
2. **50% ceiling** on backward-class reservation, breachable only in extraordinary situations.
3. **Creamy layer** must be excluded.
4. Reservation in **promotion** was disallowed — later restored by the **77th Amendment**.

⚠️ **Correction — the 50% ceiling has moved.** **Janhit Abhiyan (2022)** upheld the 103rd Amendment and held the 50% ceiling is **not part of the basic structure**, with the **EWS 10% sitting outside** the Indra Sawhney limit. So "reservation can never cross 50%" is now **wrong**.

**Art 17 — absolute.** Untouchability abolished; practice in any form is an **offence**. **No reasonable restriction, no exception, no emergency suspension in practice.** It binds **private individuals** — horizontal. Enforced through the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** and the **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**. The Constitution deliberately **does not define** "untouchability".

**Art 18 — titles.**
- The State may not confer **titles**, except **military and academic** distinctions.
- A **citizen** may not accept a foreign title at all under 18(2); a **non-citizen holding office of profit under the State** needs the **President's** consent (18(3)); and 18(4) covers presents and offices from foreign States.
- **Balaji Raghavan, 1996** — Bharat Ratna and the Padma awards are **awards, not titles**, so they survive Art 18 — but they must **not be used as a prefix or suffix** to a name.

**Exceptions to Art 14** — the five that get asked:
- **Art 31C** — laws giving effect to **Art 39(b)–(c)** are shielded from Arts 14 and 19. ⚠️ Post-**Property Owners Association (Nov 2024, 9 judges)**: the **unamended 31C survives**, and 39(b) is **narrower** — not every private resource is a "material resource of the community".
- **Art 33** — Parliament may restrict FRs for the **armed forces and police**.
- **Art 105 / 194** — parliamentary and legislative privileges.
- **Art 359** — enforcement of certain FRs suspended during an emergency (**never 20 and 21**).
- **Art 361** — the President and Governors answer to **no court** for the exercise of their office, and face **no criminal proceedings** while in office.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI is told by a local leader to register a case against one shopkeeper for an offence that thirty shopkeepers on the same street are committing, because that one shopkeeper belongs to a rival group.

- The **law** is valid. The **classification** is fine — there is no classification at all, only selection.
- But **selective enforcement with no determining principle is arbitrary**, and under *Royappa* and *Maneka*, **arbitrariness alone violates Art 14**.
- The order is challengeable by writ under **Art 226** before the AP High Court at Amaravati, and the SI who executes it has no Art 361-style immunity — only the President and Governor have that.

**The transferable lesson:** in policing, Art 14 almost never fails at the stage of "is the law valid". It fails at "**why this person, and not that one**". If you cannot state the principle behind your selection, the selection is arbitrary.

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

**Art 14** → Art 12 (who is "State") → Art 13 (laws inconsistent with FRs are void) → Art 21 (Maneka fused 14, 19 and 21 into the "golden triangle") → Art 32/226 (remedy) → Art 31C, 33, 361 (exceptions) → Art 16(4) and the reservation line → **Art 371D** (AP's own local-cadre employment regime) → Police Law: selective prosecution and BNSS discretion.

## 6️⃣ 📝 ADVANCED QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

Consider the following statements regarding Article 15(2):

1. It prohibits denial of access to shops and hotels only where they are maintained wholly or partly out of State funds.
2. It is one of the few Fundamental Rights enforceable against private individuals.
3. It covers wells, tanks and bathing ghats dedicated to the use of the general public.
4. Its grounds are identical in number to those under Article 16(2).

Which of the above statements are correct?

A) 1 and 3 only
B) 2 and 3 only
C) 2, 3 and 4 only
D) 1, 2 and 4 only

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** Statement 2 is correct — 15(2) is **horizontal**, binding private persons, which is unusual for a Fundamental Right. Statement 3 is correct — 15(2)(b) covers wells, tanks and bathing ghats either maintained out of State funds **or dedicated to the use of the general public**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 1** — the exact error in the source pack. The "State funds" condition attaches **only to limb (b)**. Shops and hotels under **15(2)(a)** carry **no** funding condition.
- **Statement 4** — a number trap. Art 15 has **five** grounds; Art 16(2) has **seven** (adds descent and residence). Not identical.
- **Option A** pairs the corrupted rule with a correct one — it is the option a candidate taught by the original material would choose.
- **Option D** rewards anyone who spotted the horizontality but kept the funding error.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The question tests whether you read 15(2) as **one rule or two**. Almost every coaching note compresses it into one. The examiner splits it again.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Split the clause by **what is being accessed**. **Things you walk into and pay at** (shop, hotel, restaurant, cinema) → **no funding condition**. **Things the community shares** (well, tank, ghat, road) → **funding or public dedication required**.

### Q2 — Assertion & Reason
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

**Assertion (A):** A State law reserving 60% of seats in State medical colleges for backward classes, EWS and SC/ST taken together is automatically unconstitutional.

**Reason (R):** Indra Sawhney (1992) fixed a 50% ceiling on reservation.

A) Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A
B) Both A and R are true, but R does not correctly explain A
C) A is false, but R is true
D) Both A and R are false

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** **R is true** — Indra Sawhney did lay down a 50% ceiling. **A is false** because the ceiling applies to **Art 16(4) backward-class reservation**; after **Janhit Abhiyan (2022)** the **EWS 10% sits outside it**, and the 50% limit was expressly held **not to be part of the basic structure**. So a combined figure crossing 50% is **not automatically** void — it has to be examined limb by limb.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** requires A to be true. The word doing the damage is "**automatically**".
- **B)** also requires A to be true.
- **D)** wrongly discards R — Indra Sawhney's ceiling is very much still law for 16(4).

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The candidate knows "50% ceiling" as a slogan and never learned **what it is a ceiling on**. The examiner inserts a **combined** figure to see whether you separate EWS from backward-class quota.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** When "50%" appears, ask **"50% of what kind of reservation?"** If EWS is in the stem, the ceiling question is already contaminated — the answer will not be a flat yes.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟡 Level 2 — Competitive

A private three-star hotel in Vijayawada refuses to serve a family, the manager stating openly that it is on the ground of their caste. The family approaches the police station.

Which of the following is the **most accurate** legal position?

A) No Fundamental Right is involved, because Art 15 binds only the State; only a civil suit lies
B) Art 15(2)(a) is violated, and the conduct also attracts penal law relating to atrocities
C) Art 17 alone applies, since refusal of service amounts to untouchability in every case
D) Art 14 is violated, as the hotel has arbitrarily classified its customers

**✅ Answer: B**

**🧠 Explanation.** A hotel is expressly named in **15(2)(a)**, which needs **no State funding** and binds **private** parties. Separately, the conduct is penal under the **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989** and the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955**, so the SI has a registrable offence, not merely a constitutional grievance.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** true of **15(1)**, false of **15(2)**. The classic vertical/horizontal confusion.
- **C)** Art 17 is about **untouchability**, a narrower historical practice. Caste-based refusal of service may amount to it, but "in every case" is an **absolute word** — and the safer, squarely applicable provision is 15(2)(a).
- **D)** Art 14 binds the **State**. A private hotel is not State; Art 14 does not reach it directly.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Testing whether "Fundamental Rights bind only the State" has been memorised as an unqualified rule. **Arts 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 are the horizontal exceptions.**

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Memory hook — **"15(2), 17, 23, 24 reach private hands."** If the wrongdoer in the stem is a private person and one of those four fits, the "only the State is bound" option is a decoy.

### Q4 — Chronological order
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

Arrange the following in correct chronological order:

1. Creamy layer exclusion laid down
2. Reservation in promotion with consequential seniority restored by amendment
3. Arbitrariness recognised as the antithesis of equality
4. EWS reservation held not to violate the basic structure

A) 3 – 1 – 2 – 4
B) 1 – 3 – 2 – 4
C) 3 – 2 – 1 – 4
D) 3 – 1 – 4 – 2

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** *E.P. Royappa* **1974** (3) → *Indra Sawhney* **1992** (1) → **77th Amendment 1995 / 85th Amendment 2001** restoring promotion quota (2) → *Janhit Abhiyan* **2022** (4).

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** places creamy layer before Royappa — a 1992 holding cannot precede a 1974 one.
- **C)** places the promotion amendment before Indra Sawhney. It is the **reverse of cause and effect**: the amendment exists *because* Indra Sawhney disallowed promotion quota.
- **D)** puts EWS (2022) before the promotion amendments (1995/2001).

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Option C reverses a cause–effect chain. Whenever an amendment and a judgment appear in the same list, ask **which one was a reaction to the other**.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Anchor on **1992 = Mandal**. Everything about creamy layer, the ceiling and promotion clusters around it: **Royappa is before, the corrective amendments are after.**

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Art 14 = person; Arts 15 and 16 = citizen.** If the stem names a foreigner or a company, Arts 15/16 are dead on arrival.
2. **15(2)(a) carries no funding condition and binds private shops and hotels.** Only 15(2)(b) needs State funds.
3. **The 50% ceiling governs 16(4), not EWS** — Janhit Abhiyan, 2022.
4. **Arbitrariness alone kills** — a valid rule, applied without principle, still breaches Art 14.

# 📚 DAY 2 — FREEDOMS AND PERSONAL LIBERTY (Arts 19–22)

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** Art 19 gives citizens **six freedoms**, and immediately lets the State restrict each one. The whole topic is the pairing: **which freedom, restricted on which grounds, by whom**.

The six under 19(1): **speech and expression (a), assembly peaceably and without arms (b), association (c), free movement throughout India (d), residence and settlement (e), profession/occupation/trade/business (g)**.

Clause **(f), the right to property, was DELETED by the 44th Amendment, 1978**. Property is now only a **constitutional legal right** under **Art 300A** — no longer a Fundamental Right, so **Art 32 does not lie** for it directly.

**Advanced.** Two structural rules the pack does not state:

**Rule 1 — restrictions must be reasonable AND from the listed grounds.** A restriction on speech can only rest on the **eight heads in 19(2)**: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence. **"National interest" and "law and order" are NOT in that list.** *Public order* is narrower than *law and order* and wider than *security of the State* — three concentric circles, and examiners live on that distinction.

**Rule 2 — Art 19 is available only to citizens, and only while the State is not in a war-time emergency** (Art 358).

**Arts 20, 21 and 22 protect every person, citizen or not.**

**Art 20** — three protections:
- **20(1) no ex post facto criminal law** — no conviction under a law not in force when the act was done, and no penalty **greater** than the one then prescribed. Applies to **criminal** law only; **civil and tax** laws may be retrospective. It bars retrospective *conviction and penalty*, not retrospective *procedure*.
- **20(2) no double jeopardy** — only against a second **prosecution and punishment before a court or judicial tribunal** for the same offence. **Departmental proceedings alongside a criminal trial are permitted.**
- **20(3) no self-incrimination** — for an **accused**, against **testimonial compulsion**. It does **not** cover fingerprints, handwriting specimens, blood samples or DNA (*State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad*).

**Art 21** — the arc: *A.K. Gopalan* (1950, procedure need only exist) → **Maneka Gandhi (1978)**, procedure must be **fair, just and reasonable** → *Puttaswamy* (2017), **privacy is a Fundamental Right** under Art 21.

**Art 21A** — free and compulsory education for **6–14** (86th Amendment, 2002).

**Art 22** — read it as **two completely separate tracks**:

| | Punitive detention (arrest) | Preventive detention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clauses | 22(1), 22(2) | 22(4)–22(7) |
| Grounds of arrest | Must be informed | Must be communicated (22(5)) |
| Right to counsel | Yes | **No** right to a lawyer before the Board |
| Magistrate in 24 hrs | **Yes**, journey time excluded | **No** |
| Who is excluded | **Enemy aliens** and **persons under preventive detention** | — |

⚠️ **Correction.** The 44th Amendment reduced the Advisory Board reference period to **2 months** and required a **sitting** High Court judge to chair it — but **that amendment was never brought into force**. The operative rule is still the original **3 months**, with a Board chaired by someone **qualified to be** an HC judge.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ex post facto law | A law made after the act, applied backwards | 20(1) — **criminal** only |
| Double jeopardy | Tried and punished twice for the same offence | 20(2) — needs **prosecution AND punishment** |
| Testimonial compulsion | Being forced to *speak or produce* evidence against yourself | 20(3) — excludes physical samples |
| Preventive detention | Detention to **stop** a future act, without trial | No charge, no counsel, no magistrate |
| Public order | Disturbance affecting the community at large | Narrower than "law and order", wider than "security of State" |
| Procedure established by law | The literal text of Art 21 | Read post-Maneka as **due process** in substance |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Public order — the three circles.** Picture three concentric circles. The **widest is law and order** (any breach of peace — a street quarrel). **Inside it is public order** (a disturbance that affects the community — a riot). **Innermost is security of the State** (a threat to the State's existence). A restriction justified for "law and order" alone **cannot** be saved under 19(2), because *law and order* is **not** one of the eight heads. This single distinction decides preventive-detention questions too: a detention order for a mere law-and-order problem is bad; it must reach **public order**.

**Art 21 in policing.** Post-Maneka, the procedure that deprives liberty must itself be fair. **D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997)** turned that into concrete arrest guidelines — memo of arrest, information to a relative, medical examination, right to meet a lawyer during interrogation — now substantially codified in the **BNSS**.

**Art 22 constitutional minimum vs BNSS.** The 24-hour production rule is constitutional (22(2)) **and** statutory (BNSS). **Journey time is excluded** from the 24 hours. Preventive detention runs on a **parallel track outside BNSS** — under laws such as the **National Security Act** or a State preventive-detention law. An SI must know that producing a preventive detenu before a magistrate in 24 hours is **not** the applicable requirement; the Advisory Board mechanism is.

**Art 358 vs 359** (previewed here, drilled on Day 5): **358 suspends Art 19 automatically, but only in a war or external-aggression emergency. 359 suspends the right to move courts, requires a Presidential order, and can never touch Arts 20 and 21.**

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

A procession is planned in a town where a communal incident occurred last week. The SI proposes a blanket, indefinite ban on **all** processions in the town.

- The right engaged is **19(1)(b)** — assembly peaceably and without arms.
- A restriction is permissible under **19(3)** on grounds of **sovereignty and integrity** or **public order**.
- But it must be **reasonable**: a ban that is **blanket, indefinite and without a review mechanism** fails the reasonableness test, and after *Maneka* also fails Art 21's fair-procedure standard.
- The legally sound course is a **narrow, time-bound, route-specific** order under the relevant BNSS public-order provision, recording reasons.

**The transferable lesson:** in public-order policing, the constitutional question is never "may I restrict?" — it is "**is this restriction the narrowest one that works, and did I record why?**"

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

Art 19 → Art 19(2) heads → **BNS** offences on defamation, incitement, public tranquillity → Art 21 + **D.K. Basu** + **BNSS** arrest procedure → Art 22 two tracks → preventive detention statutes → Art 32/226 **habeas corpus** → Art 358/359 in emergency → Art 300A (property, after the 44th).

## 6️⃣ 📝 ADVANCED QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

Consider the following statements regarding Article 20:

1. It protects only citizens of India.
2. A person may face a departmental inquiry and a criminal trial for the same act without violating the protection against double jeopardy.
3. Compelling an accused to give a specimen handwriting violates the protection against self-incrimination.
4. A tax law may be given retrospective effect without violating Article 20(1).

Which of the above are correct?

A) 2 and 4 only
B) 1, 2 and 3 only
C) 2, 3 and 4 only
D) 1 and 4 only

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** Statement 2 is correct — 20(2) bars a second **prosecution and punishment before a court**; a departmental proceeding is neither. Statement 4 is correct — 20(1) restrains **criminal** law only; **civil and tax** legislation may operate retrospectively.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 1** — Art 20 protects "**no person**", so it covers foreigners too. Only **Art 19** is citizen-only in this block.
- **Statement 3** — *Kathi Kalu Oghad*: handwriting specimens, fingerprints, and blood or DNA samples are **not testimonial compulsion**. 20(3) protects against being made to *testify*, not against being physically measured.
- **Option B** is the trap for a candidate who assumes the whole of Part III splits neatly into citizen-only rights.
- **Option C** is for the candidate who gets the tax point but keeps the handwriting misconception.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Statement 3 is the misconception the question is really hunting. It matters operationally: a police officer **can** lawfully take specimen handwriting and samples from an accused.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **20(3) covers the mouth, not the body.** Anything you are made to *say or produce as a statement* is protected; anything measured *off your body* is not.

### Q2 — Statement I / Statement II
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

**Statement I:** In preventive detention, the case of a detenu must be placed before an Advisory Board within two months, and the Board must be chaired by a sitting judge of a High Court.

**Statement II:** The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 made provision to this effect.

A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct, and II explains I
B) Both are correct, but II does not explain I
C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
D) Both statements are incorrect

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** **Statement II is correct** — the 44th Amendment did contain exactly that provision. **Statement I is incorrect as a statement of law in force**, because that provision of the 44th Amendment was **never brought into force** — no commencement notification was issued. The operative rule remains the **original 3 months**, with a Board chaired by a person **qualified to be** a High Court judge.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** and **B)** both treat Statement I as current law — the error the whole question exists to catch.
- **D)** wrongly denies the 44th Amendment's text. The amendment **says** it; it just never commenced.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The gap between "**an amendment was passed**" and "**an amendment is in force**". This is the only well-known provision of a Constitutional Amendment in India that remains unnotified, which is precisely why it is asked.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** On preventive detention, **the number is 3 months**. If you see **2 months**, look for the word "**in force**" or "**at present**" in the stem — the question is testing the unnotified 44th Amendment.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A person is arrested at 8:00 p.m. on Monday at a place 300 km from the nearest competent magistrate. He is produced before the magistrate at 11:00 a.m. on Wednesday. The journey itself took eleven hours.

Which of the following is correct?

A) Art 22(2) is violated, since more than 24 hours elapsed between arrest and production
B) Art 22(2) is not violated, because journey time is excluded and the remaining period is within 24 hours
C) Art 22(2) is violated, because journey time is excluded only for preventive detention
D) Art 22(2) does not apply, as the 24-hour rule is only a BNSS requirement and not constitutional

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** Total elapsed time is **39 hours**. Excluding the **11 hours** of journey leaves **28 hours** — still beyond 24. So even with the exclusion, **22(2) is breached**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** the reasoning is right but the arithmetic is not done. This is the option for a candidate who recognises the rule and stops thinking.
- **C)** inverts the law twice over: journey exclusion applies to **punitive** arrest, and **22(2) does not apply to preventive detention at all**.
- **D)** false — the 24-hour rule is **both** constitutional (22(2)) and statutory (BNSS).

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The question rewards **doing the subtraction**. Most candidates see "journey excluded", tick B, and lose the mark. The examiner deliberately chose a journey short enough that the exclusion does not save the officer.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Whenever a 22(2) stem gives **numbers**, subtract before you choose. **Elapsed minus journey — is it over 24?**

### Q4 — Match the following
**Difficulty:** 🟡 Level 2 — Competitive

| List I (Provision) | List II (Effect) |
| --- | --- |
| A. Art 19(1)(f) | 1. Right to privacy read into personal liberty |
| B. Art 300A | 2. Omitted by the 44th Amendment |
| C. Art 21A | 3. Constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right |
| D. Art 21 | 4. Inserted by the 86th Amendment |

A) A-2, B-3, C-4, D-1
B) A-3, B-2, C-1, D-4
C) A-2, B-3, C-1, D-4
D) A-4, B-3, C-2, D-1

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** 19(1)(f) was **omitted** by the 44th (1978); **Art 300A** is the successor and is a **constitutional but not fundamental** right; **Art 21A** came from the **86th** (2002); **privacy** was read into **Art 21** in *Puttaswamy* (2017).

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** swaps A and B — the commonest slip, since both concern property.
- **C)** swaps C and D, attaching privacy to 21A. 21A is **education**, not privacy.
- **D)** attaches the 86th Amendment to 19(1)(f).

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Two property entries placed adjacently so that the candidate pairs "property" with "property" without reading which one was **deleted** and which was **created**.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Fix the **86th = school** anchor first; it locks C. Then **44th = property out**, which locks A. B and D follow.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Art 19 = citizens; Arts 20, 21, 22 = every person.**
2. **Preventive detention: 3 months, not 2** — the 44th Amendment's change was never notified.
3. **20(3) protects the mouth, not the body** — handwriting, fingerprints and DNA are outside it.
4. **"Law and order" is not a ground under 19(2).** Public order is. Three circles: law and order ⊃ public order ⊃ security of the State.

# 📚 DAY 3 — DPSP (Arts 36–51) AND FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES (Art 51A)

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** Part IV lists goals the State should aim at. **Art 37** makes them **non-justiciable** — you cannot go to court to enforce a Directive Principle. Borrowed from **Ireland**.

But "non-justiciable" does **not** mean "irrelevant". Art 37 itself says they are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is the **duty of the State to apply them** in making laws.

**Advanced.** The real exam content is the **FR vs DPSP war**, which ran in four rounds:

1. **Champakam Dorairajan (1951)** — FRs prevail; DPSP are subsidiary. Parliament responded with the **1st Amendment**, inserting **Art 15(4)**.
2. **Golaknath (1967)** — Parliament cannot amend FRs at all. Parliament responded with the **24th Amendment**.
3. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** — Parliament may amend FRs but cannot damage the **basic structure**. The **42nd Amendment** then tried to shield **all** DPSP laws via a widened Art 31C.
4. **Minerva Mills (1980)** — struck the widening down. The shield reverted to laws giving effect to **Art 39(b)–(c)** only, and the Court held that the **balance between FRs and DPSP is itself part of the basic structure**.

⚠️ **Update:** **Property Owners Association (Nov 2024, 9 judges)** held the **unamended Art 31C survives**, and narrowed **Art 39(b)** — **not all private property is a "material resource of the community"**.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Justiciable | Enforceable in a court | FRs yes, DPSP **no** (Art 37) |
| Directive Principle | A goal the State should pursue | Part IV, Arts 36–51 |
| Art 31C shield | Protection of certain DPSP laws from Arts 14 and 19 challenge | Cut to **39(b)–(c)** by Minerva |
| Basic structure | The core the Constitution cannot lose by amendment | Kesavananda 1973 |
| Fundamental Duty | A citizen's obligation | Art 51A — **11**, non-justiciable directly |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**The three buckets — know which Article sits where.**

**Socialist:** 38 (welfare and reducing inequalities), 39 (six principles — **39(b) material resources, 39(c) no concentration of wealth**), **39A legal aid (42nd)**, 41 (right to work, education, public assistance), 42 (just and humane conditions, maternity relief), 43 (living wage), **43A worker participation in management (42nd)**, **43B cooperatives (97th)**.

**Gandhian:** 40 (village panchayats), 43 (cottage industries), 46 (educational and economic interests of SCs, STs and weaker sections), 47 (nutrition, standard of living, public health, **prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs**), 48 (agriculture and animal husbandry; **cow slaughter**).

**Liberal-intellectual:** 44 (**Uniform Civil Code**), 45 (early childhood care and education **below 6**, as reworded by the 86th), **48A environment (42nd)**, 49 (monuments), 50 (**separation of judiciary from executive**), 51 (international peace).

**The 86th Amendment split — asked every cycle.** Before 2002, **Art 45** covered free and compulsory education for children **up to 14**. The **86th** moved the **6–14** part **up into Part III as Art 21A (a Fundamental Right)**, left **Art 45** covering **below 6**, and added **Art 51A(k)**, the parent's duty. So one amendment touched **Part III, Part IV and Part IVA simultaneously** — which is exactly why it is a favourite.

**Fundamental Duties (Art 51A).** Added by the **42nd (1976)** on the **Swaran Singh Committee's** recommendation — **10** duties. The **86th (2002)** added the **11th**, 51A(k). They are **not directly enforceable by writ**, but they are enforced **through statutes** (Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, Wildlife Protection Act, Forest Conservation Act) and are used by courts as an **interpretive aid**. The **Verma Committee (1999)** identified the existing legal mechanisms for their operationalisation.

**Art 50 in the police world.** "Separation of the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State" is the constitutional root of splitting magistracy into **Judicial Magistrates** (under the High Court) and **Executive Magistrates** (under the State government) — a division an SI works within daily: remand goes to the **Judicial** Magistrate, while preventive and public-order orders come from the **Executive** Magistrate.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI produces an indigent labourer before a court; the man has no lawyer and cannot afford one.

- **Art 39A** (DPSP) directs the State to provide **free legal aid**.
- Standing alone, 39A is **non-justiciable**. But *Hussainara Khatoon* and *Khatri* read it **together with Art 21** — and free legal aid to an indigent accused thereby became an **enforceable component of Art 21**.
- It is now operational through the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (NALSA, the State Legal Services Authority, and the District Legal Services Authority) and through BNSS provisions on legal aid.

**The transferable lesson — the single most important DPSP mechanism:** a Directive Principle is unenforceable **alone**, but becomes enforceable **when a court reads it into a Fundamental Right**. Art 39A + Art 21 = enforceable. Art 47 + Art 21 = enforceable right to health. Art 48A + Art 21 = enforceable right to a clean environment.

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

Art 37 (non-justiciable) → the **FR vs DPSP** chain: Champakam → 1st → Golaknath → 24th → Kesavananda → 42nd → Minerva → **Property Owners Association 2024** → Art 31C → Arts 39(b)–(c) → Art 21 as the enforcement vehicle (39A, 47, 48A) → Art 50 and the magistracy → Art 51A and the statutes that give duties teeth.

# 📚 DAY 4 — PREAMBLE, CITIZENSHIP (Arts 5–11), ARTS 12–13

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** The Preamble states **who made the Constitution, what kind of State India is, and what it promises**.

- **Source of authority:** "We, the People of India" — the Constitution is **not** a grant from the British Parliament.
- **Nature of the State:** Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic. **Socialist, Secular and Integrity were added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976.**
- **Objectives:** **Justice** (social, economic, political — from the Russian Revolution), **Liberty** (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship — from the French Revolution), **Equality** (of status and of opportunity), **Fraternity** (assuring the **dignity of the individual** and the **unity and integrity of the Nation**).
- **Date: 26 November 1949** — the date of adoption, which appears in the Preamble itself. Commencement was **26 January 1950**.

**Advanced — the Preamble's legal status moved:**
- ***Berubari Union* (1960):** the Preamble is **NOT part** of the Constitution.
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973):** the Preamble **IS part** of the Constitution and **can be amended**, but not so as to destroy the **basic structure**.
- The **42nd Amendment (1976)** is the **only** amendment to the Preamble so far.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Republic | The head of State is elected, not hereditary | Distinguishes India from a constitutional monarchy |
| Sovereign | No external authority over India | Preamble |
| Single citizenship | One Indian citizenship; no separate State citizenship | Contrast the USA |
| Domicile | Permanent home, with intention to remain | Art 5 |
| Doctrine of eclipse | A pre-1950 law inconsistent with an FR is dormant, not dead | Art 13(1) |
| Doctrine of severability | Only the offending part of a law is struck down | Art 13 |
| Instrumentality of the State | A body so controlled by government that it counts as "State" | Art 12 |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Citizenship at commencement (Arts 5–11) — the dates are the question.**

- **Art 5** — domiciled in India at commencement **and** (born in India, **or** either parent born in India, **or** ordinarily resident for **5 years** immediately before commencement).
- **Art 6** — migrated **from Pakistan to India**. Before **19 July 1948** → citizen if ordinarily resident since. On or after 19 July 1948 → needs **registration** plus 6 months' residence.
- **Art 7** — migrated **to Pakistan after 1 March 1947** and then **returned** under a **permit for resettlement**. This clause **overrides** Arts 5 and 6.
- **Art 8** — a person of Indian origin **residing outside India**, by **registration** with an Indian diplomatic mission.
- **Art 9** — voluntarily acquiring the citizenship of a foreign State **ends** Indian citizenship. This is why **OCI is not dual citizenship**.
- **Art 10** — continuance, subject to any law of Parliament.
- **Art 11** — **Parliament's** plenary power over citizenship, exercised through the **Citizenship Act, 1955**.

**Direction is the trap.** Art 6 is **Pakistan → India**. Art 7 is **India → Pakistan → back to India**. Candidates reverse them constantly.

**CAA 2019** — flows from Art 11. Cut-off **31 December 2014**; covers **Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian** migrants from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan**; excludes **Sixth Schedule** tribal areas and **Inner Line Permit** States. Rules notified **11 March 2024**.

**Art 12 — "the State" means** the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and Legislature of each State, and **all local or other authorities** within India or under the control of the Government of India.
- **Local authorities:** panchayats, municipalities, district boards.
- **Other authorities:** the contested category. The test evolved from a narrow statutory-function view to the **"instrumentality or agency" test** (*R.D. Shetty*, *Ajay Hasia* — deep financial control, monopoly status, governmental functions, State control over management).
- ***Zee Telefilms* (2005)** — **BCCI is not "State"** under Art 12, but it **is** amenable to writ jurisdiction under **Art 226** for its **public functions**. Both halves must be stated; giving only one produces a wrong answer.
- ⚠️ **The judiciary** is State in its **administrative and rule-making** capacity, but its **judicial orders** are not challengeable as FR violations.

**Art 13 — the enforcement engine.**
- **13(1)** pre-Constitution laws are void **to the extent of inconsistency** → **doctrine of eclipse** (the law is dormant, and revives if the inconsistency is removed) and **severability**.
- **13(2)** the State shall make no law taking away or abridging FRs.
- **13(3)** "law" includes ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, **custom and usage**.
- **13(4)** — inserted by the **24th Amendment, 1971** — a **constitutional amendment under Art 368 is NOT "law" for Art 13**, reversing *Golaknath*. **But** Kesavananda then held such an amendment is still void if it damages the **basic structure**. So the check moved from Art 13 to the basic structure doctrine.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

An SI is asked whether a complaint can be registered against a **private security agency** for allegedly violating a person's Fundamental Rights during a detention at a private mall.

- A private agency is **not "State"** under Art 12, so **Art 32 against it fails** and most FRs do not bind it directly.
- But that does **not** leave the complainant without remedy. **Wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement are offences under the BNS**, whoever commits them — **the criminal law binds private persons even where Part III does not.**
- And **Arts 15(2), 17, 23 and 24** do operate horizontally if the facts fit.

**The transferable lesson:** "not State under Art 12" answers only the **constitutional writ** question. It never answers the **criminal liability** question. An SI who conflates the two refuses a registrable case.

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

Preamble → basic structure (Kesavananda) → Art 368 → Art 13(4) and the 24th Amendment → Art 12 (who can be sued) → Art 32/226 → citizenship Arts 5–11 → Citizenship Act 1955 and CAA 2019 → Art 9 and the OCI misconception → single citizenship and federalism.

1. **Berubari said no, Kesavananda said yes** — and the Preamble has been amended **exactly once** (42nd).
2. **Art 6 is one-way into India; Art 7 is out-and-back with a permit.**
3. **Art 9 kills dual citizenship** — OCI is a status, not a second citizenship.
4. **"Not State under Art 12" blocks Art 32, not Art 226, and never blocks the criminal law.**

# 📚 DAY 5 — EMERGENCY PROVISIONS (Arts 352, 356, 360)

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** The Constitution has three emergencies, and the fastest way to keep them apart is by **what has gone wrong**:

- **Art 352 — National Emergency.** The **security of India** is threatened.
- **Art 356 — President's Rule.** A **State government** cannot function constitutionally.
- **Art 360 — Financial Emergency.** The **financial stability or credit** of India is threatened.

**Advanced.** Precision on Art 352:

- **Grounds:** war, external aggression, or **armed rebellion**. ⚠️ The **44th Amendment (1978)** replaced "**internal disturbance**" with "**armed rebellion**", raising the threshold after the 1975 experience.
- **Procedure safeguards added by the 44th:** the President may proclaim only on the **written recommendation of the Cabinet** (not the Prime Minister alone).
- **Approval:** by **both Houses within 1 month**, by a **special majority** — a majority of the **total membership** of the House **and** two-thirds of members **present and voting**.
- **Duration:** **6 months** at a time, extendable indefinitely with fresh approval every 6 months.
- **Revocation:** by the President at any time; and — the 44th's key democratic safeguard — if **one-tenth of the members of the Lok Sabha** give notice, a **special sitting** must be held, and the emergency ends if the Lok Sabha disapproves it by a **simple majority**.
- **Used three times: 1962 (China), 1971 (Pakistan), 1975 (internal disturbance).**

**Art 356:**
- **Trigger:** on the **Governor's report** "**or otherwise**" — those two words matter; the President is not confined to the Governor's report.
- **Approval:** both Houses within **2 months**, by **simple majority**.
- **Duration:** **6 months** at a time, **maximum 3 years**. Extension **beyond 1 year** requires **both**: (i) a **National Emergency in force** in the whole of India or the State, **and** (ii) the **Election Commission certifying** that elections cannot be held.
- ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** — the landmark: a proclamation is **justiciable**; the majority of a government is to be tested on the **floor of the House**, not in the Governor's subjective satisfaction; the Court can **restore** a dismissed government; **secularism is part of the basic structure**.

**Art 360:** **never invoked**. Permits the Centre to direct States on financial propriety and to reduce the salaries of **all** public servants, **including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts**.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Armed rebellion | Organised violent revolt against the State | 44th replaced "internal disturbance" |
| Special majority | Majority of total membership **plus** two-thirds present and voting | 352 approval; **356 needs only simple majority** |
| Floor test | Proving a majority by voting in the House | Bommai 1994 |
| Art 358 | **Automatic** suspension of **Art 19** | Only in **war / external aggression** |
| Art 359 | Suspension of the **right to move courts** for specified FRs | Needs a **Presidential order**; **never 20 and 21** |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**Art 358 vs Art 359 — the highest-value contrast in the topic.**

| | **Art 358** | **Art 359** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which rights | **Art 19 only** | Any FR **except Arts 20 and 21** |
| How it operates | **Automatic** on proclamation | Requires a **separate Presidential order** |
| When available | Only on **war or external aggression** | **Any** ground, including armed rebellion |
| What is suspended | The **right itself** | Only the **right to move a court** to enforce it |
| Geographic reach | Whole country | May be confined to **part** of India |
| Post-44th limit | Covers only laws/actions **connected with the emergency** | Same limitation |

Two things the 44th Amendment fixed, both reacting to **ADM Jabalpur (1976)**, in which the Court had held that even the right to life could not be enforced during an emergency: **Arts 20 and 21 can never be suspended**, and **Art 358 is confined to war and external aggression**. *ADM Jabalpur* was expressly **overruled in Puttaswamy (2017)**.

**Art 356 and federalism.** The **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and later the **Punchhi Commission (2010)** recommended that 356 be used only as a last resort and that all alternatives be exhausted. *Bommai* converted that advice into judicially enforceable discipline. Post-1994, the frequency of 356 fell sharply.

**AP connection.** Andhra Pradesh was placed under **President's Rule in January 1973** during the **Jai Andhra agitation** — the same crisis that produced the **Six-Point Formula (1973)** and hence **Article 371D** (see Module C). This is the cleanest available link between the Emergency chapter, AP political history and AP's special constitutional provision, and it is missing from the original pack entirely.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

During a proclaimed National Emergency on the ground of **armed rebellion**, an SI is told that "all Fundamental Rights stand suspended, so no court will interfere".

- **False.** Art **358** does not even operate here — it applies only to **war or external aggression**, and this emergency rests on **armed rebellion**.
- Suspension of the right to move courts for other rights needs a **separate Presidential order under Art 359**.
- **Arts 20 and 21 can never be suspended.** So **habeas corpus for an illegal detention remains maintainable**, and the 24-hour production rule and the protection against self-incrimination continue to bind the officer.

**The transferable lesson:** "emergency" is not a blanket authorisation. An officer must ask **which Article was proclaimed, on which ground, and whether a 359 order exists** — and must know that **Arts 20 and 21 survive all three answers**.

## 5️⃣ 🔗 Concept Connections

Art 352 → Arts 358/359 → Arts 20, 21 and the 44th Amendment → ADM Jabalpur → Puttaswamy → Art 356 → Bommai → Sarkaria and Punchhi → Centre–State relations → Governor's report and Art 200 discretion (Day 6) → **AP 1973, Six-Point Formula, Art 371D** → Art 360 and the Finance Commission.

### Q1 — Multi-statement

2. Article 19 stands automatically suspended on any proclamation under Article 352.

B) 1, 2 and 3 only

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** 1, 3 and 4 are each exactly right — one month, special majority; the one-tenth special-sitting device introduced by the 44th; and the absolute shield on Arts 20 and 21.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 2** — the trap, carried by "**any**". Art 358 suspends Art 19 **automatically only where the emergency rests on war or external aggression**. On an **armed rebellion** proclamation, Art 19 is **not** automatically suspended.
- **Option D** is for a candidate who knows the 44th Amendment's safeguards but never learned that the **44th also narrowed Art 358's trigger**.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Candidates learn "352 → Art 19 goes" as a single rule. The 44th Amendment split that rule by **ground**. The examiner inserts "any proclamation" to test it.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Art 358 has an external trigger only.** If the word **"armed rebellion"** or **"any"** appears near Art 19, the statement is false.

### Q2 — Assertion & Reason
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

**Assertion (A):** President's Rule in a State was continued beyond one year on the strength of a resolution of Parliament alone.

**Reason (R):** Extension of a proclamation under Article 356 beyond one year requires a National Emergency to be in operation and a certificate from the Election Commission.

A) Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A
B) Both A and R are true, but R does not correctly explain A
C) A is false, but R is true
D) Both A and R are false

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** **R is exactly right** — extension beyond one year requires **both** conditions **cumulatively**. **A is therefore false**: a parliamentary resolution **alone** is not sufficient past the one-year mark, though it *is* sufficient for the first year.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** and **B)** both accept the Assertion. The 44th Amendment inserted these twin conditions precisely to make parliamentary approval alone insufficient.
- **D)** discards R, which states the law correctly.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The word "**alone**". Parliamentary approval remains **necessary** — it simply stops being **sufficient** after one year. Candidates who know "356 can run three years" tick A without noticing what the extra two years cost.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **356: one year on Parliament's word; years two and three need an Emergency plus the EC's certificate.**

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A Governor reports that the ruling party in the State has lost its majority, and recommends a proclamation under Article 356 without any vote being taken in the Assembly. The proclamation is issued.

Which of the following best states the legal position?

A) The proclamation is immune from judicial review, being a political question
B) The proclamation is valid, since Article 356 permits action on the Governor's report
C) The proclamation is open to challenge; the majority must ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House
D) The proclamation is invalid, because Article 356 requires a prior report from the Election Commission

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** holds that a 356 proclamation is **justiciable**, and that the **floor of the House is the appropriate forum** for testing majority — not the Governor's subjective assessment. The Court may even **restore** a dismissed government.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** the pre-Bommai position, abandoned in 1994. This is the classic **"historically correct but wrong period"** distractor.
- **B)** true as far as the **text** goes — 356 does permit action on the Governor's report — but incomplete, because Bommai overlays a **substantive check** on the material. The technically-accurate-but-legally-incomplete option.
- **D)** invents a requirement. The **EC certificate** belongs to **extension beyond one year**, not to the **initial** proclamation. A genuine provision, transplanted to the wrong stage.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Option D is the strongest distractor because the EC certificate **is** real — it is simply attached to the wrong step. Examiners routinely move a true requirement one stage up or down.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** On any 356 question, run the sequence: **Governor's report or otherwise → Parliament in 2 months → 6 months at a time → beyond 1 year needs Emergency + EC → maximum 3 years.** Then check which step the option is describing.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **352 needs a special majority in one month; 356 needs only a simple majority, in two months.** The harder emergency has the shorter clock.
2. **Art 358 fires automatically only on war or external aggression. Art 359 needs a Presidential order and can never touch Arts 20 and 21.**
3. **Bommai: floor test, justiciable, restoration possible, secularism is basic structure.**
4. **Art 360 has never been used** — including in the 1991 crisis.

# 📚 DAY 6 — THE EXECUTIVE: PRESIDENT, VICE-PRESIDENT, GOVERNOR

## 1️⃣ Core Concept

**Foundation.** India's executive is **parliamentary**: the President is the **head of State** and the real power rests with the **Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister**. **Art 74** makes the President's aid-and-advice binding, and the **42nd Amendment** made it explicitly binding while the **44th** added the power to **require reconsideration once** — after which the President **must** act on the advice.

**Advanced — the electoral college (Art 54), which is asked by exclusion.**

Who votes: the **elected** members of both Houses of Parliament, and the **elected** members of the Legislative **Assemblies** of the States **and of Delhi and Puducherry**.

Who does **not** vote:
- **Nominated** members of either House
- **Nominated** members of State Assemblies
- **All** members of State Legislative **Councils** — elected or not

The election uses **proportional representation by a single transferable vote**, with voting by **secret ballot**. Disputes go **only to the Supreme Court**, whose decision is final; and — a favourite — a **vacancy in the electoral college does not invalidate the election**.

## 2️⃣ 🔑 Technical Terms

| Term | Simple meaning | Exam relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suspensive veto | Returning a Bill once; if re-passed, assent must follow | Art 111 |
| Pocket veto | Sitting on a Bill indefinitely with no time limit | Available because **Art 111 fixes no time** |
| Ordinance | Law made when the legislature is not in session | Arts 123 / 213 |
| Pardon (Art 72) | Complete absolution, wiping out conviction and sentence | Distinguish from commute, remit, respite, reprieve |
| Reservation for the President | A Governor sends a State Bill to the President | Art 200 |
| Discretionary power | A Governor acting without ministerial advice | Art 163 — **the Governor has it; the President does not** |

## 3️⃣ 🧠 Advanced Understanding

**President vs Governor — the comparison that generates most questions.**

| | **President** | **Governor** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selected by | **Elected** (Art 54) | **Appointed** by the President (Art 155) |
| Tenure | 5 years; holds office during pleasure only in a limited sense | 5 years, but holds office **during the pleasure of the President** |
| Minimum age | **35** | **35** |
| Must be qualified for | **Lok Sabha** | **Legislative Assembly** |
| Discretion | **No** constitutional discretion (Art 74) | **Yes** — Art 163 expressly contemplates it |
| Removal | **Impeachment**, Art 61 | **No impeachment** — recall by the President |
| Pardon power | **Art 72** — includes **court martial** and **death sentence** | **Art 161** — **cannot** pardon a **court martial**, and **cannot pardon a death sentence** |
| Emergency ordinance-type power | Art 123 | Art 213 — but **needs prior instructions** from the President in specified cases |

**The pardon distinction (Arts 72 vs 161) is one of the most-asked comparisons in State PSC and police exams.** A Governor can **commute or remit** a death sentence but **cannot pardon** it; and court martial cases lie **wholly outside** Art 161.

**Veto powers under Art 111.** For a Bill passed by Parliament, the President may: give **assent**; **withhold** assent (absolute veto); or **return** it for reconsideration (**suspensive veto**) — but **only if it is not a Money Bill**, and **if the Houses pass it again, with or without amendment, the President must assent**. Because Art 111 prescribes **no time limit**, a **pocket veto** is possible in practice — famously exercised over the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986.

**Art 200 — the Governor's options on a State Bill:** assent; withhold assent; return for reconsideration (not a Money Bill); or **reserve it for the consideration of the President**. If the Bill would **derogate from the powers of the High Court**, reservation is **compulsory**. Where a Bill is reserved, **Art 201** governs, and if the President returns it and the State legislature re-passes it, the President is **still not obliged to assent** — a sharp contrast with Art 111.

⚠️ **Current position on delay — corrected.** The April 2025 judgment in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor* prescribed **timelines** and introduced **"deemed assent"**. In the **Presidential Reference opinion of 20 November 2025**, a five-judge Bench held that **courts cannot judicially prescribe timelines** under Arts 200/201 and that **"deemed assent" is impermissible**, while confirming that **prolonged unexplained inaction remains justiciable** with the remedy being a **direction to decide**. ⚠️ Re-verify this item close to the exam — it is the most volatile point in the syllabus.

**Ordinances (Arts 123 / 213).** Promulgated only when the House(s) are **not in session** and the President or Governor is satisfied that **immediate action** is necessary. An ordinance must be **laid before the legislature on reassembly** and **ceases to operate six weeks from reassembly** (or earlier, on a disapproval resolution). ***D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987)** — **re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature is a fraud on the Constitution.** *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) added that **failure to lay an ordinance before the legislature is itself an abuse**.

**The 91st Amendment, 2003 — two limbs:**
- The Council of Ministers, including the Prime Minister or Chief Minister, must not exceed **15% of the total strength** of the Lok Sabha or the Legislative Assembly. For States there is a **floor of 12 ministers**.
- A member **disqualified on defection** is barred from being appointed a minister until the term expires or he is re-elected.

**Vice-President (Arts 63–71).** Elected by an electoral college of **both Houses of Parliament only** — **State legislatures do not vote**, which is the single cleanest contrast with the President. He is **ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** and is **not a member** of it. He may be removed by a resolution of the **Rajya Sabha** passed by a majority of its members and **agreed to by the Lok Sabha** — **14 days' notice** required, and **no grounds are specified** in the Constitution.

⚠️ **Correction.** There is **no "acting Vice-President"**. If the **VP's** office falls vacant, the **Deputy Chairman** (or an RS member authorised by the President) performs the **Chairman's** functions and an election is held as soon as possible. The **VP acts as PRESIDENT** when the **President's** office falls vacant (Art 65), for a **maximum of 6 months**.

## 4️⃣ 👮 Field / Real-World Application

A convict under a death sentence confirmed by the High Court files a mercy petition addressed to the Governor. The district police are asked about the effect on the execution warrant.

- Under **Art 161**, the Governor may **commute, remit, respite or reprieve** — but **cannot grant a pardon in a death-sentence case**. The **pardon** power over a death sentence lies with the **President under Art 72**.
- The power is exercised on the **advice of the Council of Ministers**, not personally.
- It is **not wholly beyond review**: ***Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh* (2006)** — itself an **AP case** — held that an order under Art 72/161 is reviewable where it is **arbitrary, mala fide, passed without application of mind, or on wholly irrelevant considerations**.

**The transferable lesson:** executive clemency is **discretionary but not lawless**. *Epuru Sudhakar* is an AP-origin authority and therefore doubly worth remembering for an APSLPRB paper.

## 6️⃣ 📝 ADVANCED QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Multi-statement
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

Consider the following regarding the election of the President of India:

1. Nominated members of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha participate in the election.
2. Elected members of State Legislative Councils participate in the election.
3. Elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry participate in the election.
4. A vacancy in the electoral college is not a ground for challenging the election.

Which are correct?

A) 3 and 4 only
B) 1, 3 and 4 only
C) 2, 3 and 4 only
D) 1 and 2 only

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** Statement 3 is correct — the **70th Amendment (1992)** brought in the Assemblies of **Delhi and Puducherry**. Statement 4 is correct and is an express constitutional rule.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **Statement 1** — **nominated** members are excluded from the electoral college, because the President is meant to be elected by directly representative members. (Note the contrast: nominated members **do** vote in the **Vice-Presidential** election.)
- **Statement 2** — **no member of any Legislative Council votes**, elected or otherwise. The word "elected" in the statement is placed there to make the exclusion look conditional. It is **absolute**.
- **Option C** is the strongest decoy for a candidate who correctly remembers "elected members only" but applies it to the wrong House.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** The rule is **not** "elected members vote". It is "**elected MPs and elected MLAs vote; MLCs never vote**". Statement 2 exploits candidates who learned the first, sloppier version.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **"No Council, no nominee."** For the Presidential election, strike out every Legislative Council member and every nominated member, and what remains is the electoral college.

### Q2 — Statement I / Statement II
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Level 4 — Examiner Trap

**Statement I:** The Governor of a State may grant pardon to a person sentenced to death by a court within the State.

**Statement II:** The pardoning power of the Governor under Article 161 extends to all offences against laws relating to matters within the executive power of the State.

A) Both are correct, and II explains I
B) Both are correct, but II does not explain I
C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
D) Both are incorrect

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** **Statement II is a correct statement of Art 161's scope.** **Statement I is wrong** because Art 161 **does not include the power to pardon a death sentence** — the Governor may **commute, remit, respite or reprieve**, but the **pardon** of a death sentence lies with the **President under Art 72**. Art 161 also excludes **court martial** sentences, which Art 72 covers.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** and **B)** both accept Statement I. The stem is engineered so that Statement II **sounds like it supports** Statement I — it describes the subject-matter breadth of Art 161 without mentioning the two carve-outs.
- **D)** wrongly rejects Statement II, which is accurate as far as it goes.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** **Subject-matter scope versus type of sentence.** Statement II is about *which laws* Art 161 reaches; Statement I is about *which kind of sentence* it reaches. A candidate who does not separate those two axes reads II as proving I.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Two things only the President can do: pardon a death sentence, and touch a court martial.** Any Governor-plus-death-sentence or Governor-plus-court-martial option is false.

### Q3 — Case / Situation based
**Difficulty:** 🟠 Level 3 — Advanced SI

A State Legislative Assembly passes a Bill. The Governor returns it for reconsideration. The Assembly passes it again without any amendment and presents it to the Governor.

Which of the following is correct?

A) The Governor must give assent, as under Article 111 for the President
B) The Governor must give assent, but may simultaneously reserve it for the President
C) The Governor shall not withhold assent, but the option of reserving it for the President in an appropriate case is not thereby extinguished
D) The Governor may return it a second time for reconsideration

**✅ Answer: C**

**🧠 Explanation.** Under **Art 200**, once a Bill is returned and re-passed, the Governor "**shall not withhold assent therefrom**". But Art 200's **first proviso** governs return, while the **reservation** route under the main clause and Art 201 is a distinct option — most clearly where the Bill falls within a category attracting reservation, such as one derogating from the powers of the High Court. The precise contours of reservation at this stage were the subject of the 2023–25 litigation and the **Presidential Reference opinion of 20 November 2025**.

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **A)** imports **Art 111** wholesale. The Presidential route has **no reservation option** at all, because there is no higher authority to reserve to. Art 200 is structurally different.
- **B)** internally contradictory — assent and reservation are **alternative** dispositions of the same Bill, not cumulative.
- **D)** **false**. Return is available **once**. A second return is not open under either Art 111 or Art 200.

**🎯 Examiner's Trap.** Treating Arts 111 and 200 as mirror images. They are not: **only the Governor has a reservation option, and only the President is compelled to assent on a re-passed Bill.**

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** **Art 111 has three options; Art 200 has four.** The fourth — **reservation for the President** — is the whole difference, and every Art 200 question is built on it.

### Q4 — Chronological order
**Difficulty:** 🟡 Level 2 — Competitive

Arrange the following in chronological order:

1. Re-promulgation of ordinances held a fraud on the Constitution
2. Council of Ministers capped at 15% of the House
3. Presidential Reference opinion on the Governor's assent under Articles 200 and 201
4. Pardon under Articles 72 and 161 held reviewable for mala fides in an Andhra Pradesh case

A) 1 – 2 – 4 – 3
B) 2 – 1 – 4 – 3
C) 1 – 4 – 2 – 3
D) 4 – 1 – 2 – 3

**✅ Answer: A**

**🧠 Explanation.** *D.C. Wadhwa* **1987** (1) → **91st Amendment 2003** (2) → *Epuru Sudhakar* **2006** (4) → **Presidential Reference opinion, 20 November 2025** (3).

**❌ Distractor Analysis**
- **B)** places the 91st Amendment (2003) before *D.C. Wadhwa* (1987).
- **C)** places *Epuru Sudhakar* (2006) before the 91st Amendment (2003).
- **D)** places *Epuru Sudhakar* first of all, ahead of a 1987 judgment.

**⚡ 30-Second Shortcut.** Anchor the two you are surest of — **1987 Wadhwa** and **2003 the 15% cap** — and the remaining two sort themselves, since **Epuru Sudhakar is a 2000s case** and the Reference opinion is the **most recent** item in the entire Polity syllabus.

## 7️⃣ 📌 Final Takeaway

1. **Presidential electoral college: elected MPs + elected MLAs + Delhi and Puducherry. No MLCs, no nominees.** For the **Vice-President**, both Houses only — and **nominated members do vote**.
2. **Art 161 cannot pardon a death sentence and cannot touch a court martial.** Art 72 can do both.
3. **Art 200 has four options; Art 111 has three.** Reservation for the President is the difference.
4. **An ordinance dies six weeks after reassembly, and re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution** (*D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987).
